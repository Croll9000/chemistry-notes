The Combined Gas Law

The combined gas law relates the pressure, volume and Kelvin temperature of a fixed amount of gas: P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2.

The Combined Gas Law

The Combined Gas Law

Each of the three gas laws so far holds one variable constant. Boyle’s law relates pressure and volume. Charles’s law relates volume and temperature. Gay-Lussac’s law relates pressure and temperature. But what happens when all three change at once?

Consider a sealed bag of chips packed at a factory near sea level and then carried up a mountain on a cool day. As the bag rises, the pressure of the surrounding air falls, and so does the temperature. Neither Boyle’s law nor Charles’s law alone can predict its volume. When all three variables change, the three relationships are combined into a single equation, called the combined gas law. This is the gas equation found on the Reference Table.

Combined Gas Law – the ratio of the pressure-volume product to the temperature of a gas remains constant if the amount of gas in the system does not change.

The combined gas law states that for a fixed amount of gas, the pressure multiplied by the volume, divided by the Kelvin temperature, stays the same. The value of PVT before a change therefore equals its value after the change. Pressure and volume remain inversely proportional. Volume and pressure are each directly proportional to the Kelvin temperature.

When one of the three variables is held constant, it has the same value on both sides of the equation, so it cancels. Canceling the temperature leaves Boyle’s law. Canceling the pressure leaves Charles’s law, and canceling the volume leaves Gay-Lussac’s law. In this way, the combined gas law contains all three of the individual laws.

  • In Boyle’s law, pressure and volume are inversely related. In Charles’s law, volume and temperature are directly related. In Gay-Lussac’s law, pressure and temperature are directly related. Each of these laws assumes that the third state variable is held constant.
  • When all three variables change at once, the three relationships are combined into a single equation, the combined gas law.
  • Combined Gas Law – the ratio of the pressure-volume product to the temperature of a gas remains constant if the amount of gas in the system does not change.
  • PVT stays constant for a fixed amount of gas, so the value before a change equals the value after the change.
Example
Weather balloons are designed to rise into the upper atmosphere, where the pressure and temperature are low. The volume of a balloon expands until it pops, and the equipment it carries falls back to Earth. Pressure, volume and temperature all change during this trip, but each quantity stays related to the others according to the combined gas law.
P1V1T1=P2V2T2
P1 – initial pressure (kPa or atm)V1 – initial volume (L or mL)T1 – initial temperature (K)P2 – final pressure (kPa or atm)V2 – final volume (L or mL)T2 – final temperature (K)

Solving Combined Gas Law Problems

Solving Problems

Every combined gas law problem describes one sample of gas in two states, an initial state (1) and a final state (2). If the problem does not mention a variable, or says that it stays the same, that variable is held constant and cancels. If the problem says the gas is at STP, the given values are T = 273 K and P = 101.3 kPa (1 atm).

Engineers use the combined gas law to predict the conditions inside a car engine. The fuel and air in each cylinder are squeezed to about one tenth of their volume before the spark plug fires, and their temperature and pressure both rise sharply. To solve a combined gas law problem:

  1. List the variables and the unknown. Read the question and list every given variable, with its unit, and the unknown.
  2. Convert temperatures to kelvin. If a temperature is given in °C, add 273: K = °C + 273. Both pressures must have the same unit, and so must both volumes.
  3. Write down the combined gas law.
  4. Cancel the constant variables. Identify any variable held constant and omit it from both sides of the equation.
  5. Substitute and solve. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation, then solve for the unknown algebraically.
  6. Write the final answer with units.
