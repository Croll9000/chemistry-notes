The Combined Gas Law

A weather balloon launched by the National Weather Service is about 1.5 m across on the ground, but by the time it reaches about 30 km above Earth it has stretched to roughly 6 m across before it bursts. As the balloon rises, both the pressure and the temperature of the surrounding air fall, so neither Boyle’s law nor Charles’s law alone can predict its volume. In this section, we will discuss the combined gas law, which relates pressure, volume and temperature together.

The Combined Gas Law

Boyle’s, Charles’s and Gay-Lussac’s laws each hold one variable constant and study the other two. Real changes are rarely so tidy: a gas in an engine cylinder is compressed and heated at the same time. The combined gas law brings all three relationships into one equation, which is the gas equation on the Reference Table.

  • In Boyle’s law, pressure and volume are inversely related. In Charles’s law, volume and temperature are directly related. In Gay-Lussac’s law, pressure and temperature are directly related. Each of these laws assumes that the third state variable is held constant.
  • When all three variables change at once, the three relationships are combined into a single equation, the combined gas law.
  • Combined Gas Law – the ratio of the pressure-volume product to the temperature of a gas remains constant if the amount of gas in the system does not change.
  • PVT stays constant for a fixed amount of gas, so the value before a change equals the value after the change.
Example
Weather balloons are designed to rise into the upper atmosphere, where the pressure and temperature are low. The volume of a balloon expands until it pops, and the equipment it carries falls back to Earth. Pressure, volume and temperature all change during this trip, but each quantity stays related to the others according to the combined gas law.
P1V1T1=P2V2T2
P1 – initial pressure (kPa or atm)V1 – initial volume (L or mL)T1 – initial temperature (K)P2 – final pressure (kPa or atm)V2 – final volume (L or mL)T2 – final temperature (K)
  • When one variable is held constant, it is the same on both sides of the equation, so it cancels. Canceling each variable gives one of the three gas laws:
P1V1T1=P2V2T2→Boyle’s lawP1V1 = P2V2temperature constant
P1V1T1=P2V2T2→Charles’s lawV1T1 = V2T2pressure constant
P1V1T1=P2V2T2→Gay-Lussac’s lawP1T1 = P2T2volume constant
  • Relationships in the combined gas law:
    • Pressure and volume are inversely proportional.
    • Volume and Kelvin temperature are directly proportional.
    • Pressure and Kelvin temperature are directly proportional.

Solving Combined Gas Law Problems

The fuel and air in a car engine cylinder are squeezed to about one-tenth of their volume before the spark plug fires, and the temperature and pressure both rise sharply. Engineers use the combined gas law to predict the conditions inside the cylinder at each stage. In this section, we will solve combined gas law problems.

Solving Problems

Every combined gas law problem describes one sample of gas in two states, an initial state (1) and a final state (2). If the problem does not mention a variable, or says it stays the same, that variable is held constant and cancels.

  • Steps for solving gas law problems:
    1. List the variables and the unknown. Read the question and list every given variable, with its unit, and the unknown.
    2. Convert temperatures to kelvin. If a temperature is given in °C, add 273: K = °C + 273.
    3. Write down the combined gas law.
    4. Cancel the constant variables. Identify any variable held constant and omit it from the equation.
    5. Substitute and solve. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation, then solve for the unknown algebraically.
    6. Write the final answer with units.
  • Temperatures are converted with K = °C + 273. Both pressures must have the same unit (1 atm = 101.3 kPa), and so must both volumes.
  • If the problem says “at STP,” the given values are T = 273 K and P = 101.3 kPa (1 atm).
