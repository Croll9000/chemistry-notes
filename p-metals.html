Where They Are

Metals and Nonmetals

Copper is used in electrical wiring, aluminum is rolled into foil and sulfur is used to make rubber tires stronger. Elements are chosen for these uses because of their properties, and these properties are closely tied to where an element sits on the periodic table. Elements are classified as metals, nonmetals or metalloids, and the periodic table has a dividing line shaped like a staircase that separates them. The elements to the left of the staircase, except hydrogen, are metals, and more than three-quarters of all the elements are metals. The elements to the right of the staircase are nonmetals; hydrogen is also a nonmetal, even though it sits on the left side. The elements that touch the staircase, except aluminum, are metalloids: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, Po and At.

  • Elements are classified as metals, nonmetals or metalloids based on their properties.
  • We can identify whether an element is a metal, nonmetal or metalloid from its position on the periodic table.
    • The periodic table has a dividing line that looks like a “staircase.”
  • Elements to the left of the staircase (except hydrogen) are metals.
    • Most elements are metals.
  • Elements to the right of the staircase are nonmetals.
    • Hydrogen is a nonmetal, even though it sits on the left side.
  • Elements that touch the staircase (except aluminum) are metalloids.

Properties

Properties of Metals

A single gram of gold can be drawn into a wire more than 2 km long. Metals are good conductors of heat and electricity, and they are lustrous, meaning shiny. They are also malleable, able to be hammered into different shapes, and ductile, able to be drawn into wires. In chemical reactions, metals lose electrons and form positive ions. Most metals are solids at STP with high melting points; mercury is the only metal that is a liquid at STP.

  • Found left of the staircase
  • Good conductors of heat and electricity
  • Lustrous – shiny
  • Malleable – able to be hammered into different shapes
  • Ductile – able to be drawn into wires
  • Lose electrons and form positive ions
  • Mostly solids at STP, with high melting points
    • Mercury is the only metal that is a liquid at STP.

Properties of Nonmetals

A crystal of iodine crumbles when it is pressed, which is the opposite of how a metal behaves. Nonmetals are poor conductors of heat and electricity. As solids, they are brittle, meaning they shatter or crumble when hit, and they are dull rather than shiny. In chemical reactions, nonmetals gain electrons and form negative ions. Many nonmetals are gases at STP with low melting and boiling points; bromine is the only nonmetal that is a liquid at STP.

  • Found right of the staircase
  • Poor conductors of heat and electricity
  • Brittle – shatter or crumble when hit (as solids)
  • Dull – not shiny
  • Gain electrons and form negative ions
  • Many are gases at STP, with low melting and boiling points
    • Bromine is the only nonmetal that is a liquid at STP.

Metalloids

Silicon is the main material in nearly every computer chip and solar panel. It is useful because it conducts electricity better than a nonmetal but not as well as a metal. Metalloids are the elements that touch the staircase on the periodic table, except aluminum, and they share some properties of both metals and nonmetals: they are shiny like metals but brittle like nonmetals. Several metalloids are semiconductors, materials whose conductivity can be good or poor depending on what they are combined with.

  • Metalloids – elements that touch the staircase on the periodic table (except aluminum).
    • They share some properties of both metals and nonmetals.
  • Metalloids are shiny like metals.
  • Metalloids are brittle like nonmetals.
  • Semiconductors – materials whose conductivity can be good or poor depending on what they are combined with. Silicon is the semiconductor in computer chips and solar panels.

Metallic Character

Metallic Character

Magnesium, a lightweight metal used in laptop cases, and phosphorus, a nonmetal used on the striking strip of a matchbox, are only three places apart in period 3. The properties of the elements change gradually from metallic to nonmetallic. As we move from left to right across the periodic table, the metallic character of the elements decreases and their nonmetallic character increases, so the most metallic elements are found on the left and the most nonmetallic elements on the right. Moving down a group, metallic character increases. Group 15, for instance, begins with nitrogen, a nonmetal, passes through the metalloids arsenic and antimony and ends with bismuth, a metal. Thus, the most metallic elements of all are at the bottom left, cesium and francium.

  • As you move from left to right across the periodic table, the metallic character of elements decreases.
    • The nonmetallic character of elements increases.
  • The most metallic elements are found on the left.
  • The most nonmetallic elements are found on the right.
  • Moving down a group, metallic character increases.
    Example
    Group 15 goes from nitrogen (nonmetal) to arsenic and antimony (metalloids) to bismuth (metal).

Phases of the Elements

Phases at STP

Gallium melts at 29.8 °C, so a piece of it will melt in a person’s hand, yet it is classified as a solid. The phase of an element depends on the temperature and pressure, which change from day to day, so chemists compare every element under the same set of conditions. STP, standard temperature and pressure, is 273 K (0 °C) and 1 atm (101.3 kPa). Most elements are solids at STP. Only two elements are liquids at STP, bromine (Br) and mercury (Hg). Eleven elements are gases: the five diatomic gases hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine and chlorine (H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2), and the six noble gases helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon, which exist as single atoms. All of the gases are nonmetals, found at the upper right of the table, plus hydrogen.

  • STP – standard temperature and pressure: 273 K (0 °C) and 1 atm (101.3 kPa).
  • Most elements are solids at STP.
  • Only two elements are liquids at STP: bromine (Br) and mercury (Hg).
  • Eleven elements are gases at STP.
    • Hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine and chlorine.
      • These five are diatomic gases: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2.
    • The six noble gases: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon.
      • These are monatomic: they exist as single atoms.
  • All of the gases are nonmetals.
    • They are found at the upper right of the table (plus hydrogen).
