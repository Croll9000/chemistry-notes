Empirical and Molecular Formulas

Glucose, C6H12O6, is the sugar that fuels the cells in your body. Formaldehyde, CH2O, is a toxic gas used to preserve biology specimens. The two substances have very different properties, yet both contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms in exactly the same 1 : 2 : 1 ratio. A glucose molecule simply contains 6 times as many atoms of each element as a formaldehyde molecule.

Chemists therefore use two kinds of formulas. The molecular formula of glucose is C6H12O6. Its empirical formula is CH2O, the same as formaldehyde.

Molecular Formula – indicates how many atoms of each element are present in a compound.

Empirical Formula – indicates the simplest (lowest whole-number) ratio of elements in a compound.

The word “empirical” means based on observation or experiment, and the name fits. When a chemist analyzes a new substance, the first thing the lab can usually measure is the mass of each element it contains. Those masses reveal only the ratio of the atoms, not how many atoms are in each molecule. Thus, data collected in a lab often gives the empirical formula but not the molecular formula. A second measurement, the molar mass of the compound, is needed to find the molecular formula.

Note that ionic compounds, which do not form molecules, are always written with an empirical formula, since it represents the ratio of ions in the crystal.

  • A molecular formula indicates exactly how many atoms of each element are present in a compound.
    • Another type of formula, known as an empirical formula, conveys only the simplest ratio of elements in a compound.
    • Data collected in a lab will often give information about the empirical but not the molecular formula.
    • Molecular Formula – indicates how many atoms of each element are present in a compound.
    • Empirical Formula – indicates the simplest (lowest whole-number) ratio of elements in a compound.
Example
Glucose, C6H12O6: dividing every subscript by 6 gives CH2O (empirical formula).

Molecular Formula → Empirical Formula

Since an empirical formula is the simplest ratio of the atoms in a molecule, finding it from a molecular formula is the same as reducing a fraction to lowest terms. Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, contains 2 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms. The 2 : 2 ratio reduces to 1 : 1, so its empirical formula is HO. Ethane, C2H6, reduces to CH3. To find the empirical formula from a molecular formula:

  1. Find the largest number that divides every subscript evenly.
  2. Divide every subscript by that number.

If the only number that divides every subscript evenly is 1, the molecular formula is already an empirical formula. Water, H2O, and carbon dioxide, CO2, are examples.

  • An empirical formula is found by reducing the subscripts of a molecular formula to the smallest whole-number ratio.
  • Steps for Finding the Empirical Formula from a Molecular Formula:
    1. Find the largest number that divides every subscript evenly.
    2. Divide every subscript by that number.
  • If the only number that divides every subscript evenly is 1, the molecular formula is already an empirical formula.

Empirical Formula → Molecular Formula

Going in the other direction takes one more piece of information. The empirical formula NO2 could describe nitrogen dioxide, NO2, the brown gas in smog, or dinitrogen tetroxide, N2O4, a colorless liquid used as rocket fuel. Both have the same ratio of atoms, so the ratio alone cannot tell them apart. Their mass can.

A molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of its empirical formula, so its mass is the same whole-number multiple of the empirical formula’s mass. The mass of the empirical formula NO2 is 46.01 g/mol. A compound with a molar mass of 92.02 g/mol has exactly twice that mass, so each molecule contains two NO2 units, and its molecular formula is N2O4. The molar mass of the compound tells us which multiple to use. To find the molecular formula from an empirical formula:

  1. Determine the mass of the empirical formula.
  2. Divide the molar mass of the compound by the mass of the empirical formula.
  3. Multiply the subscripts in the empirical formula by the answer from step 2.
  • A molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of its empirical formula. The molar mass of the compound tells us which multiple.
  • Steps for Finding the Molecular Formula from an Empirical Formula:
    1. Determine the mass of the empirical formula.
    2. Divide the molar mass of the compound by the mass of the empirical formula.
    3. Multiply the subscripts in the empirical formula by the answer from step 2.
