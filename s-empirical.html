Empirical and Molecular Formulas

Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, and formaldehyde, CH₂O, contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms in the same 1 : 2 : 1 ratio, yet one is a sugar and the other is a toxic gas. Both have the same empirical formula, CH₂O, but a glucose molecule contains 6 times as many atoms of each element. In this section, we will distinguish empirical formulas from molecular formulas and convert between them.

Empirical and Molecular Formulas

When chemists discover a new substance, such as a compound from a plant that might become a medicine, the first thing a lab can measure is how much of each element it contains. That measurement gives the ratio of the atoms, but not their exact numbers. The two kinds of formulas describe what is known at each stage.

  • A molecular formula indicates exactly how many atoms of each element are present in a compound.
    • Another type of formula, known as an empirical formula, conveys only the simplest ratio of elements in a compound.
    • Data collected in a lab will often give information about the empirical but not the molecular formula.
    • Molecular Formula – indicates how many atoms of each element are present in a compound.
    • Empirical Formula – indicates the simplest (lowest whole-number) ratio of elements in a compound.
Example
Glucose, C6H12O6 ÷ 6 → CH2O (empirical formula)

Molecular Formula → Empirical Formula

  • An empirical formula is found by reducing the subscripts of a molecular formula to the smallest whole-number ratio.
  • Steps for Finding the Empirical Formula from a Molecular Formula:
    1. Find the largest number that divides every subscript evenly.
    2. Divide every subscript by that number.
  • If the only number that divides every subscript evenly is 1, the molecular formula is already an empirical formula.

Empirical Formula → Molecular Formula

  • A molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of its empirical formula. The molar mass of the compound tells us which multiple.
  • Steps for Finding the Molecular Formula from an Empirical Formula:
    1. Determine the mass of the empirical formula.
    2. Divide the molar mass of the compound by the mass of the empirical formula.
    3. Multiply the subscripts in the empirical formula by the answer from step 2.

Percent Composition to Empirical Formula

Vitamin C is 40.9% carbon, 4.6% hydrogen and 54.5% oxygen by mass. Converting these percents to moles of each element reveals the simplest whole-number ratio of its atoms, C₃H₄O₃. In this section, we will determine empirical formulas from percent composition.

Converting Percent Composition to Empirical Formula

When a new compound is burned or broken down in an analysis lab, the result is a list of percents by mass. Turning those percents into a formula is how chemists identify unknown substances, from pollutants in water to new materials.

  • Steps for Converting Percent Composition to Empirical Formula:
    1. Assume 100 g of the substance, so each percent becomes a mass in grams.
    2. Convert the mass of each element to moles (divide by its atomic mass).
    3. Divide all mole values by the smallest number from step 2.
    4. If the resulting values are whole numbers, use them as the subscripts of the empirical formula. If a value ends in about .5, multiply every value by 2.
