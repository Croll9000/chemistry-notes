Single Replacement and the Activity Series

Activity Series

Gold is often found in nature as shiny nuggets of the pure metal. Potassium has never been found as a pure metal in nature. It is always combined with other elements in compounds, such as the potash used in fertilizer. The difference is how readily each element reacts. Gold is so unreactive that it stays pure for millions of years. Potassium reacts with almost anything it touches.

Since some elements react more readily than others, knowing which are more reactive lets us predict reactions. We use an activity series for this. The higher an element is on the list, the more active it is. An activity series is given in a question whenever one is needed.

Activity Series – table which lists elements in decreasing order of chemical activity.

However, activity means something different for metals and nonmetals. Highly reactive metals lose electrons easily, so the more active a metal is, the more likely it is to give electrons to another element. Highly reactive nonmetals gain electrons easily, so the more active a nonmetal is, the more likely it is to take electrons from another element. This matches the periodic trends. Metals with low ionization energies and nonmetals with high electronegativities are the most active.

  • Some elements undergo chemical reactions more readily than others. It is important to understand which elements are more reactive than others, as it will allow us to predict chemical reactions.
  • Activity Series – table which lists elements in decreasing order of chemical activity.
    • On an exam, an activity series is given in the question. Read it from the top: the higher an element is, the more active it is.
  • Activity differs for metals and nonmetals:
    • Metals – highly reactive metals lose electrons easily. The more active a metal is, the more likely it is to give electrons to another element.
    • Nonmetals – highly reactive nonmetals gain electrons easily. The more active a nonmetal is, the more likely it is to take electrons from another element.

Predicting Single Replacement Reactions

A more active element can replace a less active element within a compound. When zinc metal is placed in a solution of copper(II) sulfate, zinc is more active than copper, so it replaces copper in the compound:

Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu

If the lone element is less active than the element in the compound, however, no reaction occurs. Chlorine cannot replace fluorine in a fluoride compound, since fluorine is the more active element and it is already in the compound.

Note that the comparison must always be between like elements. A lone metal is compared with the metal in the compound, and a lone nonmetal with the nonmetal.

Shipbuilders put this to use. Large steel ships often have blocks of zinc bolted to their hulls below the waterline. Zinc is more active than iron, so in seawater the zinc gives up its electrons first, and the iron in the steel is spared. Over time the zinc blocks corrode away while the hull stays protected, and the old blocks are replaced with new ones. To predict a single replacement reaction:

  • When predicting whether a single replacement reaction will take place, it is often necessary to consult an activity series.
    • A more active element has the ability to replace a less active element within a compound.
  • Steps to Predicting Single Replacement Reactions:
    1. Compare the activity of the lone element with that of its corresponding element in the compound.
      • If the lone element is a metal, compare its activity with the metal in the compound.
      • If it is a nonmetal, compare its activity to the nonmetal.
    2. If the lone element is more active, substitute it into the compound. If the lone element is less active, no reaction will occur.
    3. Write the correct formulas for the products using the criss-cross method to balance charges. Also remember to take into account diatomic elements.

Example 1: Zn + CuSO4 → ?

  1. Find zinc (Zn) and copper (Cu) on the activity series. Since zinc is a metal, compare it with the other metal, copper.
  2. Zinc is more active than copper, so it replaces copper in the compound.
  3. Criss-cross the charges of the zinc (Zn2+) and sulfate (SO42−) ions and reduce: ZnSO4.

Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu

Example 2: Cl2 + NaF → ?

  1. Find chlorine (Cl2) and fluorine (F2) on the activity series. Chlorine is a nonmetal, so compare it with the nonmetal fluorine.
  2. Fluorine, the more active element, is already in the compound, so no reaction occurs.

Use the activity series to decide whether each reaction happens, then write and balance it.

Solubility and Precipitates

Solubility of Ionic Compounds

Whether a double replacement reaction happens often depends on whether one of the products dissolves in water. An ionic compound that dissolves is soluble. One that does not is insoluble, and when an insoluble solid forms in a reaction, it is called a precipitate.

Soluble – able to dissolve in water. A soluble ionic compound breaks apart into ions and is written with (aq).

Insoluble – does not dissolve in water. An insoluble ionic compound stays a solid and is written with (s).

Precipitate – insoluble solid that forms and separates from a liquid during a chemical reaction.

The Reference Table lists ions that always form aqueous solutions, such as the group 1 ions, ammonium and nitrate. If either ion in a compound is on the list, the compound is soluble, and it is written with (aq). If neither ion is on the list, the table does not decide, and the question will give the solubility, for example, “silver chloride, AgCl, does not dissolve in water.”

The white crust that builds up inside a tea kettle in places with “hard water” is a precipitate. The water contains dissolved calcium ions, and when it is heated, insoluble calcium carbonate forms and coats the kettle. To read the table:

  • Soluble – able to dissolve in water. A soluble ionic compound breaks apart into ions and is written with (aq).
  • Insoluble – does not dissolve in water. An insoluble ionic compound stays a solid and is written with (s).
  • Precipitate – insoluble solid that forms and separates from a liquid during a chemical reaction.
  • The Reference Table lists ions that form aqueous solutions (Selected Ions Forming Aqueous Solutions):
  • Steps to Reading the Selected Ions Forming Aqueous Solutions Table:
    1. Identify the positive ion and the negative ion in the compound.
    2. Look for each ion in the table. Group 1 ions include Li+, Na+, K+, Rb+ and Cs+.
    3. If either ion is in the table, the compound is soluble and is written with (aq).
    4. If neither ion is in the table, the table does not decide. The question gives the solubility.
      Example
      “Silver chloride, AgCl, does not dissolve in water.”

Double Replacement Reactions

Predicting Double Replacement Reactions

In a double replacement reaction, the positive ion of the first compound pairs with the negative ion of the second, and the negative ion of the first pairs with the positive ion of the second.

However, trading partners does not always produce a reaction. If both new compounds simply stay dissolved, the ions are still floating freely in the water exactly as before, so nothing has really happened. Thus, a reaction occurs only if a precipitate, water or a gas forms, since each of these removes ions from the solution.

Water forms when an acid reacts with a base. An antacid tablet containing magnesium hydroxide relieves heartburn by reacting with the hydrochloric acid in the stomach:

Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl → MgCl2 + 2H2O

The hydrogen ions from the acid pair up with the hydroxide ions to form water, and the acid is neutralized. To predict a double replacement reaction:

  • Steps to Predicting Double Replacement Reactions:
    1. Identify the positive and negative ions in each compound.
    2. Pair the positive ion in the first compound with the negative ion in the second compound. Pair the negative ion in the first compound with the positive ion in the second.
    3. Write the formulas for the new compounds using the criss-cross method to balance the charges.
    4. Decide whether a reaction occurs.
      • A reaction occurs if a precipitate, water or a gas forms.
      • If both products stay dissolved, no reaction occurs.
      • Use the Selected Ions Forming Aqueous Solutions table: a product that contains one of its ions stays dissolved (aq).
      • Whether other compounds dissolve is given in the question.
        Example
        “Phosphates form insoluble compounds with most metals.”
      • Water forms when an acid reacts with a base.
    5. Balance the equation.

Identify the ions, swap partners, decide whether a solid forms, then balance.
