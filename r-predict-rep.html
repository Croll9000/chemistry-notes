Single Replacement and the Activity Series

A more active element replaces a less active one.

Activity Series

Some metals, like gold, stay shiny for thousands of years, while others, like sodium, react violently with water. The activity series ranks elements by how easily they react.

  • Some elements undergo chemical reactions more readily than others. It is important to understand which elements are more reactive than others, as it will allow us to predict chemical reactions.
  • Activity Series – table which lists elements in decreasing order of chemical activity.
    • On an exam, an activity series is given in the question. Read it from the top: the higher an element is, the more active it is.
  • Activity differs for metals and nonmetals:
    • Metals – highly reactive metals lose electrons easily. The more active a metal is, the more likely it is to give electrons to another element.
    • Nonmetals – highly reactive nonmetals gain electrons easily. The more active a nonmetal is, the more likely it is to take electrons from another element.

Predicting Single Replacement Reactions

Dropping an iron nail into a blue copper(II) sulfate solution coats the nail with copper. The iron replaced the copper because iron is more active.

  • When predicting whether a single replacement reaction will take place, it is often necessary to consult an activity series.
    • A more active element has the ability to replace a less active element within a compound.
  • Steps to Predicting Single Replacement Reactions:
    1. Compare the activity of the lone element with that of its corresponding element in the compound. If the lone element is a metal, compare its activity with the metal in the compound. If it is a nonmetal, compare its activity to the nonmetal.
    2. If the lone element is more active, substitute it into the compound. If the lone element is less active, no reaction will occur.
    3. Write the correct formulas for the products using the criss-cross method to balance charges. Also remember to take into account diatomic elements.

Example 1: Zn + CuSO4 → ?

  1. Find zinc (Zn) and copper (Cu) on the activity series. Since zinc is a metal, compare it with the other metal, copper.
  2. Zinc is more active than copper, so it replaces copper in the compound.
  3. Criss-cross the charges of the zinc (Zn2+) and sulfate (SO42−) ions and reduce: ZnSO4.

Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu

Example 2: Cl2 + NaF → ?

  1. Find chlorine (Cl2) and fluorine (F2) on the activity series. Chlorine is a nonmetal, so compare it with the nonmetal fluorine.
  2. Fluorine, the more active element, is already in the compound, so no reaction occurs.

Use the activity series to decide whether each reaction happens, then write and balance it.

Solubility and Precipitates

Which ionic compounds dissolve in water?

Solubility of Ionic Compounds

Table salt disappears when it is stirred into water, but chalk just sinks to the bottom. Whether an ionic compound dissolves decides whether a solid forms when two solutions are mixed.

  • Soluble – able to dissolve in water. A soluble ionic compound breaks apart into ions and is written with (aq).
  • Insoluble – does not dissolve in water. An insoluble ionic compound stays a solid and is written with (s).
  • Precipitate – insoluble solid that forms and separates from a liquid during a chemical reaction.
  • The Reference Table lists ions that form aqueous solutions (Selected Ions Forming Aqueous Solutions):
  • Steps to Reading the Selected Ions Forming Aqueous Solutions Table:
    1. Identify the positive ion and the negative ion in the compound.
    2. Look for each ion in the table. Group 1 ions include Li+, Na+, K+, Rb+ and Cs+.
    3. If either ion is in the table, the compound is soluble and is written with (aq).
    4. If neither ion is in the table, the table does not decide. The question gives the solubility. Ex: “Silver chloride, AgCl, does not dissolve in water.”
  • The solubility of other ionic compounds will be covered in more detail in the Solutions unit.

Double Replacement Reactions

A reaction happens if a precipitate, water or a gas forms.

Predicting Double Replacement Reactions

Mixing two clear solutions can suddenly produce a cloudy solid, a fizz of gas, or water. Each of these is a sign that the ions in the two compounds have swapped partners.

  • Steps to Predicting Double Replacement Reactions:
    1. Identify the positive and negative ions in each compound.
    2. Pair the positive ion in the first compound with the negative ion in the second compound. Pair the negative ion in the first compound with the positive ion in the second.
    3. Write the formulas for the new compounds using the criss-cross method to balance the charges.
    4. Decide whether a reaction occurs. A reaction occurs if a precipitate, water or a gas forms. If both products stay dissolved, no reaction occurs.
      • Use the Selected Ions Forming Aqueous Solutions table: a product that contains one of its ions stays dissolved (aq).
      • Whether other compounds dissolve is given in the question. Ex: “Phosphates form insoluble compounds with most metals.”
      • Water forms when an acid reacts with a base.
    5. Balance the equation.

Identify the ions, swap partners, decide whether a solid forms, then balance.

The Solubility Table

Which compounds dissolve, and which form precipitates.

Solubility Table

The Reference Table lists only a few ions that always dissolve. Chemists use a larger table to look up the solubility of many more ionic compounds.

  • It is important to know how ions in aqueous solutions will interact with each other. Some compounds will dissolve completely, other compounds will dissolve partially, and some will form precipitates.
  • The table below gives the solubility of many ionic compounds. Use it to decide whether a product forms a precipitate.
Want to memorize the general patterns? Solubility rules
  1. Compounds of group 1 ions (Li+, Na+, K+, etc.) and ammonium (NH4+) are soluble.
  2. Nitrates (NO3−), acetates (C2H3O2−) and chlorates (ClO3−) are soluble.
  3. Chlorides (Cl−), bromides (Br−) and iodides (I−) are soluble, except when combined with Ag+, Pb2+ and Hg22+.
  4. Sulfates (SO42−) are soluble, except when combined with Ba2+ and Pb2+ (insoluble) or Ca2+ and Ag+ (slightly soluble).
  5. Fluorides (F−) are soluble, except when combined with Mg2+, Ca2+, Ba2+ and Pb2+.
  6. With the exception of rule 1, carbonates (CO32−), phosphates (PO43−) and sulfites (SO32−) are insoluble.
  7. With the exception of rule 1 and the barium ion (Ba2+), hydroxides (OH−) are insoluble. Calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble.

The table and the rules agree: every cell in the table follows these rules. A dash (—) means the compound does not exist as a stable solid. Rules adapted from the general solubility rules used in college chemistry textbooks (LibreTexts, Solubility Rules).
