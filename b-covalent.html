Covalent Bonding

When two nonmetals meet, neither gives up its electrons. They share them instead. The more pairs they share, the shorter and stronger the bond.

Part1

Covalent Compounds

Covalent Compounds

Consider what happens when two chlorine atoms meet. Each has 7 valence electrons, one short of an octet, and each pulls on electrons equally hard. Neither can take an electron from the other. Instead, each atom puts one electron into a pair that both atoms hold at the same time. The shared pair sits between the two nuclei, attracted to both, and this attraction holds the atoms together as a Cl2 molecule. By sharing, each chlorine atom now counts 8 valence electrons.

The same thing happens whenever two nonmetals meet. Recall that nonmetals have high electronegativities, meaning that they have a strong attraction for electrons. Neither is willing to give up its electrons, so they share them to achieve a stable valence shell. This is called a covalent bond, and compounds that contain covalent bonds are referred to as covalent compounds.

Covalent Bond – sharing of electrons.

Covalent Compounds – compounds that possess covalent bonds.

Water, sugar, carbon dioxide and the plastic in a water bottle are all covalent compounds. Unlike an ionic compound, a covalent compound is usually made of separate molecules, each one a fixed group of atoms held together by shared electrons.

  • Nonmetals have high electronegativities, meaning that they have a strong attraction for electrons.
  • When two nonmetals are brought together, neither is willing to give up its electrons.
    • Therefore, they end up sharing their electrons to achieve a stable valence shell.
    • This is known as a covalent bond.
  • Covalent Bond – sharing of electrons.
  • Covalent Compounds – compounds that possess covalent bonds.
When two or more nonmetals come together, they will share electrons. Their valence shells overlap, and the shared electrons behave as though they belong to both atoms. The positive nucleus of each atom attracts the shared pair, and this attraction holds the atoms together.

Properties of Covalent Compounds

A candle melts at about 60 °C, so a lit wick quickly forms a pool of liquid wax. A crystal of table salt held in the same flame would not melt at all, since it needs 801 °C. Candle wax is a covalent compound, and the difference shows a key point about covalent substances: when wax melts, no bonds break.

The covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong. However, the attractions between separate molecules are weak, and melting or boiling only has to overcome those weak attractions between molecules. Thus, molecular covalent compounds have low melting and boiling points, and many are liquids or gases at room temperature.

Covalent compounds are also poor conductors of heat and electricity. Their molecules are neutral, and their electrons are held in bonds, so there are no ions or free electrons to carry a current. For this reason, plastic and rubber, which are made of covalent molecules, are used to insulate electrical wires.

  • Covalent compounds differ from ionic compounds in a number of ways.
    • Composed of 2 or more nonmetals.
    • Involve a sharing of electrons between atoms.
    • Low melting and boiling points (molecular covalent).
    • Poor conductors of heat and electricity.
Cause: the covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the attractions between separate molecules are weak. Melting or boiling only has to pull the molecules away from each other, not break the bonds. Molecules have no overall charge, so there are no charged particles free to carry a current. (Network solids are the exception: see Molecular vs. Network Solids.)
Water (H2O) and ammonia (NH3) are both examples of covalent compounds. They consist of only nonmetals (hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen). Water is a liquid and ammonia is a gas at room temperature. Additionally, by themselves they are poor conductors of electricity.
Part2

Single, Double and Triple Bonds

Single Bonds

A hydrogen atom has 1 valence electron and needs 2 to be stable. Two hydrogen atoms solve the problem together. Each contributes its single electron to a shared pair, and each then counts 2 electrons, the same as helium.

In a covalent bond, each atom contributes 1 electron, and the electrons within the bond count toward the total number of valence electrons in each atom. Forming a single covalent bond therefore has the effect of increasing the number of valence electrons in each atom by 1. We call this a single covalent bond, and it is drawn as a single line between the atoms.

Single Covalent Bond – a sharing of 2 electrons, 1 from each atom participating in the bond.

  • In a covalent bond, each atom contributes 1 electron to the bond. The electrons within the bond count towards the total number of valence electrons in each atom.
    • Forming a single covalent bond has the effect of increasing the number of valence electrons in an atom by 1.
  • Single Covalent Bond – a sharing of 2 electrons, 1 from each atom participating in the bond.
    • Represented by a single line drawn between atoms.

Double Bonds

Sometimes a single covalent bond is not enough to complete the octet of each atom. Consider oxygen. Each oxygen atom has 6 valence electrons and needs 2 more. A single shared pair would bring each to only 7. Sharing a second pair brings each to 8.

When two pairs are shared, the bond is a double bond, and it is drawn as 2 lines between the atoms. The oxygen we breathe, O2, is held together by a double bond.

Double Bond – sharing of 4 electrons between 2 atoms.

Ethylene, C2H4, has a double bond between its two carbon atoms. Each carbon has only three neighbors, two hydrogens and the other carbon. A single bond to each would leave it with 7 valence electrons, so the carbons share a second pair with each other. Bananas, tomatoes and avocados release ethylene gas, and it triggers ripening. A ripe banana sealed in a bag with green fruit speeds up the ripening of everything around it, and fruit warehouses control the amount of ethylene in the air to ripen fruit on schedule.

  • Sometimes, forming a single covalent bond is not enough to complete the octet of an atom. In this case, multiple covalent bonds, such as double or triple bonds, will be formed.
  • Double Bond – sharing of 4 electrons between 2 atoms.
    • Represented by 2 lines drawn between atoms.
Each oxygen atom starts with 6 valence electrons. Forming a single bond between them only provides each with 7 valence electrons. It is necessary to form a double covalent bond between the two.

Triple Bonds

If a double bond is still not enough to complete the octet of each atom, a triple bond may form. Each nitrogen atom has 5 valence electrons and needs 3 more, so two nitrogen atoms share three pairs. A triple bond is drawn as 3 lines between the atoms.

Triple Bond – sharing of 6 electrons between atoms.

The triple bond in N2 is one of the strongest bonds known, and it has an enormous consequence. Nitrogen makes up about 78% of the air, and plants need nitrogen to grow, yet plants cannot use any of the nitrogen in the air around them. Almost nothing in nature can break the triple bond. Only a lightning strike, a few kinds of bacteria or a factory operating at high temperature and pressure can split it apart. That factory process, developed in Germany in the early 1900s, now makes the fertilizer that grows roughly half of the world’s food.

  • If forming a double bond is not enough to complete the octet of each atom, a triple bond may be necessary.
  • Triple Bond – sharing of 6 electrons between atoms.
    • Represented by 3 lines drawn between atoms.
Each nitrogen atom starts with 5 valence electrons. Forming a single bond between them gives each a total of 6 valence electrons. Forming a double bond gives them 7. It is therefore necessary to form a triple bond so that each has a full octet of 8 valence electrons.

Line and Dot Notation

Covalent bonds can be drawn as pairs of dots between the atoms or as lines. The two notations show exactly the same molecule, since each line stands for one shared pair of electrons. A single bond is one line, a double bond is two and a triple bond is three. Lone pairs are still drawn as dots. Lines make large molecules quicker to draw and easier to read. Dots make it easier to count every electron, so it helps to be comfortable with both.

Number of Bonds

Number of Bonds

How many bonds will an atom form? Count how many electrons it needs. Oxygen has 6 valence electrons and needs 2 more, so it forms 2 bonds. Carbon has 4 and needs 4 more, so it forms 4. Thus, an atom usually forms one bond for each electron it needs to complete its valence shell.

Chemists who draw the molecules of living things remember the most common numbers as “HONC 1-2-3-4”: hydrogen forms 1 bond, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3 and carbon 4.

Since elements within the same group have the same number of valence electrons, elements within the same group will usually form the same number of covalent bonds. Sulfur forms 2 bonds like oxygen. Chlorine forms 1 bond like fluorine.

  • Elements within the same group will usually form the same number of covalent bonds.
    • An atom usually forms one bond for each electron it needs to complete its valence shell.

Bond Length and Bond Strength

Bond Length

The distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms is known as the bond length. Compare three molecules with two carbon atoms each. In ethane, C2H6, the carbons are joined by a single bond and sit 154 pm apart. In ethylene, C2H4, they are joined by a double bond and sit 134 pm apart. In acetylene, C2H2, the gas burned in welding torches, they are joined by a triple bond and sit only 120 pm apart.

You can see that as the number of bonds between 2 atoms increases, from single to double to triple, the distance between those atoms decreases. More shared electrons between the nuclei means more negative charge for both positive nuclei to be attracted to, so the nuclei are pulled in closer. Bond length therefore follows the order single bonds > double bonds > triple bonds.

  • As the number of bonds between 2 atoms increases (single → double → triple), the distance between those atoms decreases.
    • As the number of electron pairs between 2 atoms increases, the length of the bond between them decreases.
  • Bond Length – the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms.

Bond Strength

The same shared electrons that pull two atoms closer together also hold them more tightly. Breaking one mole of carbon–carbon single bonds takes about 347 kJ. A mole of double bonds takes about 614 kJ, and a mole of triple bonds about 839 kJ.

As the number of bonds between 2 atoms increases, the amount of energy required to break those bonds increases. Bond strength therefore follows the order triple bond > double bond > single bond.

Putting this together with bond length, bond length and bond strength are inversely related. The shorter a bond, the stronger it is.

  • As the number of bonds between 2 atoms increases, the amount of energy required to break those bonds increases.
    • The strength of a bond increases with the number of electron pairs shared between 2 atoms.
