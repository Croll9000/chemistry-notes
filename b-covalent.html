Covalent Bonding

When two nonmetals meet, neither gives up its electrons. They share them instead. The more pairs they share, the shorter and stronger the bond.

Part1

Covalent Compounds

Nonmetals share electrons.

Covalent Compounds

Most of the substances that make up living things, such as water, sugar, proteins and DNA, are made entirely of nonmetals. When nonmetal atoms bond, neither atom is able to take electrons from the other.

  • Nonmetals have high electronegativities, meaning that they have a strong attraction for electrons.
  • When two nonmetals are brought together, neither is willing to give up its electrons. Therefore, they end up sharing their electrons to achieve a stable valence shell. This is known as a covalent bond.
  • Covalent Bond – sharing of electrons.
  • Covalent Compounds – compounds that possess covalent bonds.
When two or more nonmetals come together, they will share electrons. Their valence shells overlap, and the shared electrons behave as though they belong to both atoms. The positive nucleus of each atom attracts the shared pair, and this attraction holds the atoms together.

Properties of Covalent Compounds

  • Covalent compounds differ from ionic compounds in a number of ways.
    • Composed of 2 or more nonmetals.
    • Involve a sharing of electrons between atoms.
    • Low melting and boiling points (molecular covalent).
    • Poor conductors of heat and electricity.
Cause: the covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the attractions between separate molecules are weak. Melting or boiling only has to pull the molecules away from each other, not break the bonds. Molecules have no overall charge, so there are no charged particles free to carry a current. (Network solids are the exception: see Molecular vs. Network Solids.)
Water (H2O) and ammonia (NH3) are both examples of covalent compounds. They consist of only nonmetals (hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen). Water is a liquid and ammonia is a gas at room temperature. Additionally, by themselves they are poor conductors of electricity.
Part2

Single, Double and Triple Bonds

Shared electrons count for both atoms.

Single Bonds

  • In a covalent bond, each atom contributes 1 electron to the bond. The electrons within the bond count towards the total number of valence electrons in each atom.
    • Forming a single covalent bond has the effect of increasing the number of valence electrons in an atom by 1.
  • Single Covalent Bond – a sharing of 2 electrons, 1 from each atom participating in the bond.
    • Represented by a single line drawn between atoms.

Double Bonds

  • Sometimes, forming a single covalent bond is not enough to complete the octet of an atom. In this case, multiple covalent bonds, such as double or triple bonds, will be formed.
  • Double Bond – sharing of 4 electrons between 2 atoms.
    • Represented by 2 lines drawn between atoms.
Each oxygen atom starts with 6 valence electrons. Forming a single bond between them only provides each with 7 valence electrons. It is necessary to form a double covalent bond between the two.

Triple Bonds

The nitrogen gas that makes up most of the air is so unreactive that most living things cannot use it directly. The reason lies in the triple bond holding each pair of nitrogen atoms together.

  • If forming a double bond is not enough to complete the octet of each atom, a triple bond may be necessary.
  • Triple Bond – sharing of 6 electrons between atoms.
    • Represented by 3 lines drawn between atoms.
Each nitrogen atom starts with 5 valence electrons. Forming a single bond between them gives each a total of 6 valence electrons. Forming a double bond gives them 7. It is therefore necessary to form a triple bond so that each has a full octet of 8 valence electrons.

Bond Length and Bond Strength

More shared pairs: shorter and stronger.

Bond Length

  • As the number of bonds between 2 atoms increases (single → double → triple), the distance between those atoms decreases.
    • As the number of electron pairs between 2 atoms increases, the length of the bond between them decreases.
  • Bond Length – the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms.
  • Bond Length: Single Bonds > Double Bonds > Triple Bonds

Bond Strength

  • As the number of bonds between 2 atoms increases, the amount of energy required to break those bonds increases.
    • The strength of a bond increases with the number of electron pairs shared between 2 atoms.
  • Bond Strength: Triple Bond > Double Bond > Single Bond

Number of Bonds

Each group forms a predictable number of bonds.

Number of Bonds

  • Elements within the same group will usually form the same number of covalent bonds.
    • An atom usually forms one bond for each electron it needs to complete its valence shell.
