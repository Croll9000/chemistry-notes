Why can’t the salt in seawater be strained out?

Seawater looks as clear as tap water, yet every kilogram of it holds about 35 g of dissolved salts, most of it sodium chloride. Pour seawater through the finest filter paper and the water that drips through is just as salty as before. Leave it in a sealed jar for a year and no salt settles to the bottom. The salt has not disappeared, though. Boil the water away and a white crust of salt is left behind, which is exactly how sea salt is made. The salt is still there; it has simply been broken down into individual sodium and chloride ions that are spread evenly among the water molecules, and particles that small pass straight through the holes in a filter.

A mixture like seawater is referred to as a solution: a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. Because the substances are mixed at the level of individual atoms, ions and molecules, every drop of a solution has the same composition as every other drop. Thus, a solution never settles and cannot be separated by filtering. A solution may be colored, like the blue of copper(II) sulfate in water, but it is always clear, meaning light passes straight through it instead of being scattered by large particles.

Every solution has two parts. The solute is the substance present in the smaller amount, the substance being dissolved, while the solvent is the substance present in the larger amount, the substance doing the dissolving. In seawater, the salt is the solute and the water is the solvent. Since no matter is created or destroyed when a solute dissolves, it makes sense that the mass of a solution equals the mass of the solute plus the mass of the solvent: 35 g of salt dissolved in 965 g of water gives 1,000 g of seawater.

A solution in which water is the solvent is known as an aqueous solution, and it is labeled (aq) in a chemical formula, as in NaCl(aq). Water dissolves so many different substances that it is often called the universal solvent, which is why it is so hard to find truly pure water in nature. Solutions are not limited to solids dissolved in liquids, however. Air is a solution of oxygen, argon and other gases dissolved in nitrogen, the gas present in the largest amount, and brass is a solid solution of zinc dissolved in copper.

  • Solution – a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.
    • The particles are evenly distributed, so every part of a solution has the same composition.
    • A solution does not settle and cannot be separated by filtering. It can be colored, but it is always clear (see-through).
  • Every solution has two parts:
    • Solute – the substance present in the smaller amount in a solution; the substance being dissolved.
    • Solvent – the substance present in the larger amount in a solution; the substance doing the dissolving.
  • Solute + solvent = solution. The mass of a solution equals the mass of the solute plus the mass of the solvent.
  • Aqueous Solution – a solution in which water is the solvent. It is labeled (aq).
    • Water dissolves so many substances that it is often called the universal solvent.
  • Solutions can also be gases or solids. Air is a solution of oxygen and other gases in nitrogen, and brass is a solid solution of zinc in copper.
Example
In NaCl(aq), sodium chloride is the solute and water is the solvent.
In 10. g of sugar dissolved in 150. g of water, sugar is the solute and water is the solvent. The mass of the solution is 160. g.

How Substances Dissolve

Dissolving Ionic and Molecular Substances

An instant cold pack from a first-aid kit contains a packet of water and a solid called ammonium nitrate. Squeeze the pack to break the inner packet and, within seconds, it becomes cold enough to soothe a sprained ankle. Nothing has burned or exploded; the solid has simply dissolved. Yet dissolving clearly involves energy, and that tells us something important about what happens to the particles. Dissolving is not just a solid crumbling into smaller pieces. Attractions are broken, and new ones form.

A substance that can dissolve in a given solvent is described as soluble, while one that cannot is insoluble. Consider what happens when an ionic compound such as sodium chloride is placed in water. Water molecules are polar: the oxygen end carries a partial negative charge and the hydrogen ends carry partial positive charges. The water molecules crowd around the ions at the surface of the crystal and attract them strongly enough to pull them away from their neighbors. The partially negative oxygen ends of water molecules face the positive ions, while the partially positive hydrogen ends face the negative ions. Each ion leaves the crystal wrapped in a shell of water molecules, which keeps it from rejoining the crystal.

The separation of an ionic compound into its ions as it dissolves is referred to as dissociation, and it is written as an equation:

NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq)

Whether dissolving absorbs or releases energy depends on the balance between the attractions that are broken and the attractions that form. In the cold pack, breaking apart the ions of ammonium nitrate takes more energy than is released when the ions attract water molecules, so the pack absorbs heat from its surroundings. In a hot pack containing calcium chloride, the opposite is true, and the pack warms up.

Molecular substances dissolve differently. When sugar dissolves, the attractions between neighboring sugar molecules are broken, but the bonds inside each molecule are not, so the molecules separate from one another and stay whole. No ions form. This difference can be detected with electricity, because an electric current in a solution is carried by moving charged particles. A substance that conducts electricity when it is dissolved in water, because it forms mobile ions, is referred to as an electrolyte. Soluble ionic compounds (salts), acids and bases are electrolytes, while sugar and other substances that dissolve as whole, neutral molecules are nonelectrolytes. This is why a sports drink advertises its electrolytes: the dissolved sodium and potassium ions it contains are the same ions the body loses in sweat.

  • Soluble – able to dissolve in a given solvent.
  • Insoluble – unable to dissolve in a given solvent.
  • When an ionic compound dissolves in water, its ions separate from one another.
    • Dissociation – the separation of an ionic compound into its ions when it dissolves.
    • The partially negative oxygen ends of water molecules face the positive ions.
    • The partially positive hydrogen ends of water molecules face the negative ions.
    • For example, NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq)
  • When a molecular substance such as sugar dissolves, its molecules separate from one another but stay whole. They do not form ions.
  • Electrolyte – a substance that conducts electricity when it is dissolved in water, because it forms mobile ions.
    • Soluble ionic compounds (salts), acids and bases are electrolytes.
    • Sugar and other molecular substances that dissolve as whole molecules are nonelectrolytes.
