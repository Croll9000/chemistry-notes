Octet Exceptions & Molecular Geometry

Not every atom stops at 8 valence electrons, and molecules are not flat drawings. Electron pairs push each other as far apart as possible, giving every molecule a 3-dimensional shape.

Exceptions to the Octet Rule

Fewer than 8, or more than 8.

Fewer Than 8: Boron

  • Recall the common exceptions to the octet rule: hydrogen and helium are stable with 2 electrons, and boron is stable with 6 electrons.
  • Boron has only 3 valence electrons. When it shares each of them, it forms 3 bonds and has 6 valence electrons.
In boron trifluoride (BF3), boron forms 3 single bonds and has only 6 valence electrons. This is why BF3 can accept a lone pair from NH3 to form a coordinate covalent bond (see Lewis Structures).

More Than 8: Expanded Octets

The octet rule describes most atoms, but not all of them. Sulfur hexafluoride (SF6), a gas used to insulate high-voltage electrical equipment, has six atoms bonded to a single sulfur atom.

  • Elements in period 3 and higher can hold more than 8 electrons in their valence shells. When these elements are the central atom in a molecule, they will sometimes form more than 4 total bonds.
    • These atoms are larger, so more atoms can fit around them. Expanded octets usually form when the outer atoms are small and very electronegative, such as fluorine, chlorine or oxygen.
    • Period 2 atoms (C, N, O, F) are never found with more than 8 valence electrons.
  • Expanded Octet (Hypervalent Molecules) – molecules that contain central atoms with more than 8 electrons in their valence shell.
Sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) is an example of a molecule with an expanded octet. Note how the sulfur in the center of the molecule has 6 single bonds, giving it a total of 12 valence electrons.

Molecular Geometry

Molecules are 3-dimensional.

Molecular Geometry

The shape of a molecule determines how it behaves. Enzymes in the body, for example, react only with molecules that fit precisely into them, much like a key fits a lock.

  • The arrangement of atoms within a molecule can have a significant impact on how that molecule will react. Small, simple molecules can sometimes fit into areas where large, bulky molecules cannot.
  • Although we draw molecules on paper, in 2 dimensions, it is important to be able to visualize how they appear in 3 dimensions.
  • Molecular Geometry – the 3-dimensional arrangement of atoms in a molecule.
  • The geometry of a molecule is dependent on how many bonds and how many lone pairs surround the central atom.
Although we may draw a Lewis structure for methane (CH4) like the diagram on the left, we must remember that the molecule exists in 3 dimensions and appears closer to the image on the right.

Wedge and Dash Notation

  • In order to convey 3-dimensional structures on a 2-dimensional page, chemists use a type of visual known as wedge-and-dash notation.
    • Straight lines represent bonds in the plane of the paper.
    • Solid wedges represent bonds pointing out of the paper (towards you).
    • Dashed lines represent bonds pointing into the paper (away from you).

VSEPR Theory

  • The shape of a molecule is determined by the repulsion of electrons in both bonds and lone pairs surrounding the molecule’s central atom.
  • Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) Theory – negatively charged electrons will repel one another. Therefore, bonds and lone pairs will arrange themselves in such a way as to maximize the 3-dimensional space around the atom.
  • Lone pairs of electrons have a greater repulsive strength than electrons in bonds due to their higher concentration of electron density.
Lone pairs have more repulsive strength than electrons in bonds. Note how NH3 has 3 bonds just like BF3; however, the presence of a lone pair forces them closer together.
