The Scientific Method

Science is not a single recipe. Scientists ask questions, observe, reason from evidence, test their ideas and share them with others, in whatever order a problem demands.

Part1

Science as Problem Solving

Solving Problems Scientifically

Say, for instance, that you bake a loaf of bread and it comes out of the oven flat and dense instead of tall and airy. You start to think about what might have gone wrong. Perhaps the yeast was too old and had died. Perhaps the water you mixed it with was too hot and killed it. Perhaps you did not let the dough rise long enough. Each of these is a possible explanation, and each one can be checked. The yeast packet is well before its expiration date, so the first idea seems unlikely. You let the dough rise for the full two hours, which rules out the third idea. When you test a pinch of the same yeast in lukewarm water it foams, but in water straight from the kettle nothing happens. The explanation that survives the evidence is that the water was too hot.

Without realizing it, you worked through a scientific investigation. You began with an observation and a question, proposed several possible explanations, gathered evidence to test each one, and kept the one that the evidence supported. A possible explanation like “the water was too hot” is referred to as a hypothesis.

Hypothesis – a possible explanation, or a testable prediction, that can be supported or ruled out by evidence.

Evidence – observations and measurements used to support or rule out an explanation.

Note how differently the evidence treated the three ideas. Two of the hypotheses were ruled out for certain, but the third was only supported. It is the best explanation we have, and it would take new evidence to overturn it, but no amount of evidence can prove an idea beyond all doubt.

Scientists follow the same general pattern, although real investigations rarely move through the steps in a neat line. A surprising result can send a scientist back to ask a new question, and a test may need to be repeated many times before a conclusion is drawn. The steps of a scientific investigation are:

  • A scientific investigation usually follows a pattern like this one, although real scientists often repeat or reorder the steps:
    1. Make an observation and ask a question about it.
    2. Propose possible explanations (hypotheses) that could answer the question.
    3. Gather evidence that could support or rule out each explanation.
    4. Draw a conclusion: keep the explanation that survives the evidence.
    5. Share the results so that others can check them.
  • Hypothesis – a possible explanation, or a testable prediction, that can be supported or ruled out by evidence.
  • Evidence – observations and measurements used to support or rule out an explanation.
  • Evidence can rule out an explanation for certain, but it can only support an explanation, never prove it beyond all doubt.

The Science and Engineering Practices

The steps of an investigation are a useful summary, but they make science sound more orderly than it really is. A better description is that scientists and engineers use a set of eight practices, choosing whichever ones a problem demands, often using several at once and often repeating them. Consider a chemist trying to understand why a new battery loses its charge too quickly. She might begin by building a model of how ions move inside it, then plan an investigation, analyze the data, revise the model and run the investigation again, all before arguing for an explanation in front of other scientists.

The eight practices are listed in the table, along with what each one looks like in a chemistry class. Looking back, the flat bread investigation used only some of them, since not every investigation needs all eight.

  • Scientists use these practices in whatever order a problem demands, often several at once, and often repeating some of them.
  • Not every investigation uses all eight.

Reasoning from Evidence

Observations, Inferences and Models

When a strip of blue litmus paper is dipped into a solution and turns red, two very different kinds of statements can be made about what happened. The first is “the litmus paper turned red.” Anyone watching would agree on this, since the color change can be seen directly. The second is “the solution is acidic.” No one can see the particles that make a solution acidic; this statement is a conclusion drawn from the color change. Scientists are careful to keep these two kinds of statements apart. The first is an observation, while the second is an inference.

Observation – information gathered directly with the senses or with instruments, such as “the litmus paper turned red.”

Inference – a conclusion about something that cannot be observed directly, based on observations, such as “the solution is acidic.”

The distinction matters since observations and inferences can be trusted to different degrees. Different observers almost always agree on an observation, but they may reasonably disagree about an inference, since more than one explanation could fit the same observation. In fact, most of the central ideas in chemistry are inferred, not observed. No one has ever seen an atom the way we see a beaker. Our picture of an atom, with a nucleus surrounded by electrons, was built up from indirect evidence, such as the way alpha particles bounce off gold foil and the colors of light given off by heated gases.

A picture like this is referred to as a model. A model is never a perfect copy of the real thing, and it does not need to be. The Bohr model of the atom, with electrons circling the nucleus like planets, is wrong in many details, yet it is still useful since it explains why atoms give off only certain colors of light. Thus, a model is judged by how well it explains and predicts observations, not by how much it looks like reality.

Model – a diagram, physical object, equation or simulation that stands for something that cannot be observed directly.

  • Observation – information gathered directly with the senses or with instruments, such as “the litmus paper turned red.”
  • Inference – a conclusion about something that cannot be observed directly, based on observations, such as “the solution is acidic.”
  • Different observers usually agree on an observation, but they may disagree about an inference.
  • Model – a diagram, physical object, equation or simulation that stands for something that cannot be observed directly.
    • Most of the central ideas in chemistry, such as atoms, electrons and bonds, are inferred, not observed.
    • A model is judged by how well it explains and predicts observations, not by how much it looks like the real thing.

Inductive and Deductive Reasoning

Drop a small piece of sodium into a dish of water and it races across the surface, fizzing and sometimes bursting into flame. Do it again with a fresh piece, and the same thing happens. After seeing the same result many times, it is reasonable to conclude that sodium reacts violently with water. This kind of thinking, from many specific observations to one general conclusion, is referred to as inductive reasoning. Most scientific laws were first reached this way, by noticing a pattern that held true in every case that was tested.

Inductive Reasoning – reasoning from specific observations to a general conclusion.

Reasoning can also run in the other direction. Suppose we already accept the general rule that acids turn blue litmus paper red, and we are handed a bottle labeled “sulfuric acid.” We can predict, before running any test, that a drop of the liquid will turn blue litmus paper red. Reasoning from a general rule to a specific prediction is referred to as deductive reasoning. This is how scientists test their general conclusions: they use a rule to predict what should happen in a new situation, then check whether it does.

Deductive Reasoning – reasoning from a general rule to a specific prediction.

The two kinds of reasoning work together, and each has its limits. No matter how many times sodium reacts with water, inductive reasoning can never guarantee that the next piece will behave the same way; it can only make the conclusion very likely. On the other hand, a single reliable observation that does not fit can disprove a general conclusion. This is why scientists value results that could have turned out to be wrong, and why a prediction that is tested and confirmed makes an idea much stronger.

  • Inductive Reasoning – reasoning from specific observations to a general conclusion.
    Example
    Sodium reacts violently with water every time it is tested, so sodium reacts violently with water.
  • Deductive Reasoning – reasoning from a general rule to a specific prediction.
    Example
    Acids turn blue litmus paper red. This bottle contains an acid, so it will turn blue litmus paper red.
  • A general conclusion reached by induction can be strongly supported, but a single reliable observation that does not fit can disprove it.

Scientific Knowledge

Hypotheses, Theories and Laws

In the late 1700s, Antoine Lavoisier carefully weighed the substances in sealed containers before and after chemical reactions. What he found was that the total mass never changed, and this pattern, observed in reaction after reaction, became the law of conservation of mass. A statement like this, which describes a pattern or relationship in nature, is referred to as a scientific law. Note what the law does not do: it says that mass is conserved, but it says nothing about why.

Scientific Law – a statement that describes a pattern or relationship in nature, often as an equation. It tells what happens.

The explanation came a few years later from John Dalton, who proposed that all matter is made of tiny particles called atoms, and that a chemical reaction only rearranges atoms into new combinations without creating or destroying any of them. If the atoms are all still there after a reaction, it makes sense that their total mass is the same. A well-tested explanation like this, supported by a large body of evidence, is referred to as a scientific theory. In short, a law describes what happens, while a theory explains why it happens.

Scientific Theory – a well-tested explanation, supported by a large body of evidence, that explains why a pattern occurs. It tells why it happens.

A common misconception is that ideas move up a ladder, with a hypothesis growing into a theory and a theory eventually being promoted to a law. This is not the case. Laws and theories are different kinds of knowledge that do different jobs, and no amount of evidence turns one into the other. Additionally, the everyday meaning of the word “theory,” a hunch or a guess, is nearly the opposite of its scientific meaning. A scientific theory is among the most thoroughly tested ideas in science. Even so, both laws and theories remain open to revision if new, reliable evidence does not fit them.

  • Scientific Law – a statement that describes a pattern or relationship in nature, often as an equation. It tells what happens.
    • Example: the law of conservation of mass (mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction).
  • Scientific Theory – a well-tested explanation, supported by a large body of evidence, that explains why a pattern occurs. It tells why it happens.
    • Example: atomic theory explains the law of conservation of mass: atoms are only rearranged in a reaction, never created or destroyed.
  • A hypothesis does not “grow up” into a theory, and a theory does not become a law. They are different kinds of knowledge with different jobs.
  • Both laws and theories can be revised when new, reliable evidence does not fit them.
  • In science, “theory” does not mean a guess. A scientific theory is among the best-supported ideas we have.

Experiments and Other Ways of Investigating

In a controlled experiment, a scientist changes one variable on purpose, keeps the others the same and measures the result. This makes it possible to say with confidence that one thing causes another, and for many questions in chemistry, it is exactly the right tool.

Experiment – an investigation in which one variable is changed on purpose while others are kept the same, usually to find a cause and its effect (see Experimental Design).

Some questions, however, cannot be answered with an experiment at all. Consider astronomers who want to know what stars are made of. No one can bring a star into a laboratory, heat it to different temperatures and see what happens. Instead, astronomers spread the light from a star into a spectrum and compare its lines with the lines given off by elements heated in the lab. Since each element produces its own pattern of lines, the lines in a star’s light reveal which elements it contains. This is how we know that the Sun is mostly hydrogen and helium, even though no one has ever collected a sample of it.

In cases like this, careful observation, comparison and models take the place of an experiment. The same is true whenever a system is too large, too far away or too slow to change, or when changing it would be unsafe or unethical, such as exposing people to a pollutant on purpose to see whether it makes them sick.

  • Experiment – an investigation in which one variable is changed on purpose while others are kept the same, usually to find a cause and its effect (see Experimental Design).
  • Some questions cannot be answered with a controlled experiment, because the system is too large, too far away, too slow or would be unsafe or unethical to change. Scientists then rely on careful observation, comparison and models.
    • Astronomers cannot experiment on stars, but they can compare the light from thousands of them.
    • Scientists studying the health effects of air pollution cannot expose people to pollution on purpose, so they compare people who already live in more and less polluted places.

Science Is Done by People

In 1989, two well-respected chemists, Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons, announced that they had produced nuclear fusion, the reaction that powers the Sun, in a jar of water on a laboratory bench at room temperature. If it were true, “cold fusion” would have provided nearly limitless clean energy. Within weeks, laboratories around the world tried to repeat the experiment. What they found was that almost none of them could produce the same results, and the few that did could not reproduce them reliably. As a result, the scientific community concluded that the original measurements had been mistaken.

The story shows that science is done by people, and people make mistakes. What a scientist notices, what they record and what they dismiss as unimportant depends partly on what they already expect to find, and the excitement of a possible discovery can make anyone less careful. Science guards against this in two main ways: replication, in which other scientists repeat the investigation, and peer review, in which other experts check the work before it is published.

Replication – repeating an investigation, ideally by a different group of scientists, to see whether the same results are obtained.

Peer Review – the checking of a scientist’s methods, data and conclusions by other experts before the work is published.

This is why scientists share their methods and results openly. A result that can be repeated by others, using the methods described, earns trust. A result that cannot be repeated does not, no matter who reported it.

  • What a scientist notices, and what they dismiss as unimportant, depends partly on what they already believe.
  • Replication – repeating an investigation, ideally by a different group of scientists, to see whether the same results are obtained.
  • Peer Review – the checking of a scientist’s methods, data and conclusions by other experts before the work is published.
  • Replication and peer review help catch mistakes and bias, which is why results are shared rather than kept private.
