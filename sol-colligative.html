Colligative Properties

In winter, New York State spreads hundreds of thousands of tons of road salt on its highways. Salt water freezes at a lower temperature than pure water, so the salt keeps ice from forming even when the air is below 0 °C. The same idea keeps the coolant in a car engine from freezing in winter and from boiling over in summer. In this section we will discuss how dissolved particles change the freezing point and the boiling point of a solvent.

Freezing Point Depression and Boiling Point Elevation

Dissolved particles get in the way of the solvent. To freeze, water molecules must line up into a crystal, and the solute particles interfere; to boil, water molecules must escape from the surface, and the solute particles take up part of it. The effect depends on how many solute particles there are, not on what they are.

  • Colligative Property – a property of a solution that depends on the concentration of dissolved solute particles, not on their identity.
  • Freezing Point Depression – the lowering of the freezing point of a solvent when a solute is dissolved in it.
    • The more dissolved particles, the lower the freezing point.
  • Boiling Point Elevation – the raising of the boiling point of a solvent when a solute is dissolved in it.
    • The more dissolved particles, the higher the boiling point.
Example
Road salt (NaCl or CaCl2) lowers the freezing point of water on roads. Ethylene glycol in a car’s coolant lowers its freezing point and raises its boiling point.

Counting Dissolved Particles

Calcium chloride melts ice at lower temperatures than sodium chloride, which is why it is used on roads and sidewalks during the coldest weeks of winter. Each formula unit of calcium chloride breaks into three ions, while sodium chloride breaks into only two. In this section we will discuss how to count the dissolved particles a solute forms and how to compare the freezing and boiling points of solutions.

Ions per Formula Unit

A molecular solute such as sugar dissolves as whole molecules, so each molecule gives one dissolved particle. A soluble ionic compound dissociates, so each formula unit gives two or more ions.

  • Molecular solutes (sugar, alcohols) form 1 particle per molecule.
  • Ionic solutes form 1 particle for every ion in the formula unit.
    • NaCl → Na+ + Cl−: 2 ions
    • CaCl2 → Ca2+ + 2Cl−: 3 ions
    • AlCl3 → Al3+ + 3Cl−: 4 ions
    • Polyatomic ions stay together: K3PO4 → 3K+ + PO43−: 4 ions
  • For the same concentration, the solute that forms more particles has the greater effect: a lower freezing point and a higher boiling point.
  • Steps to comparing the freezing or boiling points of solutions:
    1. Decide whether each solute is ionic or molecular.
    2. Count the particles each formula unit or molecule forms in water.
    3. Multiply by the concentration to find the concentration of dissolved particles.
    4. Compare: the most particles gives the lowest freezing point and the highest boiling point.
