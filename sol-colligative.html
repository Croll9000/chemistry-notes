Part1

Colligative Properties

Freezing Point Depression and Boiling Point Elevation

Every winter, road crews in New York spread salt on highways before a storm. Pure water freezes at 0 °C, but salt water must be cooled several degrees lower before it freezes, so a road coated with salt stays wet instead of turning to ice. The salt does not melt the ice by warming it; it changes the temperature at which water can freeze. Remarkably, sugar would work too, if it were cheap enough, because the effect depends on how many particles are dissolved and not on what those particles are.

A property of a solution that depends on the concentration of dissolved solute particles, and not on their identity, is referred to as a colligative property. Two colligative properties are especially important. The lowering of the freezing point of a solvent when a solute is dissolved in it is referred to as freezing point depression, while the raising of the boiling point is referred to as boiling point elevation.

If we analyze freezing and boiling at the particle level, it becomes clear why dissolved particles change both. To freeze, water molecules must slow down and lock into the orderly pattern of an ice crystal. Dissolved particles get in the way, so the water must be cooled further, removing more kinetic energy, before the crystal can form. Thus, the more dissolved particles, the lower the freezing point. To boil, water molecules must escape from the liquid fast enough that the vapor pressure equals the pressure of the air above it. Dissolved particles take up part of the surface and hold on to nearby water molecules, so fewer water molecules escape and the vapor pressure is lower at every temperature. The solution must be heated to a higher temperature before its vapor pressure reaches the air pressure. Thus, the more dissolved particles, the higher the boiling point.

A car’s engine coolant takes advantage of both properties at once. The mixture of water and ethylene glycol in the radiator freezes far below 0 °C, which keeps the engine from cracking on a cold winter night, and it boils well above 100 °C, which keeps it from boiling over on a hot summer day.

  • Colligative Property – a property of a solution that depends on the concentration of dissolved solute particles, not on their identity.
  • Freezing Point Depression – the lowering of the freezing point of a solvent when a solute is dissolved in it.
    • The more dissolved particles, the lower the freezing point.
  • Boiling Point Elevation – the raising of the boiling point of a solvent when a solute is dissolved in it.
    • The more dissolved particles, the higher the boiling point.
Example
Road salt (NaCl or CaCl2) lowers the freezing point of water on roads. Ethylene glycol in a car’s coolant lowers its freezing point and raises its boiling point.

Counting Dissolved Particles

Ions per Formula Unit

Rock salt stops working well on roads once the temperature drops below about −10 °C, so during the coldest weeks of winter, many towns switch to calcium chloride. Mole for mole, calcium chloride produces more dissolved particles: each formula unit of sodium chloride breaks into two ions when it dissolves, while each formula unit of calcium chloride breaks into three. Calcium chloride has other advantages as well, such as releasing heat as it dissolves, but since freezing point depression depends on the number of dissolved particles, three ions per formula unit means a greater effect.

Counting dissolved particles therefore depends on what kind of solute dissolves. Molecular solutes (sugar, alcohols) form 1 particle per molecule, because the molecules separate from one another but stay whole. Ionic solutes form 1 particle for every ion in the formula unit, because they dissociate:

  • NaCl → Na+ + Cl−: 2 ions
  • CaCl2 → Ca2+ + 2Cl−: 3 ions
  • AlCl3 → Al3+ + 3Cl−: 4 ions
  • K3PO4 → 3K+ + PO43−: 4 ions, since the polyatomic phosphate ion stays together

The concentration of dissolved particles is the concentration of the solute multiplied by the number of particles each formula unit forms. A 1.0 M solution of NaCl holds 2.0 moles of dissolved ions in every liter, while a 1.0 M solution of sugar holds only 1.0 mole of dissolved molecules. It makes sense, then, that for the same concentration, the solute that forms more particles has the greater effect: a lower freezing point and a higher boiling point. When the concentrations are different, both must be considered, so a 1.5 M sugar solution (1.5 moles of particles per liter) has a smaller effect than a 1.0 M NaCl solution (2.0 moles of particles per liter). To compare the freezing or boiling points of solutions:

  1. Decide whether each solute is ionic or molecular.
  2. Count the particles each formula unit or molecule forms in water.
  3. Multiply by the concentration to find the concentration of dissolved particles.
  4. Compare: the most particles gives the lowest freezing point and the highest boiling point.
