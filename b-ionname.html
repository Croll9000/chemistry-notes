Writing Ionic Formulas

Writing Ionic Formulas

The formula of an ionic compound does not have to be memorized. It follows directly from the charges of the ions. Since the net charge of every ionic compound is zero, the formula shows the lowest ratio of ions that makes the charges cancel.

We can find the charge of each ion from the element’s group on the periodic table. Groups 1, 2 and 13 form 1+, 2+ and 3+ ions. Groups 15, 16 and 17 form 3−, 2− and 1− ions. The positive ion is always written first, and the negative ion second.

Consider aluminum oxide, the mineral that makes up rubies and sapphires. Aluminum forms Al3+ and oxygen forms O2−. The only way to make these charges cancel with whole numbers of ions is to use two aluminum ions (6+) and three oxide ions (6−). The formula is Al2O3.

A shortcut called the criss-cross method finds the ratio quickly. The size of each ion’s charge becomes the subscript of the other ion. To write the formula of an ionic compound:

  • Recall that the net charge of every ionic compound is zero. The formula shows the lowest ratio of ions that makes the charges cancel.

Steps for writing ionic compound formulas:

  1. Write the symbol and charge for each ion in the compound. Make sure to put the positive ion first and the negative ion second.
    • Use the element’s group on the periodic table: groups 1, 2 and 13 form 1+, 2+ and 3+ ions; groups 15, 16 and 17 form 3−, 2− and 1− ions.
  2. “Criss-cross” the charge of the positive ion to become the subscript of the negative ion.
    • Move the charge of the negative ion to become the subscript of the positive ion.
    • Leave off the + and − signs.
  3. Reduce to the lowest ratio.
  4. If there is more than one of a polyatomic ion, surround the polyatomic formula with parentheses (see Polyatomic Ions).

Ex: Write the Formula for Calcium Oxide

Why the Criss-Cross Works

The criss-cross method can seem like a trick, but it is really a shortcut for balancing charges. Each subscript is the number of ions of that kind. In aluminum oxide, each Al3+ ion carries 3 positive charges and each O2− ion carries 2 negative charges. Using the charge of oxygen, 2, as the number of aluminum ions, and the charge of aluminum, 3, as the number of oxide ions guarantees that both totals come out to the same number:

2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0

This also explains why the subscripts must sometimes be reduced. For calcium oxide, criss-crossing Ca2+ and O2− gives Ca2O2. The charges balance, but this is not the simplest ratio. Since an ionic formula shows the lowest ratio of ions, it reduces to CaO.

  • Criss-crossing is a shortcut for balancing charges. Each subscript is the number of ions of that kind.
  • In aluminum oxide, each Al3+ ion carries 3 positive charges and each O2− ion carries 2 negative charges.
    • 2 Al3+ ions give 6+ and 3 O2− ions give 6−. Together: 2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0.
In these ion puzzle pieces, each positive charge is a tab (+) and each negative charge is a slot (−). The pieces lock together only when every tab fills a slot, which means the charges cancel. Two Al3+ pieces (6 tabs) fit three O2− pieces (6 slots): Al2O3.

Naming Ionic Compounds

Type I Binary Ionic Compounds

A compound that contains exactly two elements is referred to as binary. Some metals can only form one ion. Ions of groups 1 and 2 can only have charges of 1+ and 2+ respectively, and group 13 metals such as aluminum normally form 3+ ions, as in aluminum chloride, AlCl3. An ionic compound made of a metal with a fixed charge and a nonmetal is known as a Type I binary ionic compound.

Naming these compounds is simple. Since the charge of the metal is fixed, the charges decide how many of each ion there are, and the name does not need to say. The metal keeps its name. The nonmetal’s ending is changed to “-ide,” so chlorine becomes chloride and oxygen becomes oxide.

The label on a box of iodized salt lists “potassium iodide,” added in tiny amounts to prevent iodine deficiency. Potassium is in group 1, so it can only form K+ ions. There is only one possible compound of potassium and iodine, KI, and the name needs no numbers at all. To name a Type I binary ionic compound:

  • Some metals can take on multiple charges when forming ions, while other metals have only one possible charge to adopt.
    • Ions of groups 1 and 2 can only have charges of 1+ and 2+ respectively.
    • Group 13 metals, such as aluminum, normally form 3+ ions.
      Example
      AlCl3 is aluminum chloride.
  • Binary – containing exactly 2 elements.
  • Type I Binary Ionic Compound – ionic compound containing a metal with a fixed charge and a nonmetal.

Steps for naming Type I binary ionic compounds:

  1. Write the full name of the metal.
  2. Write the name of the nonmetal, remove its ending and add the suffix “-ide”.

Type II Binary Ionic Compounds

Iron forms two different oxides. Iron(II) oxide, FeO, is a black powder. Iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, is the reddish-brown substance in rust. Both are made of iron and oxygen, so calling either one “iron oxide” would not tell us which compound is meant. The problem is that iron can form both Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions.

Many transition metals, and a few other metals such as tin and lead, can have more than one charge, called multiple oxidation states. A compound of one of these metals and a nonmetal is called a Type II binary ionic compound. In its name, the charge of the metal ion is shown with a Roman numeral in parentheses right after the metal’s name. This system was introduced by the German chemist Alfred Stock in 1919.

Be careful with what the Roman numeral means. It gives the charge of one metal ion, not the number of metal ions in the formula. In iron(III) oxide, each iron ion has a 3+ charge, even though the formula contains two iron ions. To name a Type II binary ionic compound:

  • Some transition metals (and a few other metals, like tin and lead) can have multiple oxidation states. When writing the names of ionic compounds containing these metals, it is necessary to specify the oxidation state using Roman numerals.

  • Type II Binary Ionic Compound – ionic compound containing a metal that can take on multiple charges and a nonmetal.

Steps for naming Type II binary ionic compounds:

  1. Write the full name of the metal.
  2. Determine the charge of the metal ion. Indicate the charge using Roman numerals (I, II, III, …) within parentheses.
  3. Write the name of the nonmetal, remove its ending and add the suffix “-ide”.
Iron (Fe) is a metal that can take on multiple charges. It can exist as Fe2+ or Fe3+. When naming ionic compounds containing iron, it is necessary to specify which ion is being used. In FeCl3, Fe3+ is being used, so we use the Roman numeral (III). In FeO, Fe2+ is used, so we use the Roman numeral (II).

Determining the Charge of Transition Metals

To name a Type II compound from its formula, we need the charge of the metal, but the formula does not show it. We can work it out from the nonmetal, since the total positive charge must cancel the total negative charge.

Consider the two oxides of copper. Cu2O is red, and CuO is black. Oxygen has only one possible charge, 2−. In Cu2O, one oxide ion (2−) is balanced by two copper ions, so each copper ion must be 1+. The compound is copper(I) oxide. In CuO, one copper ion balances one oxide ion, so the copper must be 2+, and the compound is copper(II) oxide. To determine the charge of a transition metal:

  • The formula of a Type II compound does not show the metal’s charge. Since the compound is neutral, we can work it out from the nonmetal, which has only one possible charge.
  1. Write the charge of the nonmetal ion.
  2. Count the number of ions of each kind using the subscripts.
  3. Set the total charges equal to each other: the total positive charge must cancel the total negative charge.
  4. Divide the total positive charge by the number of metal ions to find the charge of each one.

Work out the metal’s charge one step at a time, then name the compound.
