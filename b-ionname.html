Writing Ionic Formulas

Every ionic compound is neutral overall, so the charges of its ions must cancel. This single rule determines the formula of any ionic compound, from the sodium chloride in table salt to the aluminum oxide that coats and protects aluminum foil.

  • Recall that the net charge of every ionic compound is zero. The formula shows the lowest ratio of ions that makes the charges cancel.

Steps for writing ionic compound formulas:

  1. Write the symbol and charge for each ion in the compound. Make sure to put the positive ion first and the negative ion second.
    • Use the element’s group on the periodic table: groups 1, 2 and 13 form 1+, 2+ and 3+ ions; groups 15, 16 and 17 form 3−, 2− and 1− ions.
  2. “Criss-cross” the charge of the positive ion to become the subscript of the negative ion. Move the charge of the negative ion to become the subscript of the positive ion. Leave off the + and − signs.
  3. Reduce to the lowest ratio.
  4. If there is more than one of a polyatomic ion, surround the polyatomic formula with parentheses (see Polyatomic Ions).

Ex: Write the Formula for Calcium Oxide

Why the Criss-Cross Works

  • Criss-crossing is a shortcut for balancing charges. Each subscript is the number of ions of that kind.
  • In aluminum oxide, each Al3+ ion carries 3 positive charges and each O2− ion carries 2 negative charges.
    • 2 Al3+ ions give 6+ and 3 O2− ions give 6−. Together: 2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0.
In these ion puzzle pieces, each positive charge is a tab (+) and each negative charge is a slot (−). The pieces lock together only when every tab fills a slot, which means the charges cancel. Two Al3+ pieces (6 tabs) fit three O2− pieces (6 slots): Al2O3.

Drag ion puzzle pieces together until every + tab fills a − slot and the compound is neutral, then write the formula.

Naming Ionic Compounds

Name the metal, then the nonmetal with an “-ide” ending.

Type I Binary Ionic Compounds

The name of an ionic compound identifies the ions it contains. Most of these names follow a simple pattern that can be learned once and applied to hundreds of compounds.

  • Some metals can take on multiple charges when forming ions, while other metals have only one possible charge to adopt.
    • Ions of groups 1 and 2 can only have charges of 1+ and 2+ respectively.
    • Group 13 metals, such as aluminum, normally form 3+ ions. Ex: AlCl3 is aluminum chloride.
  • Binary – containing exactly 2 elements.
  • Type I Binary Ionic Compound – ionic compound containing a metal with a fixed charge and a nonmetal.

Steps for naming Type I binary ionic compounds:

  1. Write the full name of the metal.
  2. Write the name of the nonmetal, remove its ending and add the suffix “-ide”.

Type II Binary Ionic Compounds

Iron forms two different oxides: one is a black powder and the other is the red-brown rust found on old cars. Since both contain only iron and oxygen, chemists need a naming system that tells them apart.

  • Some transition metals (and a few other metals, like tin and lead) can have multiple oxidation states. When writing the names of ionic compounds containing these metals, it is necessary to specify the oxidation state using Roman numerals.
    • The periodic table below highlights the common metals with more than one oxidation state and lists their usual charges. Click an element to see its ions and their names.
  • Type II Binary Ionic Compound – ionic compound containing a metal that can take on multiple charges and a nonmetal.

Steps for naming Type II binary ionic compounds:

  1. Write the full name of the metal.
  2. Determine the charge of the metal ion. Indicate the charge using Roman numerals (I, II, III, …) within parentheses.
  3. Write the name of the nonmetal, remove its ending and add the suffix “-ide”.
Iron (Fe) is a metal that can take on multiple charges. It can exist as Fe2+ or Fe3+. When naming ionic compounds containing iron, it is necessary to specify which ion is being used. In FeCl3, Fe3+ is being used, so we use the Roman numeral (III). In FeO, Fe2+ is used, so we use the Roman numeral (II).

Determining the Charge of Transition Metals

  • The formula of a Type II compound does not show the metal’s charge. Since the compound is neutral, we can work it out from the nonmetal, which has only one possible charge.
  1. Write the charge of the nonmetal ion.
  2. Count the number of ions of each kind using the subscripts.
  3. Set the total charges equal to each other: the total positive charge must cancel the total negative charge.
  4. Divide the total positive charge by the number of metal ions to find the charge of each one.

Work out the metal’s charge one step at a time, then name the compound.
