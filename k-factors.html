What Changes a Reaction Rate?

Factors That Affect Reaction Rate

A cook slows the spoiling of food by putting it in the refrigerator, and a mechanic speeds up the reactions in a car’s exhaust with a catalytic converter. Chemists can make a reaction faster or slower by changing its conditions, and six factors affect the rate of a reaction: the nature of the reactants, concentration, surface area, temperature, pressure (for gases only) and a catalyst. Every one of these factors works the same way. A change that increases the number of effective collisions per second increases the rate of the reaction, and a change that decreases the number of effective collisions decreases the rate. In the labs on this page, each container makes the same product from reactants A and B, and a stopwatch times how long it takes to make a set amount of product: the shorter the time, the faster the rate.

  • Six factors affect the rate of a reaction:
    1. the nature of the reactants
    2. concentration
    3. surface area
    4. temperature
    5. pressure (gases only)
    6. a catalyst
  • A change that increases the number of effective collisions per second increases the rate of the reaction. A change that decreases the number of effective collisions decreases the rate.
  • In the labs on this page, each container makes the same product, AB, from reactants A and B. The stopwatch times how long the container takes to make a set amount of product; the shorter the time, the faster the rate.

The Nature of the Reactants

The Nature of the Reactants

When a solution of silver nitrate is poured into salt water, a white solid appears the instant the two liquids touch, while burning a candle or cooking an egg needs heat and time to get going. Different substances react at different rates because of the bonds that must break before new bonds can form. Ionic compounds dissolved in water react faster than covalent (molecular) substances, because their ions are already separated, so no bonds have to break before the ions can combine. Molecules, on the other hand, must break covalent bonds before new ones form, so more of their collisions fall short of the activation energy. Additionally, gases and liquids generally react faster than solids, because their particles move freely and collide more often.

  • Ionic compounds dissolved in water react faster than covalent (molecular) substances. The ions are already separated, so no bonds have to break before the ions can combine.
  • Molecules must break covalent bonds before new bonds form, so more of their collisions fall short of the activation energy.
  • Gases and liquids generally react faster than solids, because their particles move freely and collide more often.

Concentration

Concentration

A glowing wooden splint only smolders in air, which is about 21% oxygen, but it bursts back into flame when it is pushed into a test tube of pure oxygen. Concentration measures how crowded the reactant particles are. With more reactant particles in the same volume, the particles collide more frequently, so there are more effective collisions every second. Thus, increasing the concentration of a reactant increases the rate of the reaction. Concentration applies to reactants that are gases or are dissolved in solution.

  • Increasing the concentration of a reactant increases the rate of the reaction.
  • With more reactant particles in the same volume, the particles collide more frequently, so there are more effective collisions per second.
  • Concentration applies to reactants that are gases or are dissolved in solution (aq).

Surface Area

Surface Area

A log burns slowly in a fireplace, but sawdust or flour floating in the air can burn so fast that it explodes, which is why grain elevators and flour mills control the dust in their air. Only the particles on the outside of a solid can be hit by other reactant particles. Surface area is the amount of a solid’s surface that is exposed to the other reactants. Cutting, grinding or powdering a solid increases its surface area without changing its mass, so more of its particles can be hit at the same time. As a result, increasing the surface area of a solid reactant increases the rate of the reaction. Surface area applies only to solid reactants.

  • Surface area – the amount of a solid’s surface that is exposed to the other reactants.
  • Increasing the surface area of a solid reactant increases the rate of the reaction. Cutting, grinding or powdering a solid increases its surface area without changing its mass.
  • More exposed particles can be hit at the same time, so there are more effective collisions per second.
  • Surface area applies to solid reactants.

Temperature

Temperature

A glow stick dropped in hot water glows brightly but fades within an hour, while one kept in a freezer glows dimly but can last for a day. Recall that temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles. At a higher temperature, the particles move faster, which has two effects: they collide more frequently, and more of their collisions have enough energy to reach the activation energy. Therefore, increasing the temperature increases the rate of the reaction, and lowering the temperature slows reactions down, which is why refrigerators keep food from spoiling.

  • Increasing the temperature increases the rate of the reaction.
  • At a higher temperature the particles move faster, so:
    • they collide more frequently, and
    • more of the collisions have enough energy to reach the activation energy.
  • Lowering the temperature slows reactions down. This is why refrigerators keep food from spoiling.

Pressure

Pressure (Gases Only)

A diesel engine has no spark plugs. The piston squeezes the fuel and air into a small space, and the mixture reacts on its own. Pushing a gas into a smaller volume crowds its particles together, so they collide more frequently; in other words, increasing the pressure of a gas increases its concentration. Thus, increasing the pressure on reacting gases increases the rate of the reaction. Pressure, however, affects only reactions with gaseous reactants. Solids and liquids cannot be compressed, so a change in pressure does not change how often their particles collide.

  • Increasing the pressure on reacting gases increases the rate of the reaction.
  • Pushing the gas into a smaller volume crowds the particles together, so they collide more frequently. Increasing the pressure of a gas increases its concentration.
  • Pressure affects only reactions with gaseous reactants. Solids and liquids cannot be compressed, so a change in pressure does not change how often their particles collide.

Catalysts

Catalysts

The catalytic converter in a car’s exhaust system holds a thin coating of platinum, palladium and rhodium that speeds up the reactions changing poisonous carbon monoxide into carbon dioxide, and the metals are not used up. A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction by providing a different reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, without being used up. With a lower activation energy, more of the collisions are effective, so adding a catalyst increases the rate of the reaction. A catalyst changes the path of a reaction, but it does not change the reactants, the products or the heat of reaction (ΔH). Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up the reactions in living things.

  • Catalyst – a substance that increases the rate of a reaction by providing a different reaction pathway with a lower activation energy; the catalyst is not used up in the reaction.
  • Adding a catalyst increases the rate of the reaction. With a lower activation energy, more of the collisions are effective.
  • A catalyst does not change the reactants, the products or the heat of reaction (ΔH).
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up the reactions in living things.

Putting the Factors Together

Which Factors Apply?

Engineers who design a chemical plant choose the temperature, the pressure, the size of the solid pieces and the catalyst to make a product as quickly and cheaply as possible, so they need to know which factors apply to which reactants. Some factors apply only to reactants in a particular phase: concentration to gases and solutions, surface area to solids and pressure to gases. A factor that does not apply to the reactants does not change the rate. Raising the pressure on a reaction between a solid and a solution, for instance, has no effect.
