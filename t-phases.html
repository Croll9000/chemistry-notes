Phases of Matter & Phase Changes

When particles gain or lose potential energy, they move apart or closer together and the substance changes phase. The phase of a substance depends on its intermolecular forces, its temperature and the pressure on it.

Part1

Phases of Matter

Solid, liquid and gas.

Phases of Matter

The same water molecules make up an ice cube, a glass of water and the steam over a kettle. What changes from one phase to the next is how close the particles are and how they move.

  • Solids – written with (s) after the formula, like I2(s).
    • The particles are packed close together in fixed positions, where they vibrate. This is due to the strong intermolecular forces between them.
  • Liquids – written with (l), like H2O(l).
    • The particles are close together but are able to flow past each other. This is due to the moderate intermolecular forces between them.
  • Gases – written with (g), like CO2(g).
    • The particles are very far apart and move quickly in straight lines until they collide. This is due to the weak intermolecular forces between them.

Part2

Phase Changes

Six changes, three endothermic and three exothermic.

Phase Changes

Puddles dry up, frost forms on a car window, and dry ice disappears into fog without ever becoming a liquid. Each of these is a phase change.

  • When the particles in a substance gain or lose potential energy, their arrangement changes and they undergo a phase change. A phase change is a physical change: the particles do not change identity.
  • Melting (Fusion) – the physical process of turning a solid into a liquid.
    • Melting is endothermic: the particles absorb heat from their surroundings. They move farther apart and gain potential energy.
  • Freezing – the physical process of turning a liquid into a solid.
    • Freezing is exothermic: the particles release heat to their surroundings. They move closer together and lose potential energy.
  • Melting and freezing happen at the same temperature, the melting point. The only difference is whether heat is being added or removed.
  • Vaporization – the physical process of turning a liquid into a gas.
    • Vaporization is endothermic: particles absorb heat, move farther apart and gain potential energy.
    • Vaporization that happens only at the surface of a liquid, below the boiling point, is called evaporation. Vaporization throughout the liquid at the boiling point is called boiling.
  • Condensation – the physical process of turning a gas into a liquid.
    • Condensation is exothermic: particles release heat, move closer together and lose potential energy.
  • Vaporization and condensation happen at the same temperature, the boiling point.
  • Sublimation – the physical process of turning a solid directly into a gas. Example: dry ice, CO2(s) → CO2(g). Sublimation is endothermic.
  • Deposition – the physical process of turning a gas directly into a solid. Example: frost forming from water vapor. Deposition is exothermic.
  • In short: changes that spread particles apart (melting, vaporization, sublimation) absorb heat; changes that bring particles together (freezing, condensation, deposition) release heat.

Part3

Phase Equilibrium

Two phases, no net change.

Phase Equilibrium

A sealed bottle of water left on a shelf never dries out, even though water molecules are always escaping from the surface. Just as many molecules return to the liquid as leave it.

  • When a substance is at its melting point or boiling point, two phases of matter can exist at the same time. If heat is not being added or removed, the substance is in phase equilibrium.
    • Phase Equilibrium – condition in which two phases of matter exist at the same time with no net change in the amount of either one.
  • At phase equilibrium, particles keep changing from one phase into the other and back again, at equal rates. So there is no net change in the number of particles in either phase.
  • For a substance to be in phase equilibrium:
    • it must be at a phase change temperature with no heat being added or removed, and
    • it must be in a closed system, so particles cannot escape.
  • Examples: ice and water at 0 °C in an insulated, sealed container; water and water vapor in a sealed bottle.

Part4

Determining Phase of Matter

Intermolecular forces, temperature and pressure.

Phases of Matter and Intermolecular Forces

At room temperature, iodine is a solid, water is a liquid and carbon dioxide is a gas. Three things decide the phase of a substance: its intermolecular forces, its temperature and the pressure on it.

  • Intermolecular Forces (of attraction) – attractive forces between particles in a sample of matter.
    • Intermolecular forces arise from small charges on particles. Oppositely charged parts of particles (positive and negative) attract each other.
    • If a substance has weak intermolecular forces, its particles are very spread out, as in a gas.
    • If a substance has strong intermolecular forces, its particles are tightly packed, as in a solid.

Phases of Matter and Temperature

Every solid becomes a liquid and then a gas if it is heated enough (unless it breaks down first). Temperature decides how much energy the particles have to pull away from one another.

  • As the temperature of a substance increases, it changes from solid to liquid to gas. As the temperature decreases, it changes from gas to liquid to solid.
  • For a given substance, particles in the solid phase have low kinetic energy and particles in the gas phase have high kinetic energy. Solid particles are still moving: they vibrate in fixed positions.
  • Pure substances change phase at specific temperatures:
    • Melting Point – the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It is the same temperature at which the liquid freezes.
    • Boiling Point – the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid. It is the same temperature at which the gas condenses.
    • Melting points and boiling points are characteristic properties of substances (at a given pressure), so they can be used to identify an unknown substance.

Phases of Matter and Pressure

Pressure comes from the air above us or from the water around a diver. Pushing harder on a substance pushes its particles closer together.

  • Pressure – the amount of force per unit area pushing down on a substance.
  • The higher the pressure on a substance, the closer together its particles are pushed. This favors the condensed phases (liquid and solid) over the gas phase.
    • For most substances, the solid is denser than the liquid, so high pressure favors the solid.
    • Exceptions: a few substances have a solid that is less dense than their liquid, so high pressure favors the liquid. Water is the best-known example: ice floats on water, and squeezing ice can melt it. Gallium, bismuth, silicon, germanium and antimony behave the same way.
  • The lower the pressure on a substance, the farther apart its particles can be. This makes the substance more likely to be a gas.
  • This is why water boils at a lower temperature on a mountaintop (lower air pressure), and why a pressure cooker cooks food faster (higher pressure raises the boiling point).

*For most substances. Water, gallium, bismuth, silicon, germanium and antimony are exceptions: high pressure favors their liquid.
