Phases of Matter & Phase Changes

When particles gain or lose potential energy, they move apart or closer together and the substance changes phase. The phase of a substance depends on its intermolecular forces, its temperature and the pressure on it.

Part1

Phases of Matter

Mercury flows and takes the shape of its container at room temperature, but frozen below −39 °C it keeps its own shape. In both phases the particles are the same mercury atoms; only how they are arranged and how strongly they are held in place differ. In this section, we will compare the solid, liquid and gas phases.

Phases of Matter

The same H2O molecules make up a glacier, the ocean and the invisible water vapor in the air you are breathing. Nothing about the molecules themselves changes from one form to the next. What changes is how close together the particles are, how strongly they hold on to one another and how freely they move. In Unit 1 you learned to tell the phases apart; in this unit you will see how energy moves a substance from one phase to another.

  • Phase of Matter – the form that matter can take.
    • A substance’s phase can be determined by looking at the arrangement of its particles.
  • There are 3 phases of matter:
    • Solid – the particles are packed close together in fixed positions, where they vibrate. This is due to the strong intermolecular forces between them.
      • Symbol – (s), as in I2(s)
    • Liquid – the particles are close together but are able to flow past each other. This is due to the moderate intermolecular forces between them.
      • Symbol – (l), as in H2O(l)
    • Gas – the particles are very far apart and move quickly in straight lines until they collide. This is due to the weak intermolecular forces between them.
      • Symbol – (g), as in CO2(g)
  • A substance dissolved in water is written (aq), as in NaCl(aq).

Comparing the Phases

The properties of each phase, including its shape, its volume and how easily it flows, follow directly from how its particles are arranged and how strongly they attract one another. This is the same comparison table you used in Unit 1.

Particles in a solid are still moving. They vibrate in fixed positions. Particles in a gas move fast and fill their whole container.
For a given substance, particles in the solid phase have low kinetic energy and particles in the gas phase have high kinetic energy.
Part2

Phase Changes

Turning 1 gram of liquid water at 100 °C into steam absorbs 2,260 J, almost seven times the 334 J needed to melt 1 gram of ice. Separating particles completely to form a gas requires far more energy than loosening them into a liquid. In this section, we will discuss the six phase changes and the energy each one absorbs or releases.

Phase Changes

Water on Earth is constantly changing phase. Each year, roughly 500,000 cubic kilometers of water evaporate from the oceans and land, condense into clouds and fall back as rain and snow. None of these changes produce a new substance. Instead, heat added to or removed from the water changes how far apart its particles are and how much potential energy they store.

  • For a given substance at a given pressure, its phase can be changed by adding or removing heat from it.
  • When the particles in a substance gain or lose potential energy, their arrangement changes and they undergo a phase change.
    • A phase change is a physical change: the identity of the substance does not change.
  • During phase changes energy is either absorbed or released.
    • Endothermic – a change in which energy is absorbed by a substance (melting, vaporization, sublimation)
    • Exothermic – a change in which energy is released by a substance (freezing, condensation, deposition)
Endothermic: energy absorbedExothermic: energy released

Vaporization (boiling): liquid → gasCondensation: gas → liquid

Melting and Freezing

A frozen lake in January and a glass of iced tea in July both contain water at 0 °C, but in one heat is leaving and in the other heat is arriving. For a pure substance, melting and freezing occur at exactly the same temperature. Which change happens depends only on the direction in which heat flows.

  • Melting (Fusion) – the physical process of turning a solid into a liquid.
    • Melting is endothermic: the particles absorb heat from their surroundings. They move farther apart and gain potential energy.
    • H2O(s) + heat → H2O(l)
  • Freezing – the physical process of turning a liquid into a solid.
    • Freezing is exothermic: the particles release heat to their surroundings. They move closer together and lose potential energy.
    • H2O(l) → H2O(s) + heat
  • Melting Point – the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It is the same temperature at which the liquid freezes.
    • The only difference is whether heat is being added or removed.
  • While a substance is changing phase, its temperature stays the same.
    Example
    Ice stays at 0 °C until it has all melted.

Vaporization and Condensation

Wet laundry hung outside dries on a cool day without ever approaching 100 °C, while a pot of water on the stove bubbles vigorously at exactly 100 °C. Both are vaporization: particles of a liquid gaining enough energy to escape into the gas phase. The difference lies in where the escape happens, only at the surface or throughout the entire liquid.

  • Vaporization – the physical process of turning a liquid into a gas.
    • Vaporization is endothermic: particles absorb heat, move farther apart and gain potential energy.
    • Evaporation – vaporization that happens only at the surface of a liquid, below the boiling point.
    • Boiling is vaporization throughout the liquid at the boiling point. Bubbles of gas form inside the liquid and rise to the surface.
  • Condensation – the physical process of turning a gas into a liquid.
    • Condensation is exothermic: particles release heat, move closer together and lose potential energy.
    • NH3(g) → NH3(l) + heat
  • Boiling Point – the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid. It is the same temperature at which the gas condenses.
    Example
    Boiling water stays at 100 °C until it has all boiled away.
  • Evaporation cools the liquid left behind.
    • The fastest particles escape, so the average kinetic energy of the particles that remain goes down.
    • This is why sweating cools you.

Sublimation and Deposition

Dry ice at a concert turns straight into a cold fog without ever leaving a puddle, and on a freezing night frost grows on a car window without any liquid water first. Some substances can skip the liquid phase entirely.

  • Sublimation – the physical process of turning a solid directly into a gas.
    • Sublimation is endothermic.
    Example
    Dry ice, CO2(s) + heat → CO2(g). Iodine and mothballs also sublime.
  • Deposition – the physical process of turning a gas directly into a solid.
    • Deposition is exothermic.
    Example
    Frost forming from water vapor, H2O(g) → H2O(s) + heat.
  • In short: changes that spread particles apart (melting, vaporization, sublimation) absorb heat; changes that bring particles together (freezing, condensation, deposition) release heat.

Phase Equilibrium

In a closed jar of water at 25 °C, water vapor eventually makes up about 3% of the air above the liquid, and that amount then stays constant. Molecules keep escaping from the liquid and returning to it, but once the two rates are equal, the amounts of liquid and vapor no longer change. In this section, we will discuss phase equilibrium and the conditions needed for it.

Phase Equilibrium

A sealed bottle of water can sit on a shelf for years without drying out, even though water molecules are always escaping from the surface of the liquid. Above the water, molecules of vapor are just as constantly crashing back into the liquid. When the two processes happen at the same rate, the amounts stop changing, even though the particles never stop moving.

  • When a substance is at its melting point or boiling point, two phases of matter can exist at the same time. If heat is not being added or removed, the substance is in phase equilibrium.
    • Phase Equilibrium – condition in which two phases of matter exist at the same time with no net change in the amount of either one.
  • At phase equilibrium, particles keep changing from one phase into the other and back again, at equal rates. So there is no net change in the number of particles in either phase.
  • For a substance to be in phase equilibrium:
    • it must be at a phase change temperature with no heat being added or removed, and
    • it must be in a closed system, so particles cannot escape.
Example
Ice and water at 0 °C in an insulated, sealed container; water and water vapor in a sealed bottle.

Determining Phase of Matter

The phase that a substance takes depends on three factors: its intermolecular forces, its temperature and the pressure acting on it. In this section we will revisit these factors from Unit 1 and discuss each in greater depth.

Determining Phase of Matter

At room temperature, oxygen is a gas, water is a liquid and iron is a solid. Every substance has a phase under a given set of conditions, so it is important to understand what determines it. In this section we will review the three factors from Unit 1 and discuss each one in greater detail.

The phase of matter that a specific substance will take is dependent on 3 things:

  1. Intermolecular forces (attractions between particles)
  2. Temperature
  3. Pressure

Phases of Matter and Intermolecular Forces

Particles in a sample of matter are not completely independent of one another. They are attracted to the particles around them, and the strength of these attractions helps explain why some substances are solids at room temperature while others are gases.

  • Intermolecular Forces (of attraction) – attractive forces between particles in a sample of matter
    • Intermolecular forces arise due to small charges possessed by particles.
      • Opposite charges attract.
      • The slightly positive end of one particle is attracted to the slightly negative end of another.
      • The stronger the attraction, the harder the particles are to pull apart.
    • If a substance has weak intermolecular forces, its particles will be very spread out as in a gas.
    • If a substance has strong intermolecular forces, its particles will be tightly packed as in a solid.
  • Stronger attractions take more energy to overcome, so substances with stronger intermolecular forces have higher melting points and boiling points.
    • Melting and boiling do not break the bonds inside molecules. They only overcome the attractions between molecules.
  • The types of intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces) are covered in detail in Unit 4: Intermolecular Forces of Attraction.

Phases of Matter and Temperature

Every solid becomes a liquid and then a gas if it is heated enough, unless it breaks down first. Temperature decides how much kinetic energy the particles have to pull away from the attractions holding them together.

  • Temperature affects phase.
    • As temperature increases, a substance changes from solid to liquid to gas.
    • As temperature decreases, a substance changes from gas to liquid to solid.
  • Temperature is a measurement of average kinetic energy.
    • Particles of a substance in the solid phase have low kinetic energy.
    • Particles of the same substance in the gas phase have high kinetic energy.
  • Comparing the temperature to a substance’s melting and boiling points tells its phase.
    • Below the melting point: solid
    • Between the melting point and the boiling point: liquid
    • Above the boiling point: gas
  • Melting points and boiling points are characteristic properties of substances (at a given pressure), so they can be used to identify an unknown substance.

Phases of Matter and Pressure

Water does not always boil at 100 °C. In Denver, Colorado, about 1.6 km above sea level, water boils at about 95 °C because the air pressure is lower. Pressure cookers work the opposite way, raising the pressure so that water boils at a higher temperature and food cooks faster.

  • Pressure – the amount of force per unit area pushing down on a substance
    • Unit – atmospheres (atm) and kilopascals (kPa)
    • Measuring device – barometer (air pressure) or pressure gauge
  • The higher the pressure pushing down on a substance, the closer together the particles are pushed.
    • The substance is more likely to be in a condensed phase (liquid or solid).
    • For most substances, the solid is denser than the liquid, so high pressure favors the solid.
    • Exceptions: a few substances have a solid that is less dense than their liquid, so high pressure favors the liquid. Water is the best-known example: ice floats on water, and squeezing ice can melt it. Gallium, bismuth, silicon, germanium and antimony behave the same way.
  • The lower the pressure pushing down on a substance, the farther apart the particles will be.
    • The substance is more likely to be a gas.
Example
The boiling point of water changes with pressure.
  • 100 °C at 1 atm
  • About 81 °C at 0.5 atm (high in the mountains)
  • About 120 °C at 2 atm (inside a pressure cooker)

*For most substances. Water, gallium, bismuth, silicon, germanium and antimony are exceptions: high pressure favors their liquid.
