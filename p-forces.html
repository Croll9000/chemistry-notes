What Causes the Trends?

The periodic trends are caused by the attraction between the positive nucleus and the negative electrons. The strength of this attraction depends on nuclear charge, distance (number of shells) and electron shielding.

Part1

Nuclear Charge

More protons create a stronger attraction.

Nuclear Charge

Atomic radius, ionization energy and electronegativity may seem like separate topics, but each one comes back to a single idea: how strongly the nucleus holds on to its electrons. Before examining these trends, it is important to understand what determines the strength of that pull.

  • Recall that the nucleus contains protons (+) and neutrons (0).
    • The net charge of the nucleus is positive.
  • The nuclear charge of an atom is equal to its number of protons, or the element’s atomic number.
  • The positive charge of the nucleus attracts negatively charged electrons.
    • It keeps an atom’s electrons in their energy levels.
    • If strong enough, it can also pull electrons from other atoms.
  • The greater the nuclear charge, the stronger the pull on electrons.
    • This assumes all other things are kept the same (size, electron shielding, etc.).
  • This is Coulomb’s law from Unit 2: a bigger charge means a bigger force.
Part2

Periods and Shells

A greater distance creates a weaker attraction.

Periods and Shells

  • Recall that each new period (row) on the periodic table adds another energy level (shell) to the atom.
    • The period number tells you how many shells the atom has.
  • As atoms get bigger, their ability to attract electrons gets weaker.
    • The distance between the positive nucleus and the outer electrons increases.
    • Coulomb’s law again: a greater distance means a smaller force.
Part3

Electron Shielding

Inner electrons reduce the attraction.

Electron Shielding

  • Electrons within an atom repel one another.
  • Inner (core) electrons repel outer (valence) electrons.
    • This reduces the attraction between the valence electrons and the nucleus.
  • Shielding Effect – inner (core) electrons repel valence electrons, reducing the attraction between an electron and the nucleus.
  • As more shells of electrons are added, the repulsion between them grows.
    • The outer electrons end up farther from the nucleus.
    • The attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons is weaker.

Putting It Together: The Net Pull

  • A valence electron feels the pull of the protons, minus the push of the inner electrons that shield it.
    • Net pull ≈ protons − inner electrons
    • Chemists call this the effective nuclear charge.
    • Ex: sodium (2-8-1) has 11 protons and 10 inner electrons, so its valence electron feels a net pull of about +1.
    • Ex: chlorine (2-8-7) has 17 protons and 10 inner electrons, so its valence electrons feel a net pull of about +7.
  • Across a period: the number of inner electrons stays the same while protons are added.
    • The net pull increases, and the electrons are pulled closer.
  • Down a group: the net pull stays about the same, but each new shell puts the valence electrons farther away.
    • The pull on the valence electrons gets weaker.
