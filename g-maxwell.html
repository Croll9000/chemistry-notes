Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

The particles in a gas have a range of kinetic energies. Raising the temperature increases the proportion of particles with high kinetic energy.

The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

At room temperature, the nitrogen molecules in the air move at an average speed of about 500 m/s, yet at any instant some are moving much more slowly and some move faster than 1,000 m/s. A thermometer reports only the average. In this section, we will discuss the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, which shows how the kinetic energies of the particles in a sample are spread out.

Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution Curve

A glass of water at room temperature slowly evaporates even though it is far below its boiling point. Only the molecules at the surface with the most kinetic energy can break away from their neighbors, and at every moment a few molecules have far more energy than the average.

  • Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample. However, not all particles in a sample have the same amount of energy.
    • Some particles have more than the average kinetic energy, and some particles have less than the average.
  • Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution Curve – a graph showing the number of particles possessing different amounts of energy in a sample.
    • The x-axis represents kinetic energy, and the y-axis represents the number of particles.
    • Not all particles have the same kinetic energy, and the particles are not evenly distributed across every kinetic energy.
    • There are no particles with zero kinetic energy.
  • When the temperature is increased, the total number of particles does not change, but the proportion of particles at higher kinetic energies increases.
