Part1

Same Element, Different Mass

The number of protons decides the element. The number of neutrons can be different, and that changes only the mass.

Isotopes

While all atoms of the same element must possess the same number of protons, they do not all need to have the same number of neutrons.

  • Isotopes – atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
    • Same atomic number, different mass numbers
  • Isotopes are named by their mass number.
    • Ex: helium‑3 and helium‑4, or He‑3 and He‑4
  • Most elements found in nature are a mixture of two or more isotopes.
Both atoms shown have the same number of protons, 2, which makes them both Helium. However, they have different masses, meaning they have different numbers of neutrons. We call them isotopes of each other.

Isotopes of Carbon

Carbon-14 is found in very small amounts in all living things. Since it slowly breaks down over time at a known rate, scientists can measure the carbon-14 left in bones, wood or cloth to determine their age. This technique, known as radiocarbon dating, works because isotopes of the same element behave almost identically in chemical reactions.

  • Isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons (in neutral atoms).
    • They have the same chemical properties.
    • Ex: carbon‑12 and carbon‑14 both form CO2.
  • The extra neutrons change only the mass of the atom.
  • Some isotopes are stable.
  • Some isotopes are radioactive.
    • Their nucleus is unstable and breaks down over time, giving off radiation.
    • Ex: carbon‑14 is radioactive and is used to find the age of once-living things.
