Same Element, Different Mass

Isotopes

Atoms of the same element always have the same number of protons, but they do not always have the same number of neutrons. Atoms like these are isotopes of each other. Since the number of protons is the same, isotopes have the same atomic number. Since the number of neutrons is different, they have different mass numbers.

Isotopes – atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

We name isotopes by their mass number, such as helium-3 and helium-4, or He-3 and He-4.

For instance, hydrogen has more than one isotope. In ordinary water, almost every hydrogen nucleus is a single proton. In “heavy water,” every hydrogen nucleus also contains a neutron, which doubles the mass of each hydrogen atom and makes the whole molecule about 10% heavier. Heavy water looks and behaves almost exactly like ordinary water, and it is still H2O. However, an ice cube made from heavy water sinks to the bottom of a glass of ordinary water, while an ordinary ice cube floats.

Most elements found in nature are a mixture of two or more isotopes, almost always in the same proportions. Hydrogen, for instance, is about 99.98% hydrogen-1, with a tiny amount of hydrogen-2. For this reason, the atomic masses on the periodic table are averages rather than whole numbers.

While all atoms of the same element must possess the same number of protons, they do not all need to have the same number of neutrons.

  • Isotopes are named by their mass number.

Isotopes of Carbon

Carbon has three naturally occurring isotopes: carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14. All three have 6 protons, and in neutral atoms all three have 6 electrons. Since an atom’s chemical behavior depends on its electrons, isotopes of an element have nearly identical chemical properties. The extra neutrons mainly change the mass of the atom. A carbon-14 atom is built into a sugar molecule just as easily as a carbon-12 atom.

Isotopes do differ in one important way. Some isotopes are stable, meaning their nuclei stay the same forever. Others are radioactive, meaning their nucleus is unstable and breaks down over time, giving off radiation. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable, while carbon-14 is radioactive.

This difference lets scientists date ancient remains. A living plant or animal keeps taking in fresh carbon-14 from its food and the air. Once it dies, the carbon-14 already in its body slowly breaks down and is not replaced. Half of it is gone after about 5,700 years. Measuring how much carbon-14 is left therefore tells scientists how long ago a once-living thing died, a method called radiocarbon dating.

In 1991, hikers in the Alps found the frozen body of a man sticking out of a melting glacier. The police first thought he might have died in a recent accident. Radiocarbon dating showed that he had died more than 5,000 years ago, making him one of the oldest naturally preserved humans ever found. He is now known as Ötzi the Iceman.

  • Isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons (in neutral atoms).
    • They have the same chemical properties.
    Example
    Carbon‑12 and carbon‑14 both form CO2.
  • The extra neutrons change only the mass of the atom.
  • Some isotopes are stable.
  • Some isotopes are radioactive.
    • Their nucleus is unstable and breaks down over time, giving off radiation.
    • Carbon‑14 is radioactive and is used to find the age of once-living things.
