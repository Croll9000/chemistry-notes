Reaction Rates

Reaction Rate

Kinetics is the branch of chemistry which deals with the rates of chemical reactions. Generally, a rate refers to a change in something per unit of time: rate = changetime. In chemistry, the rate of a chemical reaction refers to how fast or slow the reaction is. A fast rate means the reactants are consumed and the products are produced in a short amount of time. A slow rate means the opposite: the reactants are consumed and the products are produced over a long period of time. An iron nail left outside rusts so slowly that you would need weeks to notice a change, while steel wool, which is also iron, can burn in a shower of sparks in a few seconds.

We can quantify the rate of a reaction by measuring the change in the concentration of a reactant or product over a period of time. Consider the reaction A(aq) + B(aq) → C(aq). Over time, the reactants A and B are consumed while the product C is produced. We can measure the change in concentration of any reactant or product and divide it by the time over which that change occurred to determine the rate of the reaction. Say, for instance, the concentration of A dropped from 6 M to 2 M over the course of 10 seconds. We would calculate the rate of the reaction thusly: rate = 2 M − 6 M10 s = −0.4 M/s, where the negative sign shows that A is being used up. In the lab, chemists measure the rate by timing a change they can observe, such as the volume of gas given off, the mass lost, a color change or the time it takes a solid to disappear. The shorter the time needed to make the same amount of product, the faster the rate.

Manipulating how fast or slow a reaction is, is an essential part of chemistry. Consider a pharmacist preparing a drug for a patient. Sometimes it is necessary for a medication to take effect immediately; other times, it may be desirable for the medication to be released slowly over several hours or days. To achieve the desired effect, a pharmacist has different ways of preparing the same medication. For instance, a drug can be prepared in different concentrations, with higher concentration medications taking effect more rapidly. The same medication may also be delivered in different forms, such as a gel tablet or a caplet. Caplets consist of compressed powders, which take a longer period of time for the body to digest.

  • Kinetics – the study of reaction rates.
  • Reaction rate – the change in the amount of a reactant or product over a period of time.
    • A reaction rate can be measured as how fast a reactant is used up or how fast a product forms.
    • Chemists measure the rate by timing a change they can observe: the volume of gas given off, the mass lost, a color change, or the time for a solid to disappear.
  • The shorter the time needed to make the same amount of product, the faster the rate.

Collision Theory

Collision Theory

Before reactants can turn into products, certain conditions must be met. These conditions are described by a set of principles known as collision theory. Collision theory states that in order for a chemical reaction to take place, reactant particles must collide with one another:

  1. with the proper amount of kinetic energy, and
  2. in the correct orientation.

Since chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of atoms and the formation of new bonds, it makes sense that reactant particles would have to collide with one another. However, if they lack the necessary kinetic energy, the reactant particles will bounce off one another unchanged. Thus they need to meet a certain threshold of kinetic energy for old bonds to be broken and new bonds to be formed. This minimum amount of energy needed to start a chemical reaction is called the activation energy. Additionally, the particles must strike one another with the proper orientation, or alignment: atoms that are bonding to one another must collide head-on, and if they do not, a new substance will not be formed. Collisions that produce a rearrangement of atoms are referred to as effective collisions. Alternatively, collisions that don’t result in the formation of a new bond are called ineffective collisions. The nitrogen and oxygen molecules in the air collide billions of times every second, yet they hardly react at room temperature, because most collisions are not effective. It follows that anything that increases the number of effective collisions per second increases the rate of the reaction.

  • Collision theory – for a chemical reaction to occur, the reactant particles must collide with each other in the proper orientation and with enough kinetic energy.
  • Effective collision – a collision that has the proper orientation and enough energy to break the bonds in the reactants, so that new bonds can form and products are made.
    • Most collisions are not effective. The particles bounce off each other unchanged.
  • Activation energy – the minimum amount of energy needed to start a chemical reaction.
    • A collision with less energy than the activation energy does not produce a reaction, even if the orientation is correct.
  • Anything that increases the number of effective collisions per second increases the rate of the reaction.
