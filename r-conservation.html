Conservation Laws

In every chemical reaction, mass, atoms, energy and charge are conserved: they are rearranged or transferred, but never created or destroyed.

Part1

Conservation of Mass

In the 1770s, Antoine Lavoisier heated tin inside sealed glass vessels and found that the total mass did not change, even though the shiny tin turned into a dull powder. Because the vessel was closed, no gas could enter or escape, so the mass of the reactants equaled the mass of the products. In this section, we will discuss the law of conservation of mass and use it to find unknown masses in reactions.

Law of Conservation of Mass

When wood burns, the ashes weigh much less than the log. The “missing” mass did not disappear: it left as carbon dioxide and water vapor. If every product is captured, the total mass never changes.

  • Law of Conservation of Mass – mass (matter) cannot be created or destroyed.
  • In any chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants must be equal to the total mass of the products.
When combined, the mass of the reactants equals the mass of the products.

Predict Mass in Reactions

Not every quantity in a reaction is easy to measure. The mass of a gas that escapes into the air, for example, cannot be placed on a balance. Because mass is conserved, a chemist who knows every other mass in the reaction can calculate the missing one, which is how scientists track the carbon dioxide released by burning fuels.

  • Since mass can be neither created nor destroyed, we can predict the amount of a substance that is consumed or produced in a chemical reaction.
  • Steps to Calculating a Missing Mass:
    1. Create an algebraic equation by writing the mass of the given reactants and products.
    2. Set the mass of the reactants equal to the mass of the products. Think of the arrow like an “equal sign.”
    3. Let the unknown be x.
    4. Solve for x.

Part2

Conservation of Atoms

When an iron nail rusts, 4 iron atoms combine with 3 oxygen molecules from the air to form 2 formula units of iron(III) oxide, and every one of those atoms is still present in the rust. Mass is conserved because atoms are only rearranged in a reaction, never created or destroyed. In this section, we will count the atoms of each element on both sides of an equation to show that the same atoms are present before and after a reaction.

Conservation of Atoms

Why is mass conserved? The answer is found at the particle level. During a reaction, bonds break and new bonds form, but the atoms themselves are only rearranged. Counting the atoms of each element on both sides of an equation shows that the same atoms are present before and after, which is why the total mass never changes.

  • In any chemical reaction, the total number and type of atoms remains the same before and after a chemical reaction.
    • Although the number of atoms of each element must remain the same, the number of molecules (or moles) does not necessarily need to.
For any chemical reaction, the total number of atoms of each element is the same on the reactants’ and products’ side.
Cause: mass is conserved because atoms are conserved. Every atom of the reactants ends up in the products, and each atom keeps its mass.
Part3

Conservation of Energy

A car engine converts only about 30% of the chemical energy in gasoline into motion; the rest leaves the engine as heat. None of the energy is destroyed: every joule released by burning the fuel can be accounted for as motion or as heat. In this section, we will discuss the law of conservation of energy and how chemical potential energy is converted into other forms.

Law of Conservation of Energy

Every reaction involves an energy change. A burning candle gives off heat and light, and an instant cold pack pulls heat out of your hand. In both cases energy is not created or destroyed: it is converted between chemical potential energy and heat. This idea explains where the energy from fuels and food comes from and where it goes.

  • Law of Conservation of Energy – energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted into different forms.
  • If there is a difference in the potential energy of the reactants and products, that energy will be released or absorbed as light, heat, etc.
Example
When methane burns, the chemical potential energy stored in its bonds is converted into heat and light. The energy given off equals the potential energy lost by the chemicals.

Conservation of Charge

The Daniell cell, an early battery used to power telegraphs in the 1800s, ran on the reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. The 2 electrons lost by each zinc atom are gained by a copper ion, so the total charge is 2+ on both sides of the equation. In this section, we will discuss the law of conservation of charge.

Law of Conservation of Charge

When metals react, electrons move from one atom to another. Batteries and corrosion both depend on this electron transfer. No matter how the electrons move, they are never lost or created, so the total charge before a reaction must equal the total charge after it, just as mass and energy are conserved.

  • Law of Conservation of Charge – charge cannot be created or destroyed.
    • Charge can be transferred from one object or particle to another as electrons are passed back and forth.
    • In any chemical reaction, the total charge of the reactants must equal the total charge of the products.
Note how the total charge is the same for both the products and the reactants. However, electrons were transferred from iron to copper.
