Boyle’s Law

A diver who descends 10 meters below the surface of the ocean feels the pressure double, from 1 atm to about 2 atm, and the air in the diver’s lungs is squeezed into about half its original volume. When the pressure on a gas increases at constant temperature, its particles are pushed closer together. In this section, we will discuss Boyle’s law, the relationship between the pressure and the volume of a gas.

Boyle’s Law

Pushing down the handle of a bicycle pump with its outlet blocked gets harder the farther it goes: the air trapped inside pushes back more and more. In 1662, Robert Boyle measured this effect by trapping air in a J-shaped glass tube and adding mercury, and found that doubling the pressure on the air cut its volume in half.

  • Boyle’s Law – the pressure exerted by a gas is inversely proportional to the volume of the gas when temperature is held constant.
  • If the volume of a gas decreases, the pressure it exerts increases. If the volume of a gas increases, the pressure it exerts decreases.
  • Pressure and volume are inversely proportional: if the volume is multiplied by a factor, the pressure is divided by the same factor, and vice versa. For example, when the volume of the gas doubles, its pressure is cut in half.
    • The product of pressure and volume stays the same: P × V = constant.
  • Explanation using particles: in a smaller volume, the particles travel a shorter distance between collisions, so they hit the walls more often. More collisions each second means a higher pressure.
  • A graph of pressure against volume is a curve that falls steeply and then levels off (an inverse relationship).
P1V1=P2V2
P1 – initial pressure (kPa or atm)V1 – initial volume (L or mL)P2 – final pressure (kPa or atm)V2 – final volume (L or mL)

Solving Boyle’s Law Problems

Breathing is an everyday example of Boyle’s law: when the diaphragm moves down, the volume of the lungs increases by about 0.5 L in a normal breath, the pressure inside falls slightly below the air pressure outside, and air flows in. Every Boyle’s law problem compares the same sample of gas before and after a change in volume or pressure. In this section, we will solve Boyle’s law problems.

Solving Problems

Boyle’s law problems give three of the four values P1, V1, P2 and V2. The temperature is not part of the calculation, because it is held constant. The two pressures must have the same unit, and so must the two volumes.

  • Steps for solving gas law problems:
    1. List the variables and the unknown. Read the question and list every given variable, with its unit, and the unknown.
    2. Convert temperatures to kelvin. If a temperature is given in °C, add 273: K = °C + 273.
    3. Write down Boyle’s Law.
    4. Substitute and solve. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation, then solve for the unknown algebraically.
    5. Write the final answer with units.
  • The two pressures must have the same unit, and so must the two volumes.
