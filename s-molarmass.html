Molar Mass

Significance of a Mole

Nobody can count out 6.02 × 1023 atoms, but anyone can weigh a sample on a balance. The number in a mole was chosen to connect these two ideas. Since 2019, the mole has been defined as exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 particles. This number was chosen to match the mass scale chemists already used, so that one mole of carbon-12 atoms, each with a mass of exactly 12 u, has a mass of 12 g.

Follow the reasoning one more step. A magnesium atom has about twice the mass of a carbon atom, about 24 u. One mole of magnesium atoms is the same number of atoms as one mole of carbon atoms, so it must also have twice the mass, about 24 g. Thus, the mass of 1 mole of any atom or molecule, in grams, is equal to its atomic mass, or the total mass of its atoms.

The samples on a balance can look very different: a few grams of carbon, a larger pile of magnesium, a small puddle of water. Yet if each is one mole, each contains exactly the same number of particles. In this way, every balance in a lab becomes a particle counter.

  • The unit of a mole has significance because it represents the number of atoms in 12 g of C-12. Recall that C-12 has a mass of 12 u.
    • This means that if you possess 1 mole of a substance, the mass, in grams (g), of that substance is equal to its atomic mass in unified atomic mass units (u).
  • The mass of 1 mole of any atom or molecule, in grams, is equal to its atomic mass (or the total mass of its atoms).
Each sample contains the same number of particles, 6.02 × 1023, but the masses are different because the particles have different masses.

Molar Mass

The mass of one mole of a substance is its molar mass, and its unit is grams per mole (g/mol). It does not need to be measured, since it comes straight from the periodic table.

Molar Mass – mass of one mole of a substance.

For an element, we look up its atomic mass on the periodic table, so 1 mole of Mg has a mass of 24.31 g. For a compound, we add up the atomic masses of all of the atoms in the formula, multiplying each atomic mass by the number of atoms of that element.

Consider water. A water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms, each with a mass of 1.01 u, and one oxygen atom with a mass of 16.00 u:

2(1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol

One mole of water therefore has a mass of 18.02 g and fills only about 18 mL, a little more than a tablespoon.

Notice how much the subscripts matter. CO, carbon monoxide, has a molar mass of 28.01 g/mol. CO2, carbon dioxide, has a molar mass of 44.01 g/mol. Molar mass is the bridge between the moles in a balanced equation and the grams on a balance. A reaction that calls for 2.00 moles of sodium chloride needs 2.00 × 58.44 = 116.88 g, an amount anyone can weigh out.

  • Molar Mass – mass of one mole of a substance.
    • Unit – grams/mole (g/mol)
  • Calculating molar mass:
    • Elements: look up the atomic mass of the element on the periodic table. The value of the atomic mass in “u” is equal to the mass of 1 mole in “g.”
      Example
      1 mole of Mg = 24.31 g
    • Compounds: add up the masses from the periodic table of the individual atoms that make up the compound.

Mole–Mass Conversions

Mole–Mass Conversions

A chemist planning a reaction thinks in moles, since the balanced equation is written in moles. The balance in the lab reads in grams. Converting between the two is therefore the most common calculation in any chemistry lab.

The molar mass of a substance is an equivalence between moles and grams, such as 1 mol H2O = 18.02 g H2O, so it can be used as a conversion factor. To convert from moles to mass, we multiply by the molar mass. To convert from mass to moles, we divide by the molar mass.

A teaspoon of table sugar, about 4 g, contains roughly 0.012 mole of sucrose. Since one mole of most substances has a mass of tens or hundreds of grams, the number of moles is usually smaller than the number of grams, which gives a quick way to check an answer. To convert between grams and moles:

  1. Write down the given quantity and the desired unit.
  2. Calculate the molar mass of the substance from the periodic table.
  3. Set up the molar mass as a conversion factor, with the given unit on the bottom.
  4. Multiply or divide, then write the final answer with units.
  • It is often necessary to convert from moles to mass or from mass to moles. To do so we use the molar mass of the substance.
    • To convert from number of moles to mass, multiply by the molar mass of the substance.
    • To convert from mass to number of moles, divide by the molar mass of the substance.
