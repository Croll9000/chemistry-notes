Intermolecular Forces and Properties

Intermolecular Forces and Physical Properties

If you place a drop of water and a drop of rubbing alcohol side by side on a counter, the water beads up into a dome. The alcohol spreads into a thin film and is gone within a minute. Both behaviors come from how strongly the particles attract one another.

The stronger the intermolecular forces in a substance, the more energy it takes to pull its particles apart. Thus, stronger intermolecular forces give a higher melting point, a higher boiling point, a greater surface tension and a greater viscosity, or resistance to flow.

To compare two substances, first find the strongest type of intermolecular force in each: ion–dipole, then hydrogen bonding, then dipole–dipole, then London dispersion. If both have the same type, compare their size, since larger molecules have stronger London dispersion forces.

  • The stronger the intermolecular forces in a substance, the more energy it takes to pull its particles apart.
  • To compare two substances, first find the strongest type of IFA in each (ion–dipole > hydrogen bonding > dipole–dipole > London dispersion). If both have the same type, compare size: larger molecules have stronger London forces.

Melting Point and Boiling Point

To melt a solid or boil a liquid, the particles must gain enough energy to pull away from each other. Only the attractions between molecules are overcome. The covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break, so the steam above a pot is still H2O. Recall that the temperature at which this happens for a solid is its melting point, and for a liquid, its boiling point.

Melting Point – the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It is the same temperature at which the liquid freezes.

Boiling Point – the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid. It is the same temperature at which the gas condenses.

It makes sense, then, that stronger intermolecular forces lead to higher melting and boiling points. Weaker intermolecular forces lead to lower melting and boiling points, and substances with weak attractions are often gases at room temperature.

Weaker attractions also mean a higher vapor pressure. Molecules escape from the surface of the liquid more easily, so the liquid evaporates faster. Nail polish remover (acetone) has no hydrogen bonds between its molecules, so it evaporates from your skin far faster than water does. Doctors take advantage of the same effect when they wipe skin with rubbing alcohol before an injection. The alcohol evaporates within seconds, leaving the skin dry.

  • To melt a solid or boil a liquid, the particles must gain enough energy to pull away from each other. Only the attractions between molecules are overcome; the covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break.
  • Melting Point – the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It is the same temperature at which the liquid freezes.
  • Boiling Point – the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid. It is the same temperature at which the gas condenses.
  • Stronger IFAs → higher melting and boiling points.
  • Weaker IFAs → lower melting and boiling points. These substances are often gases at room temperature.
  • Weaker IFAs also mean a higher vapor pressure: molecules escape from the surface of the liquid more easily, so the liquid evaporates faster.
Example
CH4 (London forces only) boils at −162 °C, HCl (dipole–dipole) at −85 °C, and H2O (hydrogen bonding) at 100 °C.

Surface Tension

A glass can be filled with water slightly above its rim, and the water bulges up without spilling over. Inside a liquid, each molecule is pulled equally in all directions by its neighbors. At the surface, however, the molecules are pulled only sideways and inward, so the surface acts like a stretched skin. This is referred to as surface tension.

Surface tension – the tendency of a liquid’s surface to hold together and resist being broken.

The stronger the intermolecular forces, the higher the surface tension. Water’s hydrogen bonds let it bead up into tall drops and bulge above the rim of a glass. A water strider, an insect common on ponds, walks across the surface without sinking, since its weight is not enough to break through. Liquids with weaker intermolecular forces have lower surface tension, so they spread out into flat puddles.

  • Inside a liquid, each molecule is pulled equally in all directions by its neighbors. At the surface, molecules are pulled only sideways and inward, so the surface acts like a stretched skin.
  • Surface tension – the tendency of a liquid’s surface to hold together and resist being broken.
  • Stronger IFAs → higher surface tension. Water’s hydrogen bonds let it bead up into tall drops and let small insects walk on it.
  • Weaker IFAs → lower surface tension. These liquids spread out into flat puddles.

Viscosity

Honey poured from a jar flows in a slow, thick ribbon. Water splashes out almost instantly. This resistance to flowing is known as viscosity. Viscous liquids, like honey and glycerol, have a high viscosity and flow slowly. Liquids like water and hexane have a low viscosity and flow easily.

Viscosity – a liquid’s resistance to flowing.

For a liquid to flow, its molecules must slide past each other, and strong attractions hold the molecules in place. Thus, the stronger the intermolecular forces, the higher the viscosity. Glycerol has three O–H groups per molecule, so each molecule forms many hydrogen bonds. Hexane molecules attract each other only weakly, so hexane flows easily.

However, the size of the molecule matters too. Very long molecules, like those in motor oil, tangle together and have strong London dispersion forces overall, so oil is more viscous than water even though it is nonpolar. The numbers on a bottle of motor oil, such as 10W-30, describe its viscosity. Engines need an oil thin enough to flow on a cold morning but thick enough to keep coating the moving parts once the engine is hot.

  • Viscosity – a liquid’s resistance to flowing.
    • Viscous liquids (high viscosity) flow slowly.
      Example
      Honey, glycerol
    • Nonviscous liquids (low viscosity) flow easily.
      Example
      Hexane, water
  • For a liquid to flow, its molecules must slide past each other. Strong attractions hold the molecules in place, so the liquid flows slowly.
  • Stronger IFAs → higher viscosity. Glycerol has three O–H groups per molecule, so each molecule forms many hydrogen bonds.
  • Weaker IFAs → lower viscosity. Hexane molecules attract each other only weakly, so hexane flows easily.
  • Molecule size matters too: very long molecules (like those in vegetable oil) tangle together and have strong London forces overall, so oil is more viscous than water even though it is nonpolar.

Solubility: Like Dissolves Like

Salad dressing made of oil and vinegar separates into two layers within minutes of being shaken, no matter how hard it is shaken. Vinegar is mostly water, which is polar, while oil is nonpolar. How much of a substance can dissolve in another is its solubility.

Solubility – how much of a substance (the solute) can dissolve in another substance (the solvent).

A solute dissolves when the solvent’s particles attract the solute’s particles strongly enough to pull them away from each other. When an ionic compound dissolves in water, the δ− oxygen ends of the water molecules attract the positive ions, and the δ+ hydrogen ends attract the negative ions. The water molecules surround each ion and carry it into the solution. A polar solute dissolves in a similar way. Its δ− end attracts the hydrogen ends of water molecules, and its δ+ end attracts the oxygen ends.

Water molecules have little attraction for nonpolar molecules, however, so they cannot pull them apart. The water molecules stay attracted to each other, and the nonpolar substance stays separate. That is why the oil in the dressing floats in its own layer.

This leads to the rule “like dissolves like.” Polar substances and ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents like water. Nonpolar substances dissolve in nonpolar solvents like hexane. Thus, the stronger the attractions between solute and solvent, the more soluble the solute, and weak attractions between solute and solvent mean the solute will not dissolve.

The rule matters for medicines. Vitamins A, D, E and K are nonpolar and dissolve in fat, so they are stored in the body’s fat tissue and can build up to harmful levels if too much is taken. Vitamin C is polar and dissolves in water, so any extra is simply carried out of the body.

  • Solubility – how much of a substance (the solute) can dissolve in another substance (the solvent).
  • A solute dissolves when the solvent’s particles attract the solute’s particles strongly enough to pull them away from each other.
  • “Like dissolves like” – polar substances and ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents like water. Nonpolar substances dissolve in nonpolar solvents like hexane.
    • Ionic compounds in water: the partial charges of water molecules pull the charged ions apart.
      • The δ− oxygen ends attract the positive ions, and the δ+ hydrogen ends attract the negative ions (ion–dipole forces).
      • Water molecules surround each ion and carry it into the solution.
    • Polar molecules in water: the δ− end of a polar solute molecule attracts the δ+ hydrogen ends of water molecules, and the δ+ end of the solute attracts the δ− oxygen ends of water. These attractions pull the solute molecules away from each other and into the solution.
      Example
      Sugar has O–H groups, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds with water molecules and dissolve.
    • Nonpolar molecules in water: polar water molecules have little attraction for nonpolar molecules, so they cannot pull them apart. Water molecules stay attracted to each other (hydrogen bonds), and the nonpolar substance stays separate.
      Example
      Oil and water do not mix; they form layers.
  • Stronger attractions between solute and solvent → more soluble.
  • Weak attractions between solute and solvent → not soluble.
