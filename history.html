History of the Atom

Our understanding of the atom has undergone numerous changes throughout history. The model has been revised as new information has come to light.

Part1

Early Ideas: Atoms as Tiny Spheres

The idea of the atom began with Democritus. Two thousand years later, Dalton turned the idea into a scientific theory.

Democritus

Atoms are far too small to be seen with the naked eye, so our understanding of them was built slowly over more than 2,000 years. Each scientist below proposed a model of the atom based on the evidence available at the time. As new experiments revealed new evidence, these models were revised or replaced.

Democritus (460-370 BC) – Ancient Greek philosopher who proposed that if you kept cutting matter into smaller and smaller pieces eventually you would reach a point at which the pieces could not get any smaller. He called these pieces atomos which means “uncuttable”. The word atom is derived from this.

Democritus believed that all matter was made of small particles which he called atomos. According to his theory these particles were simple and uniform throughout.

John Dalton

John Dalton (1808) – English chemist who developed an atomic theory of elements

  • John Dalton claimed that all elements are composed of tiny particles called atoms
  • All atoms of the same element are identical in mass and properties
  • Compounds are combinations of 2 or more different types of atoms
  • Chemical reactions involve a rearrangement of atoms

Dalton’s model of the atom was very simple. He pictured the atom as a small, uniform, indivisible sphere.

Dalton imagined atoms like tiny marbles. Atoms of different elements had different masses and properties.

Later discoveries changed two of Dalton’s ideas: atoms can be divided (they contain electrons, protons and neutrons), and atoms of the same element can have different masses (isotopes).

Part2

Discovering the Electron

Dalton’s atom could not be divided. Thomson’s cathode ray tube showed that atoms contain even smaller, negatively charged particles.

J.J. Thomson

Joseph John Thomson (1897) – credited with discovering the electron

  • Cathode Ray Tube Experiment – Thomson applied high voltages to metal plates (cathode and anode) made of different elements.
    • When these voltages were applied, particles were emitted.
    • The beams of particles emitted by the cathode plate were known as cathode rays.
  • Findings – He passed these particles between oppositely charged metal plates and saw that the particles were repelled by the negative plate and attracted to the positive plate.
  • Conclusion – Thomson reasoned that atoms contained negatively charged particles, which we now know as electrons.
    • Every metal he tested gave the same results, so he inferred that all atoms have electrons.
    • He knew the atom was neutral overall, so the rest of the atom had to have a positive charge.

J.J. Thomson

Based on his findings, Thomson developed a model of the atom that came to be known as the “Plum Pudding Model”.

  • Plum Pudding Model – the atom possessed regions of negative charge, now called electrons.
    • The atom was known to be neutral overall, so the electrons had to be surrounded by an equal positive charge.
    • Thomson assumed the rest of the atom was a sphere of positive charge.
In the plum pudding model of the atom, there were particles with negative charge called electrons. Surrounding that was a sphere of positive charge.
Part3

Discovering the Nucleus

If the positive charge were spread out, as Thomson proposed, alpha particles would pass straight through gold foil. A few were deflected.

Ernest Rutherford

Ernest Rutherford (1911) – discovered the nucleus of the atom

  • Gold Foil Experiment – Rutherford took a radioactive isotope that emitted particles known as alpha particles.
    • Alpha particles possess a positive charge.
    • The alpha particles were directed at a very thin (about 0.0004 mm, roughly a thousand atoms thick) sheet of gold foil.
    • A detector was placed on the opposite side.
  • Alpha particles – radioactive emissions with a mass of 4 u and a charge of +2.
    • They consist of 2 protons and 2 neutrons.
    • They can be thought of as a helium nucleus.
    • On your Reference Table:
  • Findings
    • Most of the alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil without being deflected.
    • Very few of the particles were deflected at large angles.
    • Even fewer of the particles were backscattered.
      • In the 1909 experiments, about 1 in every 8,000 alpha particles bounced back.
  • Conclusion – Each result told Rutherford something different:
    • Since most of the alpha particles passed through the gold foil, Rutherford concluded that the atom was mostly empty space.
    • The few particles that were deflected at large angles must have been repelled.
      • Alpha particles are positive, so there must be something else positive in the atom.
      • It must be very small, since only a few particles came close enough to be deflected.
      • This small, dense, positively charged region is called the nucleus.
      • The nucleus is where positively charged particles known as protons are found.
    • The very few particles that were backscattered (bounced back) indicated that the nucleus is extremely dense and holds most of the atom’s mass. An alpha particle can only be turned straight back by something much more massive than itself, hit almost head on.
  • The gold foil experiment did not show the shape of the nucleus. It showed that the nucleus is small, positive and dense.

Ernest Rutherford

Based on his findings, Rutherford developed the “Rutherford Model” of the atom.

  • Rutherford Model – the atom was mostly empty space.
    • In the center of the atom was a small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
    • The nucleus contained most of the atom’s mass.
    • Positively charged particles known as protons were found in the nucleus.
    • Electrons were found in the empty space surrounding the nucleus.
Since the atom was mostly empty space, the majority of alpha particles passed through the foil. The few particles that were deflected were repelled by the positively charged nucleus.
Part4

Energy Levels and the Modern Model

Rutherford’s model didn’t explain why atoms give off only certain colors of light. Bohr’s energy levels did, and the modern model refined them.

Niels Bohr

Niels Bohr (1913) – discovered that electrons are only found in specific regions of the atom

  • Findings – Bohr combined the findings of other chemists/physicists like Ernest Rutherford and Max Planck.
    • When exposed to high heat or electrical currents, atoms emitted light of specific wavelengths and energy.
  • Conclusion – Electrons can only possess specific (quantized) amounts of energy.
    • Electrons are only found in specific regions orbiting the nucleus, known as energy levels.
  • Energy levels – regions at fixed distances from the nucleus where electrons are found.
    • Each energy level corresponds to a specific amount of energy.
  • Why this explains the light:
    • An electron gives off light when it drops from a higher level to a lower one.
    • Only certain drops are possible, so only certain amounts of energy (certain colors) are given off.
When energy was added to atoms in the form of heat or electricity, they emitted light of specific colors (energies/wavelengths). This indicated that the electrons were found in only specific regions around the nucleus known as energy levels.
Different elements each emitted their own unique types of light and therefore have their own unique energy levels. Try it in the spectroscope simulation →

Niels Bohr

Niels Bohr developed the “Bohr Model of the Atom”

  • Bohr Model – electrons are found in circular orbits around the nucleus called energy levels.
    • Electrons in each energy level possess a specific amount of energy.
    • Each energy level is given a number starting with the one closest to the nucleus
    • Energy levels have different amounts of energy.
      • Energy levels closer to the nucleus possess a low amount of energy.
      • Energy levels further from the nucleus possess a greater amount of energy.
    • Each energy level can only hold a specific maximum number of electrons.

Electrons closer to the nucleus have less energy. Electrons further from the nucleus have more energy.

Modern Model

Many scientists including Louis de Broglie and Erwin Schrödinger contributed to our modern understanding of the atom.

  • Findings – Electrons have both wave and particle like properties
  • Conclusion – Electrons exist in regions known as orbitals.
    • Orbitals are the most probable location of finding an electron.
    • However, the exact position of an electron can never be pinpointed.
  • Modern Model of the Atom – electrons are both particles and waves.
    • Electrons are found in regions known as orbitals.
    • Their exact location can never be pinpointed.
    • Orbitals – the most probable location of finding an electron.
    • Also known as the “Wave Mechanical Model”, “Quantum Mechanical Model”, or “Schrödinger Model”

In the modern model, an electron’s exact location cannot be known. Locate hydrogen’s electron hundreds of times to see where it is most likely to be found.
