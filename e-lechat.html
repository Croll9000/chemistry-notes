Part1

Le Châtelier’s Principle

Climbers who arrive at a high-altitude base camp get out of breath because each breath holds less oxygen. In the blood, hemoglobin (Hb) and oxygen are in equilibrium: Hb + O2 ⇌ HbO2. With less oxygen, this equilibrium shifts to the left, so less oxygen is carried to the body. Over a few weeks the body makes more hemoglobin, shifting the equilibrium back to the right. In this section, we will discuss how a system at equilibrium responds when its conditions change.

Le Châtelier’s Principle

A change in the conditions of a system at equilibrium is called a stress.

  • Stress – a change in the concentration, temperature or pressure of a system at equilibrium.
  • Le Châtelier’s principle – when a stress is applied to a system at equilibrium, the system shifts in the direction that relieves the stress, until a new equilibrium is reached.
  • A shift to the right means the forward reaction speeds up, making more products. A shift to the left means the reverse reaction speeds up, making more reactants.
  • Think of a see-saw: the stress unbalances it, and the shift brings it back into balance.
Part2

Increasing a Concentration

Chemical plants that make ammonia keep pumping in extra nitrogen and hydrogen to make as much product as possible. In this section, we will discuss what happens when the concentration of a reactant or a product is increased.

Increasing a Concentration

The system uses up part of whatever was added.

  • Adding a reactant shifts the equilibrium to the right, toward the products.
  • Adding a product shifts the equilibrium to the left, toward the reactants.
  • The shift uses up only part of the added substance, so its concentration at the new equilibrium is still higher than before.
  • Adding a solid or a pure liquid does not cause a shift, because its concentration cannot change.

Part3

Decreasing a Concentration

Ammonia plants cool the gas mixture so the ammonia condenses into a liquid and can be drained away. Removing the product keeps the reaction making more. In this section, we will discuss what happens when the concentration of a reactant or product is decreased.

Decreasing a Concentration

The system replaces part of whatever was removed.

  • Removing a reactant shifts the equilibrium to the left, to make more of that reactant.
  • Removing a product shifts the equilibrium to the right, to make more of that product.
  • The shift replaces only part of what was removed.

Part4

Changing the Temperature

Sealed glass tubes of nitrogen dioxide are pale at low temperature and dark reddish-brown when hot. The color shows which way the equilibrium has shifted. In this section, we will discuss how a change in temperature shifts an equilibrium.

Changing the Temperature

Treat heat as if it were a reactant or a product.

  • Look at which side of the equation the heat (energy) term is on.
  • Raising the temperature adds heat, so the equilibrium shifts away from the heat term.
  • Lowering the temperature removes heat, so the equilibrium shifts toward the heat term.
  • Example: 2NO2(g) ⇌ N2O4(g) + heat. Raising the temperature shifts it to the left, making more brown NO2.

Part5

Changing the Pressure

Engineers run the reaction that makes ammonia at about 200 times normal air pressure. Four moles of gas react to make only two moles of gas, so squeezing the gases helps. In this section, we will discuss how pressure shifts an equilibrium involving gases.

Changing the Pressure

Pressure matters only for gases, and only when the two sides have different numbers of moles of gas.

  • Count the moles of gas on each side using the coefficients. Solids, liquids and dissolved substances do not count.
  • Increasing the pressure (decreasing the volume) shifts the equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas.
  • Decreasing the pressure (increasing the volume) shifts the equilibrium toward the side with more moles of gas.
  • If both sides have the same number of moles of gas, a change in pressure causes no shift. Example: H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g).

Part6

Adding a Catalyst

A catalyst lets an ammonia plant reach equilibrium in seconds instead of years, but it does not change how much ammonia is in the equilibrium mixture. In this section, we will discuss why a catalyst does not shift an equilibrium.

Adding a Catalyst

A catalyst lowers the activation energy for the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount.

  • A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so it does not shift the equilibrium.
  • A catalyst only makes the system reach equilibrium faster.

Part7

The Common Ion Effect

When a solution of lead(II) nitrate is added to a saturated solution of lead(II) iodide, a cloud of bright yellow solid appears. The added lead ions pushed the equilibrium back toward the solid. In this section, we will discuss the common ion effect.

The Common Ion Effect

A common ion is an ion that is already part of the equilibrium.

  • Common ion effect – the decrease in the solubility of an ionic compound when a solution containing one of its ions is added.
  • For PbI2(s) ⇌ Pb2+(aq) + 2I−(aq), adding Pb(NO3)2 adds the common ion Pb2+.
    • The equilibrium shifts to the left: more solid PbI2 forms, and the concentration of I− decreases.
  • An ion that is not part of the equilibrium (a spectator ion, such as NO3− here) does not cause a shift.

Engineering with Equilibrium

Chemical engineers use Le Châtelier’s principle to design processes that make more product.

  • Criteria – the goals a design must meet, such as how much product it makes and how much it costs.
  • Constraints – the limits a design must stay within, such as the safe pressure of the equipment or a budget.
  • The conditions that give the most product at equilibrium are not always the best choice. A low temperature favors ammonia at equilibrium, but the reaction becomes too slow; a very high pressure gives more ammonia, but costs more and is less safe.
  • Engineers balance these trade-offs: the Haber process runs at about 400–450 °C and 150–250 atm with an iron catalyst, and removes the ammonia as it forms so that the equilibrium keeps shifting to the right.
