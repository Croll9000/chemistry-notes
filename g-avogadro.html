Avogadro’s Law

Avogadro’s Law

The combined gas law assumes that the amount of gas stays the same. In many chemical reactions, however, gases are produced or used up. When two molecules of octane burn in a car engine, for example, 25 molecules of oxygen gas become 16 molecules of carbon dioxide and 18 molecules of water vapor, so the amount of gas changes during the reaction.

A party balloon filled with 0.50 mol of helium at room temperature and normal air pressure holds about 12 L of gas. With 1.00 mol of helium, the same balloon swells to about 24 L. At constant temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas depends on how many particles it contains. Recall that this is Avogadro’s law. Increasing the number of particles increases the volume, and decreasing the number of particles decreases it. As a result:

Vn = constant

A graph of volume vs. moles of gas is a straight line with a positive slope that passes through the origin.

Avogadro’s Law – the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the number of moles of gas particles in a sample when temperature and pressure are held constant.

The same law explains why equal volumes of different gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of particles, and why one mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L at STP.

  • The combined gas law works under the assumption that the number of gas particles in a sample remains constant. However, during many chemical reactions, gases can be produced or used up in addition to changes in temperature, pressure and volume.
  • The volume of a gas varies directly with the moles of gas particles in a sample, assuming temperature and pressure are held constant.
    • Increasing the number of gas particles in a sample will increase the volume.
    • Decreasing the number of gas particles in a sample will decrease the volume.
  • Avogadro’s Law – the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the number of moles of gas particles in a sample when temperature and pressure are held constant.
V1n1=V2n2
V1 – initial volume (m3 or L)n1 – initial number of moles (mol)V2 – final volume (m3 or L)n2 – final number of moles (mol)
Example
Consider blowing into a balloon. When the amount of gas particles in the balloon increases, its volume increases. When the balloon is deflated, the amount of gas particles decreases, and therefore the volume decreases.
  • A graph of volume against moles of gas is a straight line with a positive slope that passes through the origin (a direct relationship).
  • Avogadro’s law also explains why equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of particles, and why 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L at STP.

Solving Avogadro’s Law Problems

Solving Problems

Avogadro’s law problems give three of the four values V1, n1, V2 and n2, and ask for the fourth. The temperature and pressure are held constant, so they are not part of the calculation, but the two volumes must have the same unit.

An adult’s lungs hold about 6 L of air when completely full, roughly 0.24 mol of gas at body temperature and normal air pressure. Since the temperature and the pressure inside the lungs stay nearly constant, the volume of air breathed in is proportional to the number of moles of air. To solve an Avogadro’s law problem:

  1. List the variables and the unknown. Read the question and list every given variable, with its unit, and the unknown.
  2. If necessary, convert the units. The two volumes must have the same unit.
  3. Write down Avogadro’s law.
  4. Substitute and solve. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation, then solve for the unknown algebraically.
  5. Write the final answer with units.
