Describing a Gas

A typical aluminum scuba tank holds about 2,200 L of air, measured at sea-level pressure, packed into an aluminum cylinder with an inside volume of only 11 L. The same air can occupy such different volumes because the volume of a gas depends on its pressure, its temperature and how much gas there is. In this section, we will discuss the four variables used to describe a sample of gas.

Properties of Gases

Unlike a solid or a liquid, a gas has no surface of its own. Open a bottle of perfume in one corner of a room and the scent soon reaches every corner, because the gas spreads out to fill whatever space is available. Gas particles are far apart and move quickly, which is why gases can also be squeezed into much smaller spaces.

  • Gases have an indefinite shape and an indefinite volume: a gas takes the shape and the volume of its container.
  • Gas particles are spaced very far apart, so gases can be compressed (squeezed into a smaller volume) and gases expand to fill any container.
  • Gas particles move quickly in straight lines until they collide with one another or with the walls of their container.
  • Gases mix completely with other gases. Air is a mixture of mostly nitrogen and oxygen.

The Four Gas Variables

A tire pressure gauge, a thermometer, the size of a container and a balance each give part of the description of a gas. Change any one of these quantities and at least one of the others changes too, so all four must be stated to describe a sample of gas completely.

  • The behavior of any gas can be defined by different state variables.
    • State Variables – quantities that describe the condition and behavior of a system.
  • Gases have four different state variables: pressure, volume, temperature and amount.
    • Pressure (P) – the amount of force per unit area acting on a surface.
      • Units – pascals (Pa), kilopascals (kPa), atmospheres (atm) and millimeters of mercury (mmHg)
      • Measuring device – pressure gauge or barometer
    • Volume (V) – the amount of space an object takes up. A gas fills its container, so the volume of a gas is the volume of its container.
      • Units – milliliters (mL), liters (L) and cubic meters (m3)
      • Measuring device – a graduated syringe, a scale on a cylinder, or the known size of the container
    • Temperature (T) – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
      • Unit – gas calculations always use kelvin (K): K = °C + 273
      • Measuring device – thermometer
    • Amount of Particles (n) – the quantity of gas particles present.
      • Unit – moles (mol)
      • Measuring device – no instrument measures moles directly. The mass of the gas is measured with a balance and then converted to moles using its molar mass.

Standard Temperature and Pressure

The volume of a balloon filled with 1.00 mol of helium is about 22.4 L on a 0 °C day at sea level, but about 24.5 L in a 25 °C room. Because the volume of a gas changes with conditions, chemists compare gases at one agreed-upon set of conditions. In this section, we will discuss standard temperature and pressure.

Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP)

A measured volume of gas means little unless the temperature and pressure are given with it. To compare measurements made in different laboratories, chemists report gas volumes at standard temperature and pressure.

  • STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure) – the standard conditions for comparing gases: 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm (101.3 kPa).
  • At STP, 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L. This volume is called the molar volume of a gas (see Unit 6: Molar Volume).
Example
A problem states that a gas is “at STP.” This gives two values: T = 273 K and P = 101.3 kPa (1 atm).
