Describing a Gas

Properties of Gases

In a gas, the particles are spaced very far apart compared with their own size, and they move quickly in straight lines until they collide with one another or with the walls of their container. Since the particles are so far apart, a gas has no fixed shape and no fixed volume of its own. It spreads out to fill whatever container it is placed in, taking both the shape and the volume of that container.

The same empty space between the particles allows a gas to be compressed into a much smaller volume. For instance, a tank of helium no larger than a fire extinguisher can fill dozens of party balloons, each one far bigger than the tank itself. No solid or liquid could be squeezed into a container this way.

For the same reason, gases mix completely with one another. The air that we breathe is a mixture of mostly nitrogen and oxygen, spread evenly through the room, and the smell of perfume opened in one corner of a room soon reaches every other corner.

  • Gases have an indefinite shape and an indefinite volume: a gas takes the shape and the volume of its container.
  • Gas particles are spaced very far apart, so gases can be compressed (squeezed into a smaller volume) and gases expand to fill any container.
  • Gas particles move quickly in straight lines until they collide with one another or with the walls of their container.
  • Gases mix completely with other gases. Air is a mixture of mostly nitrogen and oxygen.

The Four Gas Variables

Any sample of gas can be described completely with four quantities: its pressure, its volume, its temperature and the amount of gas present. We call these the state variables of a gas. They are linked to one another, so if any one of them changes, at least one of the others changes as well. For this reason, a gas volume means very little unless the conditions it was measured under are stated with it.

State Variables – quantities that describe the condition and behavior of a system.

The pressure (P) is measured in kilopascals (kPa), atmospheres (atm) or millimeters of mercury (mmHg), using a pressure gauge or a barometer.

Pressure (P) – the amount of force per unit area acting on a surface.

The volume (V) of a gas is simply the volume of its container, measured in milliliters, liters or cubic meters.

Volume (V) – the amount of space an object takes up. A gas fills its container, so the volume of a gas is the volume of its container.

The temperature (T) is read with a thermometer, but gas calculations always use the Kelvin scale (K = °C + 273).

Temperature (T) – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.

The amount of particles (n) is the number of moles of gas present. No instrument measures moles directly, so the mass of the gas is measured on a balance and converted to moles using its molar mass.

Amount of Particles (n) – the quantity of gas particles present.

  • The behavior of any gas can be defined by different state variables.
    • State Variables – quantities that describe the condition and behavior of a system.
  • Gases have four different state variables: pressure, volume, temperature and amount.
    • Pressure (P) – the amount of force per unit area acting on a surface.
      • Units – pascals (Pa), kilopascals (kPa), atmospheres (atm) and millimeters of mercury (mmHg)
      • Measuring device – pressure gauge or barometer
    • Volume (V) – the amount of space an object takes up. A gas fills its container, so the volume of a gas is the volume of its container.
      • Units – milliliters (mL), liters (L) and cubic meters (m3)
      • Measuring device – a graduated syringe, a scale on a cylinder, or the known size of the container
    • Temperature (T) – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
      • Unit – gas calculations always use kelvin (K): K = °C + 273
      • Measuring device – thermometer
    • Amount of Particles (n) – the quantity of gas particles present.
      • Unit – moles (mol)
      • Measuring device – no instrument measures moles directly. The mass of the gas is measured with a balance and then converted to moles using its molar mass.

Standard Temperature and Pressure

Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP)

A balloon filled with 1.00 mol of helium has a volume of about 22.4 L on a 0 °C day at sea level, but about 24.5 L in a 25 °C room. The amount of helium is the same in both cases, and only the conditions have changed. To compare the volumes of gases measured in different laboratories, chemists report them at one agreed-upon set of conditions, called STP (standard temperature and pressure).

STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure) – the standard conditions for comparing gases: 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm (101.3 kPa).

At STP, one mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L, its molar volume. These conditions were chosen because they are easy to reproduce in any laboratory in the world. A mixture of ice and water sits at 0 °C, and the air at sea level pushes with about 1 atm. With the gas laws, we can convert a volume measured at any other temperature or pressure to STP, and then compare it fairly with any other measurement.

  • STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure) – the standard conditions for comparing gases: 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm (101.3 kPa).
  • At STP, 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L. This volume is called the molar volume of a gas.
Example
A problem states that a gas is “at STP.” This gives two values: T = 273 K and P = 101.3 kPa (1 atm).
