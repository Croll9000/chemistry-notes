Physical & Chemical Changes

A physical change does not change the identity of a substance. A chemical change produces new substances.

Part1

Physical Changes and Chemical Changes

Identifying Physical and Chemical Changes

A horseshoe left outside for years slowly turns from shiny gray to crumbly reddish-brown. An ice sculpture left outside on a warm day slowly melts into a puddle. Both objects change a great deal, but in very different ways.

The puddle is still water. If it is put back in the freezer, it becomes ice again. Melting is a physical change, since the identity of the substance does not change and nothing new is created. Freezing, boiling, cutting and dissolving are also physical changes.

Physical Change – a change in which the identity of the substance does not change.

The rust on the horseshoe is a different story. It is no longer iron at all. It formed when the iron combined with oxygen and water from the air, and it has its own color, its own texture and none of the strength of iron. Rusting is a chemical change, since new substances are made. A chemical change is also called a chemical reaction. Burning wood, cooking an egg and digesting food are all chemical changes.

Chemical Change – a change in which new substances are made with new properties.

Be careful not to judge a change by how big it looks. Dynamite going off and an iron nail slowly rusting are both chemical changes. A glacier carving a valley over thousands of years is only a physical one. The question to ask is always the same: was a new substance made? If the answer is “no,” a physical change took place. If the answer is “yes,” a chemical change took place.

  • Physical Change – a change in which the identity of the substance does not change.
    • Nothing new is created.
    Example
    Melting, boiling, dissolving, cutting, crushing
  • Chemical Change – a change in which new substances are made with new properties.
    • Also called a chemical reaction.
    Example
    Burning, rusting, cooking an egg
  • If the answer is “no”, a physical change took place
  • If the answer is “yes”, a chemical change took place

Example: Melting a Metal

Solid copper and liquid (molten) copper look very different, but both are made of the same copper (Cu) atoms. In the solid the atoms are packed in neat rows; in the liquid they are still close together but move around and slide past each other. The chemical identity did not change, so a physical change took place. The same is true when an ice cube melts: it is H2O before and after. In fact, all phase changes (melting, boiling, etc.) are physical changes.

Example: Burning Steel Wool

Steel wool consists mostly of iron (Fe) atoms. When it is burned, it combines with oxygen in the air to form a new compound, iron(III) oxide (Fe2O3). Since the chemical identity of the substance changed, a chemical change took place. If the chemical formula of a substance is altered, a chemical change must have taken place.
Part2

Chemical Reactions

Reactants and Products

In a chemical reaction, the bonds holding substances together are broken, and their atoms rearrange to form new substances. Nothing is added from outside and nothing is lost. The same atoms simply end up with new partners.

Chemical Reaction – a process in which the bonds of substances are broken and the atoms rearrange to form new substances.

Chemists write reactions as equations, read from left to right. The arrow means “yields” or “produces.” Consider the reaction between hydrogen and oxygen:

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

This means that two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule react to produce two water molecules. The substances on the left are the reactants, what we start with. The substance on the right is the product, what we end with.

Reactants – the starting substances in a chemical reaction, written on the left side of the arrow.

Products – the ending substances in a chemical reaction, written on the right side of the arrow.

Count the atoms on each side and you will find 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms on the left, and 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms on the right. In a reaction, atoms are rearranged. They are not created or destroyed.

This particular reaction once launched astronauts into space. The main engines of NASA’s Space Shuttle burned liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, and the only thing that came out of them was extremely hot steam. More than a million liters of fuel were used in each launch, yet every atom that went into the engines came out in a molecule of water.

Reactions also involve energy. Recall that energy must be added to break the bonds in the reactants, and energy is released when the bonds in the products form. The shuttle’s hydrogen and oxygen sat together in the engine without reacting until igniters supplied the energy to break the first bonds. After that, the energy released by forming water kept the reaction going.

  • Chemical Reaction – a process in which the bonds of substances are broken and the atoms rearrange to form new substances.
  • We can write reactions as equations.
    • Read them from left to right.
    • The arrow means “yields” or “produces”.
    • Reactants – the starting substances in a chemical reaction, written on the left side of the arrow.
    • Products – the ending substances in a chemical reaction, written on the right side of the arrow.
  • In a reaction, atoms are rearranged.
    • They are not created or destroyed.
    • The same atoms are on both sides of the equation.
  • Reactions involve energy changes.
    • To break the bonds in the reactants, energy must be added.
    • When the new bonds in the products form, energy is released.
    • That is why many reactions need a spark or heat to get started.

Evidence of a Chemical Change

Chemists cannot watch atoms rearrange. Instead, they rely on clues that a new substance has formed, much as a detective relies on evidence at a crime scene. Common evidence of a chemical change includes:

  • a gas being produced
  • an unexpected color change
  • a solid forming when two liquids are mixed
  • a temperature change
  • the release of light
  • a new odor

Each of these clues points to something new being made. When the iron in a steel wool pad is held in a flame, it sparkles and turns from silver to a dark, brittle black. When a glow stick is bent, two chemicals inside mix and begin to give off light. When milk sours, it develops a new smell.

However, none of these clues is proof on its own. Bubbles also form when water boils, and that is a physical change, since the bubbles are still water. A metal rod glows red when it is heated, but it is still the same metal when it cools. Each time, we return to the same question: was a new substance made?

We can’t see atoms rearranging, but these clues suggest a new substance has formed:

EvidenceExample
A gas is produced (bubbles) that wasn’t there beforeBaking soda and vinegar fizzing
An unexpected color changeIron turning to reddish-brown rust
Heat or light is given offWood burning
A solid forms when two liquids are mixedTwo clear solutions making a cloudy solid
A new odorMilk going sour
