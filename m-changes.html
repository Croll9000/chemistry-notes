Physical & Chemical Changes

A physical change does not change the identity of a substance. A chemical change produces new substances.

Physical Changes and Chemical Changes

The key question: did the chemical identity of the substance change?

Identifying Physical and Chemical Changes

Matter is constantly changing. Puddles evaporate after a storm, leaves change color in the fall and iron railings slowly rust. Some of these changes produce entirely new substances while others do not, and chemists sort changes into two categories based on this difference.

  • Physical Change – a change in which the identity of the substance does not change.
    • Nothing new is created.
    • Ex: melting, boiling, dissolving, cutting, crushing
  • Chemical Change – a change in which new substances are made with new properties.
    • Also called a chemical reaction.
    • Ex: burning, rusting, cooking an egg

In order to determine which type of change took place, ask yourself the question, “Did the chemical identity of the substance change?”

  • If the answer is “no”, a physical change took place
  • If the answer is “yes”, a chemical change took place

Example: Melting a Metal

Solid copper and liquid (molten) copper look very different, but both are made of the same copper (Cu) atoms. In the solid the atoms are packed in neat rows; in the liquid they are still close together but move around and slide past each other. The chemical identity did not change, so a physical change took place. The same is true when an ice cube melts: it is H2O before and after. In fact, all phase changes (melting, boiling, etc.) are physical changes.

Example: Burning Steel Wool

Steel wool consists mostly of iron (Fe) atoms. When it is burned, it combines with oxygen in the air to form a new compound, iron(III) oxide (Fe2O3). Since the chemical identity of the substance changed, a chemical change took place. If the chemical formula of a substance is altered, a chemical change must have taken place.
Part2

Chemical Reactions

In a chemical reaction, the atoms of the starting substances rearrange into new substances.

Reactants and Products

Chemical reactions take place all around us: in a car’s engine, inside a battery and in the cells of our bodies. Like a recipe, every reaction begins with certain ingredients and ends with something new. Chemists use equations to describe what goes in and what comes out.

  • Chemical Reaction – a process in which the bonds of substances are broken and the atoms rearrange to form new substances.
  • We can write reactions as equations.
    • Read them from left to right.
    • The arrow means “yields” or “produces”.
    • Reactants – the substances on the left side of the arrow: the things you start with.
    • Products – the substances on the right side of the arrow: the things you end with.
  • In a reaction, atoms are rearranged.
    • They are not created or destroyed.
    • The same atoms are on both sides of the equation.
  • Reactions involve energy changes.
    • To break the bonds in the reactants, energy must be added.
    • When the new bonds in the products form, energy is released.
    • That is why many reactions need a spark or heat to get started.

Evidence of a Chemical Change

We can’t see atoms rearranging, but these clues suggest a new substance has formed:

EvidenceExample
A gas is produced (bubbles) that wasn’t there beforeBaking soda and vinegar fizzing
An unexpected color changeIron turning to reddish-brown rust
Heat or light is given offWood burning
A solid forms when two liquids are mixedTwo clear solutions making a cloudy solid
A new odorMilk going sour

Careful: bubbles also form when water boils, and that is a physical change (the bubbles are still H2O). Always come back to the question: is there a new substance?
