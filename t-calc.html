Heat Calculations

Before the season starts, the crew at a hockey arena floods the concrete floor with water and runs a refrigeration system for several days to freeze it. How much heat has to be removed? The water from the hose starts at about 15 °C. It must first be cooled to 0 °C, then frozen, and then the ice must be cooled a few more degrees below 0 °C so that it stays hard under the skates. Each of these steps is a different segment of a cooling curve, and each needs its own calculation.

For a typical rink, the sheet of ice has a mass of about 30,000 kg. Cooling the water to 0 °C requires removing about 1.9 billion joules. Cooling the finished ice to −5 °C requires about 0.3 billion joules more. Freezing the water, however, requires removing about 10 billion joules. The phase change, where the temperature does not change at all, accounts for most of the work.

For this reason, a heat problem must always be broken into the segments of a heating or cooling curve, and two different kinds of equations are needed. On a sloped segment, the temperature changes, and the heat goes into the kinetic energy of the particles. The amount of heat depends on how much the temperature changes, so we calculate it with an equation that includes ΔT: q = mcΔT.

On a flat segment at the melting point, the substance melts or freezes. On a flat segment at the boiling point, it vaporizes or condenses. On these segments the temperature does not change, so an equation built on ΔT would give zero, even though a great deal of heat is involved. Instead, the heat depends only on how much substance changes phase, and we calculate it with q = mHf or q = mHv. Choosing the right equation always comes back to one question: is the temperature changing, or is the phase changing?

  • We use several different equations to calculate the heat added to or removed from a substance. Which one to use depends on what is happening on the heating curve:
    • Sloped segment (temperature change): q = mcΔT.
    • Flat segment at the melting point (melting or freezing): q = mHf, on the Heat During Phase Changes page.
    • Flat segment at the boiling point (vaporizing or condensing): q = mHv.

Heat During Temperature Changes

Heat During Temperature Changes

When the temperature of a substance changes, the kinetic energy of its particles changes, and we calculate the heat absorbed or released with q = mcΔT.

If we look at each part of the equation, it becomes clear why it is built this way. The heat is directly proportional to the mass, since twice as much water contains twice as many molecules to speed up, so it needs twice the heat. The heat is also directly proportional to ΔT, since raising the temperature by 20 °C takes twice the heat of raising it by 10 °C. The specific heat capacity, c, accounts for the identity of the substance, since not all substances change their temperature at the same rate. Heating half a liter of water from 20 °C to 100 °C for tea takes about 170,000 J. Heating the same mass of iron through the same temperature change takes only about 18,000 J.

The units confirm that the equation is put together correctly. Grams × J/(g·K) × K leaves only joules.

The sign of q tells us the direction of heat flow. ΔT is always calculated as the final temperature minus the initial temperature, Tf − Ti. If the temperature goes up, ΔT and q are positive, meaning heat was absorbed. If the temperature goes down, ΔT and q are negative, meaning heat was released. Since a change of 1 °C is the same size as a change of 1 K, ΔT can be calculated with Celsius temperatures, and there is no need to convert to kelvins.

  • When the temperature of a substance changes, the kinetic energy of its particles changes. The heat added to (or removed from) a substance during these intervals is proportional to its change in temperature.
  • Not all substances change their temperature at the same rate. The same amount of heat raises the temperature of some substances much more than others.
  • If the temperature goes up, ΔT and q are positive (heat absorbed). If the temperature goes down, ΔT and q are negative (heat released).

Specific Heat Capacity

At the beach on a sunny afternoon, the sand can be too hot to walk on barefoot while the water is still cool enough to be refreshing. Both have been in the same sunshine for the same amount of time, so they have absorbed similar amounts of energy per gram. The difference is that sand needs far less energy than water to warm by one degree. This property is known as the specific heat capacity (c), and it is measured in joules per gram-kelvin (J/g·K).

Specific Heat Capacity (c) – the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K).

The definition leads directly to the equation for heat. If c is the heat needed per gram per degree, then the heat for m grams and ΔT degrees is c multiplied by m and by ΔT, so q = mcΔT. Rearranged, c = q/(mΔT), which is how specific heat capacities are measured.

Specific heat capacity also tells us how a substance responds to heat. The higher the specific heat capacity, the smaller the temperature change for the same amount of heat. If 100 J is added to 1 g of water (c = 4.18 J/g·K), its temperature rises by about 24 °C. The same 100 J added to 1 g of aluminum (c = 0.897 J/g·K) raises its temperature by about 111 °C.

Water’s specific heat capacity is one of the highest of any common substance. Some of the energy added to water goes into stretching and loosening the hydrogen bonds between its molecules rather than speeding them up, so more energy is needed for each degree of temperature change. This is what keeps the ocean cool on a hot afternoon, and it also shapes the weather on Long Island. Through the summer, the ocean slowly absorbs an enormous amount of heat while barely warming. Through the fall and winter, it slowly releases that heat while barely cooling. As a result, coastal towns have cooler summers and milder winters than towns far inland at the same latitude.

  • How much the temperature of a substance changes when heat is added depends on the intermolecular forces in the substance, the complexity of its particles and its mass. We describe this with a value called specific heat capacity.
    • Specific Heat Capacity (c) – the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K).
    • Unit – joules per gram-kelvin (J/g·K)
  • The specific heat capacity of water (4.18 J/g·K) is much higher than that of aluminum (0.897 J/g·K). If the same amount of heat is added to 1 gram of each, the temperature of the aluminum rises more than that of the water.
  • The higher the specific heat capacity, the smaller the temperature change for the same amount of heat.

Same Heat, Different Substances

Thermal Conductivity vs. Specific Heat Capacity

Thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity are easy to confuse, since both describe how a material behaves when it is heated. However, they answer different questions. Thermal conductivity describes how quickly heat moves through a material. Specific heat capacity describes how much heat is needed to change the temperature of a material.

A material can be high in one and low in the other. Copper has a very high thermal conductivity, so heat from a burner spreads through it quickly. It also has a low specific heat capacity, so only a little heat raises its temperature. Together, these make a copper-bottomed pan heat up within seconds and cool down just as fast. Water is the reverse. It has a low thermal conductivity, so heat moves through it slowly, but a high specific heat capacity, so it takes a great deal of heat to warm it.

In short, thermal conductivity is about the rate of heat transfer. Specific heat capacity is about the amount of heat.

  • Thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity are related, but they measure different properties:
    • Thermal conductivity describes how quickly an object transfers heat (into and out of itself).
    • Specific heat capacity describes how much heat is needed to change the temperature of an object.
  • Copper has a high thermal conductivity (heat moves through it fast) and a low specific heat capacity (a little heat raises its temperature a lot).

Solving Temperature-Change Problems

Solving q = mcΔT Problems

A heat problem might give the mass of a sample, its specific heat capacity and its starting and ending temperatures, then ask for the heat absorbed. Other problems give the heat and ask for the mass or the temperature change instead. In every case, the same equation is used, and the same steps keep the work organized. Listing every variable with its unit before substituting is especially important. It shows you which quantity is unknown, and it catches mistakes such as using a temperature in place of a temperature change. The steps to solving heat problems are:

  1. Identify which equation to use. If the temperature changes, use q = mcΔT. If there is a solid ↔ liquid change, use q = mHf. If there is a liquid ↔ gas change, use q = mHv.
  2. List the variables and the unknown, each with its unit.
  3. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation.
  4. Solve for the unknown algebraically.
  5. Write the final answer with units.

When the unknown is not q, substitute all of the known values first, then divide both sides by the quantities multiplied by the unknown. Say, for instance, that 2,090 J raises the temperature of a sample of water by 10.0 °C:

2,090 J = m(4.18 J/g·K)(10.0 K)

Dividing both sides by (4.18 J/g·K)(10.0 K) gives m = 50.0 g.

Finally, check that the answer is reasonable. Heat absorbed should be positive. A sample of a few grams should not need millions of joules, and a temperature change should not be larger than the temperatures given in the problem would allow.

    2. List the variables and the unknown, each with its unit.
    3. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation.
    4. Solve for the unknown algebraically.

  • A change of 1 °C is the same size as a change of 1 K, so ΔT has the same value in either unit. You do not need to convert to kelvin.
  • To find the mass, the specific heat capacity or ΔT, substitute everything you know first, then divide both sides by the quantities multiplied by the unknown.
