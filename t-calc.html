Heat Calculations

As chemists, it is necessary to be able to calculate how much heat must be added to a substance, or removed from it, to cause a certain temperature change or phase change. The operator of an ice rink must calculate how much heat to remove to freeze a sheet of water, and an engineer designing a car’s cooling system must know how much heat the coolant can absorb before it boils.

  • We use several different equations to calculate the heat added to or removed from a substance. Which one to use depends on what is happening on the heating curve:
    • Sloped segment (temperature change): q = mcΔT, on this page.
    • Flat segment at the melting point (melting or freezing): q = mHf, on the Heat During Phase Changes page.
    • Flat segment at the boiling point (vaporizing or condensing): q = mHv, on the same page.

Heat During Temperature Changes

Heating 0.50 L of water from 20 °C to 100 °C for tea requires about 167,000 J. The heat needed depends on three things: the mass of the water, its specific heat capacity (4.18 J/g·K) and its change in temperature. In this section, we will calculate heat using q = mcΔT.

Heat During Temperature Changes

A backyard swimming pool takes days to warm up in the spring, while a cup of water in a microwave is hot in a minute. Both are water, so the difference is the amount of matter being heated. The heat needed to change the temperature of a sample depends on three things: its mass, the substance it is made of and how much its temperature changes.

  • When the temperature of a substance changes, the kinetic energy of its particles changes. The heat added to (or removed from) a substance during these intervals is proportional to its change in temperature.
  • Not all substances change their temperature at the same rate. The same amount of heat raises the temperature of some substances much more than others.
  • If the temperature goes up, ΔT and q are positive (heat absorbed). If the temperature goes down, ΔT and q are negative (heat released).

Specific Heat Capacity

At the beach on a sunny afternoon, the sand is too hot to walk on barefoot while the ocean still feels cool, even though the same sunlight has been falling on both all day. Different substances change temperature by different amounts when they absorb the same amount of heat. Water is especially hard to heat up, which is why coastal towns have milder weather than inland towns.

  • How much the temperature of a substance changes when heat is added depends on the intermolecular forces in the substance, the complexity of its particles and its mass. We describe this with a value called specific heat capacity.
    • Specific Heat Capacity (c) – the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K).
    • Unit – joules per gram-kelvin (J/g·K)
  • The specific heat capacity of water (4.18 J/g·K) is much higher than that of aluminum (0.897 J/g·K). If the same amount of heat is added to 1 gram of each, the temperature of the aluminum rises more than that of the water.
  • The higher the specific heat capacity, the smaller the temperature change for the same amount of heat.

Thermal Conductivity vs. Specific Heat Capacity

A copper pan heats up quickly on a stove for two different reasons that are easy to mix up: heat moves through copper fast, and copper needs only a little heat to get hot. These are two different properties with two different names.

  • Thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity are related, but they measure different properties:
    • Thermal conductivity describes how quickly an object transfers heat (into and out of itself).
    • Specific heat capacity describes how much heat is needed to change the temperature of an object.
  • Copper has a high thermal conductivity (heat moves through it fast) and a low specific heat capacity (a little heat raises its temperature a lot).

Solving Temperature-Change Problems

A Regents question might give the mass of a sample, its specific heat capacity and its starting and ending temperatures, and ask for the heat. Another might give the heat and ask for the mass. In this section, we will solve both kinds of problem with the same five steps.

Solving q = mcΔT Problems

Every heat problem can be solved with the same five steps. The first step, choosing the equation, is the one that changes from problem to problem; the rest are the same every time.

  • Steps to solving heat problems:
    1. Identify which equation to use. If the temperature changes, use q = mcΔT. If there is a solid ↔ liquid change, use q = mHf. If there is a liquid ↔ gas change, use q = mHv.
    2. List the variables and the unknown, each with its unit.
    3. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation.
    4. Solve for the unknown algebraically.
    5. Write the final answer with units.
  • A change of 1 °C is the same size as a change of 1 K, so ΔT has the same value in either unit. You do not need to convert to kelvin.
  • To find the mass, the specific heat capacity or ΔT, substitute everything you know first, then divide both sides by the quantities multiplied by the unknown.
