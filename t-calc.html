Calculating Heat

Three equations from the Reference Tables: q = mcΔT for temperature changes, q = mHf for melting and freezing, and q = mHv for vaporizing and condensing.

Heat Calculations

The operator of an ice rink must calculate how much heat to remove to freeze a sheet of water, and an engineer designing a car’s cooling system must know how much heat the coolant can absorb before it boils. Questions like these are answered with three equations. Which one to use depends on whether the substance is changing temperature or changing phase.

  • Which equation to use depends on what is happening on the heating curve:
    • Sloped segment (temperature change): q = mcΔT
    • Flat segment at the melting point (melting or freezing): q = mHf
    • Flat segment at the boiling point (vaporizing or condensing): q = mHv
Part1

Heat During Temperature Changes

Chemists must often determine how much heat is needed to reach a desired temperature. During a temperature change, this heat depends on the mass of the sample, its specific heat capacity and its change in temperature.

Heat During Temperature Changes

A backyard swimming pool takes days to warm up in the spring, while a cup of water in a microwave is hot in a minute. Both are water, so the difference is the amount of matter being heated. The heat needed to change the temperature of a sample depends on three things: its mass, the substance it is made of and how much its temperature changes.

  • When the temperature of a substance changes, the kinetic energy of its particles changes. The heat added during these intervals is proportional to the change in temperature.
  • The heat needed depends on three things: the mass of the sample, the substance (its specific heat capacity) and the change in temperature.
  • If the temperature goes up, ΔT and q are positive (heat absorbed). If the temperature goes down, ΔT and q are negative (heat released).
q=mcΔT
q – heat (J)m – mass of the sample (g)c – specific heat capacity (J/g·K)ΔT – change in temperature (K or °C) = Tfinal − Tinitial

Specific Heat Capacity

At the beach on a sunny afternoon, the sand is too hot to walk on barefoot while the ocean still feels cool, even though the same sunlight has been falling on both all day. Different substances change temperature by different amounts when they absorb the same amount of heat. Water is especially hard to heat up, which is why coastal towns have milder weather than inland towns.

  • How much the temperature of a substance changes when heat is added depends on the intermolecular forces in the substance, the complexity of its particles and its mass. We describe this with a value called specific heat capacity.
    • Specific Heat Capacity (c) – the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K).
    • Unit – joules per gram-kelvin (J/g·K)
  • The specific heat capacity of water (4.18 J/g·K) is much higher than that of aluminum (0.897 J/g·K). If the same amount of heat is added to 1 gram of each, the temperature of the aluminum rises more than that of the water.
  • The higher the specific heat capacity, the smaller the temperature change for the same amount of heat.

Thermal Conductivity vs. Specific Heat Capacity

A copper pan heats up quickly on a stove for two different reasons that are easy to mix up: heat moves through copper fast, and copper needs only a little heat to get hot. These are two different properties with two different names.

  • Thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity are related, but they measure different properties:
    • Thermal conductivity describes how quickly an object transfers heat (into and out of itself).
    • Specific heat capacity describes how much heat is needed to change the temperature of an object.
  • Copper has a high thermal conductivity (heat moves through it fast) and a low specific heat capacity (a little heat raises its temperature a lot).

Part2

Heat During Phase Changes

During a phase change, added heat increases the potential energy of the particles rather than the temperature. A different equation is therefore needed to calculate the heat involved.

Heat During Phase Changes

Bringing a pot of water to a boil takes about 10 minutes, but boiling it all away takes far longer, even though the burner delivers heat at the same rate and the temperature never rises above 100 °C. The heat added during this time is not raising the temperature; it is separating the particles. Because ΔT is zero during a phase change, q = mcΔT cannot describe it, and a different equation is needed.

  • During a phase change, the potential energy of the particles changes, not their kinetic energy. To calculate the heat, we use q = mHf for melting or freezing, and q = mHv for vaporizing or condensing.
  • Heat of Fusion (Hf) – the amount of heat needed to melt (or released when freezing) 1 gram of a substance at its melting point.
  • Heat of Vaporization (Hv) – the amount of heat needed to vaporize (or released when condensing) 1 gram of a substance at its boiling point.
  • Some substances need more heat to change phase than others, because of differences in their intermolecular forces and the conditions of the surroundings. The higher the heat of fusion or vaporization, the more energy must be added to separate the particles, or the more energy is released when the particles move closer together.
q=mHf
q=mHv

Phase Changes in Everyday Life

Because phase changes absorb or release large amounts of heat without changing temperature, people rely on them every day, often without realizing it. Your body, your refrigerator and even farmers protecting their crops on a frosty night all take advantage of the heat of fusion and the heat of vaporization.

  • Sweating cools the body.
    • As sweat evaporates from your skin, every gram absorbs about 2260 J of heat from your body.
    • On a humid day sweat evaporates slowly, so it cools you less.
  • Ice keeps a cooler cold.
    • Melting ice absorbs 334 J for every gram that melts, while staying at 0 °C.
    • This is why a cooler stays cold until the ice is gone, and why ice cools a drink better than the same mass of cold water.
  • Steam causes worse burns than boiling water at the same temperature.
    • When steam touches your skin, it first condenses, releasing 2260 J of heat per gram.
    • Only then does the hot water cool to body temperature, releasing more heat.
  • Freezing water can protect crops.
    • On frosty nights, citrus farmers spray their trees with water.
    • As the water freezes on the fruit, it releases 334 J of heat per gram, which keeps the fruit itself from dropping much below 0 °C.
  • A refrigerator works by letting a liquid refrigerant evaporate inside the coils. The evaporating refrigerant absorbs heat from the food, and the heat is released outside the refrigerator when the refrigerant condenses again.
