Pure Substances and Mixtures

Classification of Matter

A library holds millions of books, yet a reader can find any one of them in minutes, because the books are sorted into categories by subject and then sorted again within each subject. Chemists face a similar problem: there are millions of different kinds of matter. They sort matter by asking two questions about its particles. Does the sample contain only one type of particle, or more than one? And are the particles chemically bonded to one another, or just mixed together?

The answers to these questions place every sample of matter, from seawater to a diamond, into one of four categories: element, compound, homogeneous mixture or heterogeneous mixture. Each category tells a chemist something useful, such as whether the sample can be separated by simple physical means or only by a chemical reaction.

Substances and Mixtures

A bag of white sugar contains nothing but sugar: every particle in it is a sugar molecule. A glass of lemonade contains sugar too, along with water, lemon juice and many other substances. You can make the lemonade sweeter by adding more sugar or weaker by adding more water, and it is still lemonade. You cannot do the same with sugar itself, because sugar with something added is no longer pure sugar.

The first question to ask about any sample of matter is whether it contains one type of particle or more than one. A (pure) substance is a sample of matter that contains only 1 type of particle. A mixture is a sample of matter that contains 2+ different types of particles that are physically combined. Pure sugar, pure water and pure gold are substances, while lemonade, air and soil are mixtures.

Since a substance contains only one type of particle, its composition is always the same. A mixture is different. The ratios of a mixture can vary, which is why two glasses of lemonade can taste different while two spoonfuls of pure sugar always taste the same.

  • (Pure) Substance – a sample of matter that contains only 1 type of particle
  • Mixture – a sample of matter that contains 2+ different types of particles that are physically combined.
    • The ratios of a mixture can vary.

Physically and Chemically Combined

Stir iron filings into powdered yellow sulfur, and the result is a gray-and-yellow powder in which the two substances are simply mixed together. Pass a magnet over it, and the iron filings jump onto the magnet, leaving the sulfur behind. Now heat the same mixture, and it glows red as the iron and sulfur react to form a new compound, iron sulfide, a black solid. The magnet no longer pulls any iron out of the product, because the iron atoms are now bonded to sulfur atoms.

The iron and sulfur show the difference between two ways that particles can be combined. When particles are physically combined, they are not connected by strong chemical attractions (bonds). They just share the same space, so they can be separated easily by physical means, such as a magnet, a filter or boiling. When particles are chemically combined, they are held together by strong chemical attractions (bonds), and they can only be separated through a chemical reaction.

Thus, mixtures are physically combined, while compounds are chemically combined. This is why a mixture can be separated by its physical properties, but a compound cannot.

  • Physically Combined – particles in a sample are not connected by strong chemical attractions (bonds).
    • They just share the same space.
    • They can be separated easily (by physical means).
  • Chemically Combined – particles in a sample are held together by strong chemical attractions (bonds).
    • They can only be separated through a chemical reaction.

See the difference with real substances. Iron and sulfur can be physically mixed or chemically combined. Try to separate them each way.

Elements and Compounds

Pass an electric current through water, and bubbles of two gases form at the metal wires: hydrogen at one wire and oxygen at the other, with twice as much hydrogen as oxygen. The water has been broken down into the elements it is made of. Try the same thing with the hydrogen gas or the oxygen gas, and nothing simpler ever comes out. No chemical reaction can break an element into a simpler substance.

Pure substances come in two kinds. An element is a type of atom that contains a unique number of protons, and an element cannot be broken down into simpler substances by a chemical reaction. A compound is two or more different elements chemically bonded (combined) in a fixed proportion. Since its elements are bonded, a compound can be broken down into simpler substances, but only by a chemical reaction, such as passing electricity through water.

Fixed means that the proportion stays the same and cannot change. Water is always 2 hydrogen atoms bonded to 1 oxygen atom (H2O). If the ratio changes, the result is a different substance. Pyrite, a shiny mineral often called “fool’s gold,” is a compound of iron and sulfur that always contains two sulfur atoms for every iron atom, FeS2, which is why its crystals grow into the same cube shape wherever they are found.

Pure substances come in two kinds:

  • Element – type of atom that contains a unique number of protons.
    • An element cannot be broken down into simpler substances by a chemical reaction.
  • Compound – two or more different elements chemically bonded (combined) in a fixed proportion.
    • A compound can be broken down into simpler substances, but only by a chemical reaction.
    • Fixed – stays the same and cannot change.
      Example
      Water is always 2 hydrogen atoms bonded to 1 oxygen atom (H2O).
      If the ratio changes, it is a different substance.
Carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O) are all examples of elements.
Water (H2O), methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2) are all examples of compounds, as they contain 2+ elements that are chemically bonded together.

Particle Diagrams

Particle Diagrams

Atoms are far too small to see, so chemists draw models of them to show what is happening at the particle level. A particle diagram is a visual aid which shows how atoms and/or molecules are arranged in a substance. Particle diagrams are like a zoomed-in snapshot of a tiny part of a sample, and they make it possible to classify matter by looking at its particles.

In a particle diagram, individual atoms are represented by circles, and different colors or patterns represent different elements. Molecules are represented by circles that touch or overlap, because touching circles mean there is a chemical bond between the atoms. If two different types of atoms touch or overlap, the particle is both a molecule and a compound. If atoms or molecules occupy the same container but do not touch, they are considered a mixture, because they are only physically combined.

The most important question when reading a diagram is whether all of the particles are identical. If every particle is the same, whether it is a single atom or a group of bonded atoms, the sample is a pure substance. If the particles are not all the same, the sample is a mixture. Once that question is answered, the bonds inside each particle tell whether a substance is an element or a compound.

  • Particle Diagram – a visual aid which shows how atoms and/or molecules are arranged in a substance.
    • Individual atoms are represented by circles.
      • Different colors or patterns represent different elements.
    • Molecules are represented by circles that touch or overlap.
      • Touching circles mean there is a chemical bond between the atoms.
    • If two different types of atoms touch or overlap, the particle is both a molecule and a compound.
    • If atoms/molecules occupy the same container but do not touch, they are considered a mixture.
  • To classify a diagram, ask: Are all of the particles identical? Yes → pure substance.
    • No → mixture.

Types of Mixtures

Mixtures

Air is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon and a few other gases. When you breathe in, your lungs take the oxygen out of the air and use it, which is only possible because the oxygen in air is still ordinary oxygen. It has not combined with the nitrogen around it; the two gases simply share the same space. If the oxygen in air were bonded to nitrogen, it would be part of a new compound and could not keep you alive.

A mixture is a sample of matter that contains 2+ different types of particles that are physically combined. Since the particles in a mixture are only physically combined, each component retains its physical and chemical properties. Salt water still tastes salty, and the iron in a mixture of iron and sand is still pulled by a magnet.

Mixtures also have variable proportions: the amount of each ingredient can change without changing the identity of the mixture. Air high on a mountain has less oxygen in each breath than air at sea level, and the air in a crowded room has more carbon dioxide, yet each is still air. Thus, a mixture has no single formula, while a compound always does.

  • Mixture – a sample of matter that contains 2+ different types of particles that are physically combined.
    • The proportions of a mixture can vary.
    • Variable – able to change.
      • You can add more or less of each ingredient and it is still the same kind of mixture.
    • Since the particles in a mixture are only physically combined, each component retains its physical and chemical properties.
  • Variable proportions – you can change the amount of each ingredient without changing the identity of the mixture.
Both samples are mixtures of 2 elements, A and B. However, their proportions are different. Sample 1 has a ratio A:B of 2:3 while sample 2 has a ratio of 4:1. Their proportions are different, but they are still mixtures of the same substances.

Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Mixtures

A bowl of trail mix and a glass of salt water are both mixtures, but they look very different. A handful of trail mix might have more raisins than nuts, while the next handful has mostly nuts, so two samples of the same bowl can be quite different. A sip from the top of the salt water tastes exactly as salty as a sip from the bottom, because the salt particles are spread evenly through the water.

Homogeneous mixtures are mixtures in which the particles are distributed evenly (uniform) throughout, so each sample of the mixture has the same proportion of each type of particle. Heterogeneous mixtures are mixtures in which the particles are distributed unevenly throughout, so different samples of the mixture have different numbers of each type of particle.

A common mistake is to think that uniform means equal. Uniform means the same throughout, while equal means the same amount. Air is a homogeneous mixture even though it is about 78% nitrogen and only about 21% oxygen. The two gases are not present in equal amounts, but every breath of air in a room has the same proportions as every other breath.

  • Homogeneous Mixtures – particles in the mixture are distributed evenly (uniform) throughout.
    • Each sample of the mixture has the same proportion (ratio) of each type of particle.
    • Uniform – the same throughout.
      • Any sample you take has the same composition.
      • Equal – the same amount.
        • Uniform does not mean equal.
        Example
        Salt water has far more water than salt, but the salt is spread evenly, so it is still uniform.
    Example
    Salt water, air, alloys (such as brass)
  • Heterogeneous Mixtures – particles in the mixture are distributed unevenly throughout.
    • Different samples of the mixture have different numbers of each type of particle.
    Example
    Salad, sand + water, granite

Solutions

Stir a spoonful of sugar into a glass of iced tea, and within a minute the sugar seems to disappear. It has not gone anywhere: the tea tastes sweet, and if the water were allowed to evaporate, the sugar would be left behind. The sugar has broken apart into individual molecules that are far too small to see, and they have spread out evenly among the water molecules.

A solution is a type of homogeneous mixture, usually a solid, liquid or gas dissolved in a liquid. Every solution has two parts. The solute is the substance that dissolves, and it is present in the smaller amount. The solvent is the substance that does the dissolving, and it is present in the larger amount. In sweet tea, the sugar is the solute and the water is the solvent. How much of a solute can dissolve in a solvent is referred to as its solubility.

When a substance dissolves, its particles do not disappear. They separate from one another and spread out through the solvent, but they do not change their chemical identity: dissolved sugar is still sugar, which is why the tea is sweet. Since the solute particles are too small to see, a solution looks the same throughout. A solution in which the solvent is water is referred to as an aqueous solution, written with (aq), as in NaCl(aq), salt dissolved in water.

  • Solution – a type of homogeneous mixture.
    • Usually a solid, liquid or gas dissolved in a liquid.
  • Every solution has 2 parts:
    • Solute – the substance that dissolves.
      • Present in the smaller amount.
    • Solvent – the substance that does the dissolving.
      • Present in the larger amount.
  • Solubility – how much of a substance (the solute) can dissolve in another substance (the solvent).
  • When a substance dissolves, its particles do not disappear.
    • The particles separate from each other, breaking into smaller pieces that spread out through the solvent.
    • The particles do not change their chemical identity. Dissolved sugar is still sugar.
  • Aqueous Solution – a solution in which the solvent is water.
  • The solute particles in a solution are too small to be seen, so the solution looks the same throughout.
(aq) means aqueous: dissolved in water.  Ex: NaCl(aq) is salt dissolved in water.
