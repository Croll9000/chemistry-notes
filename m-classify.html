Pure Substances and Mixtures

A pure substance contains one type of particle. A mixture contains more than one.

Classification of Matter

Scientists will often categorize things to make them easier to study and draw comparisons between them. Chemists are no different. All matter can be classified into a number of different categories.

Click any bubble to see its definition and an example.

Substances and Mixtures

Very little of the matter we encounter in daily life is pure. Air, seawater, tap water and even the gold in most jewelry contain more than one type of particle.

  • (Pure) Substance – a sample of matter that contains only 1 type of particle
  • Mixture – a sample of matter that contains 2+ different types of particles that are physically combined.
    • The ratios of a mixture can vary.

The first mixture contains multiple types of particles (green + yellow). Their ratio is 3:2, green:yellow.

Physically and Chemically Combined

When substances are put together, sometimes they will form something entirely new. We say they are chemically combined. Other times they will simply coexist without forming any chemical bonds. In this case they are referred to as physically combined.

  • Physically Combined – particles in a sample are not connected by strong chemical attractions (bonds).
    • They just share the same space.
    • They can be separated easily (by physical means).
  • Chemically Combined – particles in a sample are held together by strong chemical attractions (bonds).
    • They can only be separated through a chemical reaction.

See the difference with real substances. Iron and sulfur can be physically mixed or chemically combined. Try to separate them each way.

Elements and Compounds

Sodium is a soft, reactive metal and chlorine is a toxic, greenish-yellow gas. When the two are chemically combined, they form sodium chloride, the table salt sprinkled on food. Pure substances can be single elements or compounds formed from them.

Pure substances come in two kinds:

  • Element – atoms containing the same number of protons.
    • An element cannot be broken down into simpler substances by a chemical reaction.
  • Compound – two or more elements that are chemically combined in a fixed ratio.
    • A compound can be broken down into simpler substances, but only by a chemical reaction.
    • Fixed – stays the same and cannot change.
      • Ex: water is always 2 hydrogen atoms bonded to 1 oxygen atom (H2O).
      • If the ratio changes, it is a different substance.
Carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O) are all examples of elements.
Water (H2O), methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2) are all examples of compounds, as they contain 2+ elements that are chemically bonded together.

Particle Diagrams

When studying matter it is often necessary to visualize the arrangement of particles that make up a substance. This arrangement will determine the characteristics of that substance. To aid in this process we create visual representations of them at the particle level, known as Particle Diagrams.

  • Particle Diagram – a visual aid which shows how atoms and/or molecules are arranged in a substance.
    • Individual atoms are represented by circles.
      • Different colors or patterns represent different elements.
    • Molecules are represented by circles that touch or overlap.
      • Touching circles mean there is a chemical bond between the atoms.
    • If two different types of atoms touch or overlap, the particle is both a molecule and a compound.
    • If atoms/molecules occupy the same container but do not touch, they are considered a mixture.
  • To classify a diagram, ask: Are all of the particles identical? Yes → pure substance. No → mixture.

Types of Mixtures

Mixtures keep the properties of their parts, and they can be evenly or unevenly mixed.

Mixtures

Trail mix, salad dressing and seawater are all mixtures. In each case, the ingredients can be combined in different amounts and the result is still the same type of mixture.

  • Mixture – two or more substances that are physically combined.
    • The proportions of a mixture can vary.
    • Variable – able to change.
      • You can add more or less of each ingredient and it is still the same kind of mixture.
    • Since the particles in a mixture are only physically combined, each component retains its physical and chemical properties.
  • Variable proportions – you can change the amount of each ingredient without changing the identity of the mixture.
Both samples are mixtures of 2 elements, A and B. However, their proportions are different. Sample 1 has a ratio A:B of 2:3 while sample 2 has a ratio of 4:1. Their proportions are different, but they are still mixtures of the same substances.

Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Mixtures

Some mixtures, such as a bowl of cereal and milk, have parts that are easy to tell apart. Others, such as salt water, look the same throughout, even under a microscope. Chemists classify mixtures based on how evenly their particles are distributed.

  • Homogeneous Mixtures – particles in the mixture are distributed evenly (uniform) throughout.
    • Each sample of the mixture has the same proportion (ratio) of each type of particle.
    • Uniform – the same throughout.
      • Any sample you take has the same composition.
      • Equal – the same amount.
        • Uniform does not mean equal.
        • Ex: salt water has far more water than salt, but the salt is spread evenly, so it is still uniform.
    • Ex: salt water, air, alloys (such as brass)
  • Heterogeneous Mixtures – particles in the mixture are distributed unevenly throughout.
    • Different samples of the mixture have different numbers of each type of particle.
    • Ex: salad, sand + water, granite
Note how in the homogeneous mixture the different types of particles are evenly (uniformly) distributed.
In the heterogeneous mixture the different types of particles are unevenly distributed. The red particles are at the bottom of the container while the blue particles are at the top.

Solutions

Many common liquids are solutions. Soda is carbon dioxide and sugar dissolved in water, and the ocean is water with dissolved salts. Solutions are the most common type of homogeneous mixture.

  • Solution – a type of homogeneous mixture.
    • Usually a solid, liquid or gas dissolved in a liquid.
  • Every solution has 2 parts:
    • Solute – the substance that dissolves.
      • Present in the smaller amount.
    • Solvent – the substance that does the dissolving.
      • Present in the larger amount.
  • Solubility – a substance’s ability to dissolve in a liquid.
  • When a substance dissolves, its particles do not disappear.
    • The particles separate from each other, breaking into smaller pieces that spread out through the solvent.
    • The particles do not change their chemical identity. Dissolved sugar is still sugar.
  • Aqueous Solution – a solution in which the solvent is water.
  • The solute particles in a solution are too small to be seen, so the solution looks the same throughout.
(aq) means aqueous: dissolved in water.  Ex: NaCl(aq) is salt dissolved in water.
