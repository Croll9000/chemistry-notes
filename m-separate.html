Separating by Physical Properties

Separating Mixtures

Recall that the parts of a mixture are only physically combined, so each part keeps its own properties. This is what makes it possible to separate them by physical means. Every separation method takes advantage of a difference in one physical property of the substances in the mixture:

  • Filtration separates substances by particle size.
  • Distillation separates them by boiling point.
  • Chromatography separates them by solubility and attraction to a surface.
  • A magnet separates iron from other substances, since only the iron is attracted to it.

For instance, when old computers and phones are recycled, they are shredded and then separated step by step. Magnets pull out the iron and steel, and differences in density separate the plastic from the heavier metals. In this way, small amounts of gold, silver and copper can be recovered and used again.

However, compounds cannot be separated by any of these physical methods, since their elements are chemically bonded. Separating a compound into its elements always requires a chemical reaction.

Filtration

In the lab, the simplest way to separate a solid from a liquid is filtration, and it works by particle size. Filter paper is a porous barrier, full of tiny holes. Small particles, such as water molecules and dissolved particles, pass through the holes. Large particles, such as grains of sand, are trapped.

Filtration – laboratory technique used to separate substances based on differences in particle size using filter paper.

In chemistry, filtration is usually used to separate insoluble solids from liquids. The solid that stays behind on the filter paper is collected, and the liquid that passes through, called the filtrate, is collected in a beaker below.

Note that filtration cannot separate a solution. Dissolved particles are far smaller than the holes in the paper, so they pass straight through with the water. Pouring salt water through a filter gives you salt water.

Filtration works on a much larger scale too. Many drinking water treatment plants pass their water through deep beds of sand and gravel before it reaches a faucet. Clumps of dirt and other particles are caught in the tiny spaces between the sand grains, and the water flows through. Kidney dialysis machines take the idea further. A patient’s blood flows past a membrane with holes so small that blood cells and proteins are held back, but waste molecules pass through and are carried away.

Filtration and Solubility

Say, for instance, that a bag of sand and a bag of table salt are spilled together on the floor. Both are white or tan grains of about the same size. Picking them apart grain by grain would take days. Pouring the mixture into a filter would not help either, since both kinds of grains are too large to pass through. Yet the mixture can be separated in a few minutes using nothing but water, filter paper and a heat source.

The key is solubility. Salt dissolves in water, while sand does not. When a substance dissolves, it breaks apart into individual particles that are too small to be seen and small enough to fit through filter paper. Thus, filtration can be used to separate two solids based on differences in their solubility in a liquid.

Adding water to the mixture dissolves the salt but not the sand. Passing the mixture through filter paper now separates the two solids. The filter paper traps the insoluble solid, the sand. The soluble solid passes through with the water.

Heating the salt water then evaporates the water and leaves the salt behind. Notice that each step uses a different property. Dissolving and filtration separate the sand by solubility and particle size. Evaporation separates the salt from the water by boiling point.

When sand, salt (NaCl) and water are mixed, the salt dissolves in the water but the sand doesn’t. The dissolved salt particles are small enough to pass through the filter paper. However, the insoluble sand particles are too large and get caught. To separate the salt from the water, the water can be boiled off (evaporation).

Distillation

Filtration cannot separate two liquids that are mixed together, since both pass through the paper. Instead, chemists use a difference in their boiling points, a technique called distillation.

Distillation – laboratory technique used to separate mixtures of liquids based on differences in their boiling points.

Since different liquids have different boiling points, when a mixture of liquids is slowly heated, the liquid with the lowest boiling point turns into vapor first. The vapor travels into a condenser, a glass tube surrounded by an outer jacket of cold running water. The cold water cools the vapor and turns it back into a liquid, which drips into a collecting beaker.

A thermometer at the top of the flask keeps track of which liquid is vaporizing. Recall that temperature stays constant during a phase change. The reading therefore holds steady while one liquid boils away. When the temperature starts to rise again, that liquid has finished vaporizing, and the beaker is swapped for a new one to collect the next component.

Oil refineries use the same idea on an enormous scale. Crude oil is heated until most of it boils, and the hot vapor rises through a tall tower that gets cooler toward the top. Each substance in the vapor condenses at its own boiling point. Heavy oils with high boiling points condense low in the tower. Gasoline, with a much lower boiling point, rises higher before it condenses. In this way, the tower sorts a mixture of thousands of substances into fuels collected at different heights.

Chromatography

In 1903, the Russian botanist Mikhail Tsvet poured a green extract of plant leaves through a glass tube packed with chalk powder, then washed it through with a liquid solvent. The green extract separated into bands of green and yellow that traveled down the tube at different speeds. Tsvet concluded that the leaves had contained several different pigments all along, mixed together so that only the green showed. He named his technique chromatography, from the Greek words for “color writing.”

Tsvet’s result explains why leaves turn yellow and orange in the fall. The green pigment breaks down as the days get shorter, and the other colors that were hidden beneath it finally show.

Today, chromatography separates substances based on two differences: their solubility, how well they dissolve in the solvent, and how strongly they are attracted to the paper.

Chromatography – laboratory technique used to separate substances based on two differences: their solubility in the solvent and their attraction to the paper.

All chromatography procedures involve two phases. The mobile phase is the liquid that carries the mixture along. The stationary phase is the fixed material, such as paper, that the liquid and mixture travel through.

In paper chromatography, a dot of the mixture is placed near the bottom of a strip of paper, and the bottom edge is dipped in a solvent. As the solvent soaks up the paper, it carries the substances in the dot along with it. A substance that dissolves well in the solvent and is only weakly attracted to the paper travels far up the strip. A substance that is strongly attracted to the paper travels only a short distance. The substances spread out into separate spots.

Forensic scientists use this to tell whether two inks came from the same pen. Sports drug testing labs use more advanced forms of chromatography to separate the thousands of substances in an athlete’s urine sample and check each one against a list of banned drugs.

  • Chromatography – laboratory technique used to separate substances based on two differences:
    • their solubility (how well they dissolve in the solvent)
    • how strongly they are attracted to the paper
    • Different substances can have different solubilities in the same liquid.
      • Recall: solubility is how well a substance can dissolve in a liquid.
  • All chromatography procedures involve a mobile phase and a stationary phase:
    • Mobile phase – liquid that carries the mixture up a material
    • Stationary phase – fixed material that the liquid and mixture travel through
In paper chromatography, a liquid solvent is the mobile phase and filter paper is the stationary phase. The mixture is placed near the bottom of the paper. The liquid is pulled up the paper (capillary action), carrying the components with it. Components that are more soluble in the liquid travel farther up the paper; components more attracted to the paper stay lower. This separates the components.

Run a paper chromatogram on four inks to determine which inks are mixtures and which colors they contain.
