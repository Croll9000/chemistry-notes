Separating by Physical Properties

Each separation method uses a different physical property.

Separating Mixtures

Many important processes depend on separating mixtures. Water treatment plants remove dirt and debris from drinking water, oil refineries separate crude oil into gasoline and other fuels, and forensic scientists separate the dyes in an ink sample to identify the pen that was used.

Since mixtures are physical combinations of substances, the components of a mixture retain their physical properties. Thus, they can be separated by differences in these physical properties. (Compounds can’t be separated this way: their elements are chemically bonded.)

Filtrationparticle size (solubility)sand from salt water

Filtration

Coffee filters, water pitcher filters and the air filters in a home heating system all work on the same principle. They allow some particles to pass through while trapping others.

  • Filtration – laboratory technique used to separate substances based on differences in particle size using filter paper.
    • Filter paper is a porous barrier.
      • Small particles pass through.
      • Large particles are trapped.
      • In chemistry it is usually used to separate insoluble solids from liquids.

Filtration and Solubility

  • Filtration can be used to separate 2 solids based on differences in their solubility in a liquid.
    • Adding a liquid to the mixture dissolves one solid but not the other.
      • Recall: when something dissolves, it breaks apart into individual particles that are too small to be seen.
    • Passing this mixture through filter paper separates the two solids.
      • The filter paper traps the insoluble solid.
      • The soluble solid passes through with the liquid.
When sand, salt (NaCl) and water are mixed, the salt dissolves in the water but the sand doesn’t. The dissolved salt particles are small enough to pass through the filter paper. However, the insoluble sand particles are too large and get caught. To separate the salt from the water, the water can be boiled off (evaporation).

Distillation

Some mixtures, like rubbing alcohol, cannot be separated with a filter because their components are liquids that pass right through. In cases like these, chemists take advantage of the fact that different liquids boil at different temperatures. The same process is used in refineries to separate crude oil into gasoline, diesel and other fuels.

  • Distillation – laboratory technique used to separate mixtures of liquids based on differences in their boiling points.
    • Different liquids have different boiling points.
      • In a distillation, a mixture of liquids is slowly heated so that only one liquid vaporizes at a time.
      • This liquid is then collected in a separate container.
  • When the mixture of liquids is slowly heated, the liquid with the lowest boiling point turns into vapor first.
  • A thermometer keeps track of which liquid is vaporizing.
    • When the temperature stays constant, one of the liquids is vaporizing.
    • When the temperature starts to rise again, that liquid has finished vaporizing.
  • A condenser is a glass tube with cold water running through an outer shell.
    • The cold water cools the vapor, turning it back into a liquid for collection.
  • Each component is collected in its own beaker.
    • When the temperature starts rising, the beaker is swapped for a new one to collect the next component.

Heat the mixture, read the thermometer, and change the beaker at the right moment.

Chromatography

Black ink is often not a single dye but a mixture of several colors. Forensic scientists can separate these dyes to match the ink on a document to a particular pen, and similar methods are used in drug testing and food safety labs.

  • Chromatography – laboratory technique used to separate substances based on two differences:
    • their solubility (how well they dissolve in the solvent)
    • how strongly they are attracted to the paper
    • Different substances can have different solubilities in the same liquid.
      • Recall: solubility is how well a substance can dissolve in a liquid.
  • All chromatography procedures involve a mobile phase and a stationary phase:
    • Mobile phase – liquid that carries the mixture up a material
    • Stationary phase – fixed material that the liquid and mixture travel through
In paper chromatography, a liquid solvent is the mobile phase and filter paper is the stationary phase. The mixture is placed near the bottom of the paper. The liquid is pulled up the paper (capillary action), carrying the components with it. Components that are more soluble in the liquid travel farther up the paper; components more attracted to the paper stay lower. This separates the components.

Run a paper chromatogram on four inks to determine which inks are mixtures and which colors they contain.
