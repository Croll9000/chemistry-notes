Advanced Double Replacement Reactions

The Reference Tables list only a handful of ions that always form compounds that dissolve in water. For many other pairs of ions, chemists turn to a larger solubility table or to a short set of solubility rules. With either one, it is possible to predict whether a precipitate will form when almost any two ionic solutions are mixed.

The Solubility Table

Solubility Table

To predict whether a precipitate will form, chemists need to know how ions in aqueous solutions will interact with each other. Some compounds dissolve completely, some dissolve only partially and some are insoluble and form precipitates.

The solubility table gives the solubility of many ionic compounds in a single grid. The positive ion is found along the top and the negative ion along the side, and the box where they meet shows whether the compound is soluble (S), slightly soluble (SS) or insoluble (I). A product marked I forms a precipitate. A product marked S stays dissolved. This is exactly the information needed to decide whether a double replacement reaction occurs.

Water treatment plants rely on the same information. To remove harmful dissolved metals, such as lead, from water, they add a compound whose ions form an insoluble solid with the metal ions. The solid settles out and is filtered away.

Want to memorize the general patterns? Solubility rules
  1. Compounds of group 1 ions (Li+, Na+, K+, etc.) and ammonium (NH4+) are soluble.
  2. Nitrates (NO3−), acetates (C2H3O2−) and chlorates (ClO3−) are soluble.
  3. Chlorides (Cl−), bromides (Br−) and iodides (I−) are soluble, except when combined with Ag+, Pb2+ and Hg22+.
  4. Sulfates (SO42−) are soluble, except when combined with Ba2+ and Pb2+ (insoluble) or Ca2+ and Ag+ (slightly soluble).
  5. Fluorides (F−) are soluble, except when combined with Mg2+, Ca2+, Ba2+ and Pb2+.
  6. With the exception of rule 1, carbonates (CO32−), phosphates (PO43−) and sulfites (SO32−) are insoluble.
  7. With the exception of rule 1 and the barium ion (Ba2+), hydroxides (OH−) are insoluble. Calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble.

A dash (—) means the compound does not exist as a stable solid.
