Advanced Lewis Structures

Circling pairs of electrons works well for neutral molecules, but it breaks down for polyatomic ions such as carbonate, CO32−, whose extra electrons belong to no single atom. Chemists therefore count every valence electron in the molecule or ion and place them one pair at a time. The same counting method also reveals bonds in which one atom supplies both electrons, and molecules that cannot be described by a single Lewis structure at all.

The Electron-Counting Method

Drawing Lewis Structures by Counting Electrons

The carbonate ion, CO32−, found in limestone and seashells, contains one carbon atom, three oxygen atoms and 2 extra electrons that give the ion its charge. Extra or missing electrons cannot be shown by circling pairs on individual atoms, so for ions and larger molecules chemists use a different method. They count the total number of valence electrons in the whole particle, then place those electrons as bonds and lone pairs until every atom is satisfied.

When counting, the charge must be included. A negative charge means extra electrons, which are added to the count, while a positive charge means missing electrons, which are subtracted. Carbonate has 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24 valence electrons. The method works for any molecule or ion, and it guarantees that the final structure contains exactly the right number of electrons. To draw a Lewis structure by counting electrons:

Steps to drawing Lewis structures for covalent molecules:

  1. Count up the total number of valence electrons in each atom of the molecule. If the molecule has a charge, add/subtract the appropriate number of electrons.
    • A negative charge means extra electrons (add). A positive charge means missing electrons (subtract).
  2. Determine the atom that can form the greatest number of bonds and place that in the center of the molecule.
  3. Draw a single bond to every other atom in the molecule. Keep track of the total number of electrons used.
  4. Place any remaining electrons as lone pairs around the outer atoms.
  5. If any electrons remain, add them as lone pairs to the central atom.
  6. Double/triple bond if necessary.

Coordinate Covalent Bonds

Smelling salts, once used to revive people who had fainted, release ammonia gas, NH3. An ammonia molecule has a lone pair on its nitrogen atom, and it can use that pair to bond to a hydrogen ion, H+, which has no electrons at all. The result is the ammonium ion, NH4+. In the new N–H bond, both electrons came from nitrogen.

In a normal covalent bond, each atom contributes 1 electron to the bond. However, sometimes a single atom can contribute both electrons to the bond. A coordinate covalent bond is a covalent bond where both shared electrons come from the same atom. The atom with the lone pair gives, while the atom with an empty space receives.

Once it forms, a coordinate covalent bond is identical to any other single bond. In the ammonium ion, all four N–H bonds have the same length and strength, and there is no way to tell which one formed from nitrogen’s lone pair.

    • However, sometimes a single atom can contribute both electrons to the bond.
    • This type of bond is known as a coordinate covalent bond.
  • Coordinate Covalent Bond – a covalent bond where both shared electrons come from the same atom.
  • Once it forms, a coordinate covalent bond is identical to any other single bond.

Resonance Structures

When chemists measured the bonds in ozone, O3, they found something its Lewis structure could not explain. The Lewis structure shows one single bond and one double bond, which should have different lengths, yet both bonds in ozone are exactly the same length, somewhere between a single and a double bond. The electrons of the second bond are not stuck between one particular pair of atoms. They are spread across the whole molecule.

To represent the delocalized nature of some electrons in molecules, chemists draw sets of Lewis structures that show different possible locations for the electrons and higher-order bonds. Resonance structures are sets of Lewis diagrams that show varying positions of electrons and bonds within the same molecule. Electrons that are delocalized are spread out over more than two atoms instead of belonging to one bond.

In reality, no one Lewis structure is correct. The actual molecule is a blend of all of its resonance structures, with electrons delocalized across multiple bonds. The molecule does not switch back and forth between the structures. A useful comparison is a mule, the offspring of a horse and a donkey: a mule is not a horse one moment and a donkey the next, but always a blend of both.

  • To best represent the delocalized nature of some electrons in molecules, we draw sets of Lewis structures that show different possible locations for the electrons and higher order bonds. We call these sets of structures resonance structures.
  • Resonance Structures – sets of Lewis diagrams that show varying positions of electrons and bonds within the same molecule.
  • In reality, no one Lewis structure is correct. Rather, the actual molecule is a blend of all possible Lewis structures, with electrons delocalized across multiple bonds.
    • Delocalized – spread out over more than two atoms instead of belonging to one bond.
Ozone, O3, is a molecule consisting of 3 oxygen atoms bonded together with a single bond and a double bond. We can draw 2 possible resonance structures of ozone, with the double bond to either the left or right oxygen. In reality, the bonding electrons are spread out across each bond, creating something like two 1½ bonds rather than a single and a double bond.
Drawing a normal Lewis structure for carbonate (CO32−) would lead one to believe it has one double and two single bonds, with charges of 1− on the single-bonded oxygens. Carbonate really has 3 bonds of equal length, as the electrons from the double bond are spread out over the whole ion. Additionally, each oxygen has a partially negative charge.

Formal Charge

  • When drawing Lewis structures, it can be useful to keep track of how many electrons each atom “owns” compared to how many it started with.
  • Formal Charge – the charge an atom would have if the electrons in each of its bonds were shared equally.
  • Formal Charge = (valence electrons) − (lone pair electrons) − (number of bonds)
    • Each line counts as 1 electron that belongs to the atom, since the other electron belongs to its partner.
    • The formal charges in a structure always add up to the overall charge of the molecule or ion.
  • When more than one structure can be drawn, the best structure is usually the one with formal charges closest to zero, with any negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom.
