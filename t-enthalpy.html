Bond Energy & Heats of Reaction

Breaking bonds absorbs energy; forming bonds releases energy. The difference is the heat of reaction (ΔH): negative for exothermic reactions, positive for endothermic reactions.

Part1

Bond Energies

Chemical bonds are attractions between atoms, and overcoming these attractions requires energy. The energy needed to break a specific type of bond is known as its bond energy.

Bond Energies

The main engines of the Space Shuttle were powered by one of the simplest reactions in chemistry: hydrogen combining with oxygen to form water. That reaction releases an enormous amount of energy, but the energy does not come from the hydrogen or the oxygen alone. It comes from the difference between the energy needed to break the bonds in the reactants and the energy released when the bonds in the products form.

  • Chemical bonds are strong attractions between atoms. To break a bond and overcome that attraction, energy must be added, which leaves the atoms less stable.
  • The reverse is also true: when a bond forms, energy is released as the atoms become more stable.
  • Not every bond takes the same amount of energy to break.
    • Some bonds are stronger than others because of the elements involved, their sizes, lone pairs and other factors.
    • A double or triple bond between the same two atoms is stronger than a single bond.
  • Bonds store energy as potential energy, just like the particles in a phase change.
    • Two atoms that are far apart have the most potential energy.
    • As they move closer, each nucleus attracts the other atom’s electrons, and their potential energy decreases.
    • At the bond length, attraction and repulsion are balanced and the potential energy is at its lowest. Pushed any closer, the two nuclei repel and the potential energy rises again.
  • The energy needed to break a bond is equal to the energy released when that same bond forms.
    • Bond Energy – the energy required to break 1 mole of a specific type of bond in the gas phase.
    • The stronger the bond, the larger its bond energy and the more stable the molecule.
Example
Water, H2O, and hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, are both made of hydrogen and oxygen atoms and both look like clear, colorless liquids.
Hydrogen peroxide contains a weak O–O bond (146 kJ/mol), while water contains only strong O–H bonds (467 kJ/mol).
This is why hydrogen peroxide slowly breaks down into water and oxygen in its brown bottle, while water is stable.

Breaking and Forming Bonds

In a chemical reaction, energy must be added to break the bonds of the reactants, and energy is released when the bonds of the products form. The difference between the two determines whether the reaction is endothermic or exothermic.

Breaking and Forming Bonds

Every reaction is a trade: energy in to break the old bonds, energy out when the new bonds form. It is a little like a business that spends money to buy supplies and earns money selling its products. If it earns more than it spends, there is energy left over and the reaction warms its surroundings. If it spends more than it earns, it has to take energy from its surroundings, and they cool down.

  • In a chemical reaction, energy must be added to break the bonds in the reactants. When new bonds form to create the products, energy is released.
    • If more energy is needed to break the old bonds than is released forming the new bonds, there is a net absorption of energy and the reaction is endothermic.
    • If more energy is released forming the new bonds than is needed to break the old bonds, there is a net release of energy and the reaction is exothermic.
Example
In N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g), energy is needed to break the N≡N and H–H bonds. When the new N–H bonds form, more energy is released than was absorbed, so the reaction is exothermic.
  • The heat of reaction can be estimated from these bond energies: ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed). See Calculating ΔH from Bond Energies below.

Enthalpy (Heat) of Reaction

Every chemical reaction is accompanied by a change in energy. The enthalpy of reaction describes the amount of energy absorbed or released, and its sign indicates the direction of the energy flow.

Enthalpy

Squeeze a cold pack from a first-aid kit and it turns icy within seconds; snap a hand warmer and it heats up instead. Both are chemical changes. One releases energy to its surroundings and the other absorbs energy from them. Chemists describe both with a single number, the enthalpy change, whose sign tells you which way the energy flows.

  • Every chemical reaction is accompanied by a change in energy due to the breaking and forming of bonds. We call the energy absorbed or released during a chemical reaction the enthalpy, or heat of reaction.
    • Enthalpy (Heat) of Reaction (ΔH) – the difference in energy between the products and the reactants in a chemical reaction at constant pressure. It is the energy absorbed or released during the reaction.
    • Endothermic Reaction – reaction which absorbs energy from its surroundings.
    • Exothermic Reaction – reaction which releases energy to its surroundings.
  • The enthalpy change is found by comparing the energy of the products with the energy of the reactants (see the box below).
  • If ΔH is positive (+), the reaction is endothermic and heat is a reactant.
    • N2(g) + 2O2(g) + 66.4 kJ → 2NO2(g), or N2(g) + 2O2(g) → 2NO2(g)  ΔH = +66.4 kJ
  • If ΔH is negative (−), the reaction is exothermic and heat is a product.
    • 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(ℓ) + 571.6 kJ, or 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(ℓ)  ΔH = −571.6 kJ
    • The negative sign only appears in ΔH. We do not write a negative sign on the heat term in the equation itself.
  • The ΔH value applies to the equation exactly as written, with those coefficients and phases.
ΔH=Hproducts − Hreactants

Calculating ΔH from Bond Energies

Chemists cannot always measure a heat of reaction in a calorimeter. The reaction may be too slow, too dangerous or impossible to run cleanly. Instead, they can estimate ΔH on paper by adding up the energy needed to break every bond in the reactants and subtracting the energy released by forming every bond in the products.

  • The enthalpy (heat) of reaction is equal to the sum of the bond energies of the bonds broken minus the sum of the bond energies of the bonds formed.
    • Breaking bonds in the reactants absorbs energy, so these bond energies are added.
    • Forming bonds in the products releases energy, so these bond energies are subtracted.
  • Bond energies are given per mole of bonds, so multiply each bond energy by the number of moles of that bond in the balanced equation.
    • Ex: 2HCl contains 2 moles of H–Cl bonds. CH4 contains 4 moles of C–H bonds.
  • If more energy is absorbed breaking bonds than is released forming bonds, ΔH is positive and the reaction is endothermic. If more energy is released, ΔH is negative and the reaction is exothermic.
  • Average bond energies give an estimate. Measured heats of reaction (like those in the table below) are more exact.
ΔH=Σ(bond energies of bonds broken)−Σ(bond energies of bonds formed)

Part4

Manipulating Reactions and Their Enthalpies

The enthalpy of a reaction applies to the reaction exactly as it is written. Reversing the reaction or changing its coefficients changes its enthalpy in predictable ways.

Manipulating Reactions and Their Enthalpies

Burning one mole of methane releases 890.4 kJ, so burning two moles releases twice as much. And if a reaction releases energy going forward, running it backward must take that same energy back in, or energy would be created from nothing, breaking the law of conservation of energy.

  • The enthalpy of a reaction applies to the reaction exactly as it is written. Changing the coefficients or reversing the reaction changes its enthalpy.
    • Multiplying or dividing – if all of the coefficients are multiplied or divided by a number, ΔH is multiplied or divided by the same number.
    • Reversing the reaction – if a reaction is reversed, the sign of ΔH is switched, but its size stays the same. An endothermic reaction becomes exothermic, and an exothermic reaction becomes endothermic.
