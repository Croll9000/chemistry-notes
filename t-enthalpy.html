Bond Energy & Heats of Reaction

Breaking bonds absorbs energy; forming bonds releases energy. The difference is the heat of reaction (ΔH): negative for exothermic reactions, positive for endothermic reactions.

Bond Energies

Bond Energies

A single gram of hydrogen gas burning in oxygen releases about 120 kJ of energy, more energy per gram than any other fuel, so liquid hydrogen is used to power the upper stages of many rockets. Where does that energy come from? It comes from the difference between the bonds that are broken and the bonds that are formed. Recall that to break a bond, energy must be added to pull the atoms apart against their attraction. When a bond forms, the reverse happens. Energy is released as the atoms come together and become more stable.

Bonds store energy as potential energy, just like the particles in a phase change. Two hydrogen atoms that are far apart have the most potential energy. As they approach, each nucleus attracts the other atom’s electron, and their potential energy decreases. At the bond length, attraction and repulsion are balanced, and the potential energy is at its lowest. If they are pushed any closer, the two nuclei repel, and the potential energy rises again. To break the bond, the atoms must be lifted back out of this low point, which takes exactly the energy that was released when the bond formed. Thus, the energy needed to break a bond is equal to the energy released when that same bond forms.

Not every bond takes the same amount of energy to break, and we compare them using their bond energy. The stronger the bond, the larger its bond energy and the more stable the molecule. Bond strength depends on the elements involved, their sizes and the number of shared electron pairs. A double bond between two carbon atoms (614 kJ/mol) is stronger than a single bond (347 kJ/mol), and a triple bond (839 kJ/mol) is stronger still.

Bond Energy – the energy required to break 1 mole of a specific type of bond in the gas phase.

  • Chemical bonds are strong attractions between atoms. To break a bond and overcome that attraction, energy must be added, which leaves the atoms less stable.
  • The reverse is also true: when a bond forms, energy is released as the atoms become more stable.
  • Not every bond takes the same amount of energy to break.
    • Some bonds are stronger than others because of the elements involved, their sizes, lone pairs and other factors.
    • A double or triple bond between the same two atoms is stronger than a single bond.
  • Bonds store energy as potential energy, just like the particles in a phase change.

Example
Water, H2O, and hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, are both made of hydrogen and oxygen atoms and both look like clear, colorless liquids.
Hydrogen peroxide contains a weak O–O bond (146 kJ/mol), while water contains only strong O–H bonds (467 kJ/mol).
This is why hydrogen peroxide slowly breaks down into water and oxygen in its brown bottle, while water is stable.

Breaking and Forming Bonds

Breaking and Forming Bonds

Every chemical reaction involves the same exchange of energy, and we can think of it as an energy budget. Bond breaking is the cost, and bond forming is the payment.

If more energy is needed to break the old bonds than is released forming the new bonds, there is a net absorption of energy, and the reaction is endothermic. If more energy is released forming the new bonds than is needed to break the old bonds, there is a net release of energy, and the reaction is exothermic.

Consider a gas stove. Each methane molecule that burns has its four C–H bonds broken, and each oxygen molecule has its O=O bond broken, which takes energy. The atoms then form new C=O bonds in carbon dioxide and O–H bonds in water, which releases energy. The new bonds release more energy than it took to break the old ones, and that surplus is the heat that cooks your dinner.

The stove also shows why exothermic reactions usually need a push to get started. Methane does not burst into flame the moment it mixes with air. It needs a spark. Some bonds in the reactants must be broken before any new bonds can form, so energy has to be put in first, and only then is the larger amount of energy released. Once the reaction is going, the heat it releases breaks the bonds in the next molecules, so the flame keeps itself going. This starting energy is referred to as the activation energy.

  • In a chemical reaction, energy must be added to break the bonds in the reactants. When new bonds form to create the products, energy is released.
    • If more energy is needed to break the old bonds than is released forming the new bonds, there is a net absorption of energy and the reaction is endothermic.
    • If more energy is released forming the new bonds than is needed to break the old bonds, there is a net release of energy and the reaction is exothermic.
Example
In N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g), energy is needed to break the N≡N and H–H bonds. When the new N–H bonds form, more energy is released than was absorbed, so the reaction is exothermic.
  • The heat of reaction can be estimated from these bond energies: ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed). See Calculating ΔH from Bond Energies below.

Enthalpy (Heat) of Reaction

Enthalpy

The total energy stored in a substance, its enthalpy H, cannot be measured directly. Only the change in enthalpy can be measured, so chemists always work with ΔH. The change for a reaction is known as the enthalpy (heat) of reaction (ΔH).

Enthalpy (Heat) of Reaction (ΔH) – the difference in energy between the products and the reactants in a chemical reaction at constant pressure. It is the energy absorbed or released during the reaction.

Since ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants, its sign tells us which way the energy went. In an endothermic reaction, the products have more energy than the reactants, so ΔH is positive, and the absorbed heat can be written as a reactant. Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water has a ΔH of +25.69 kJ for every mole that dissolves, so the solution feels cold.

In an exothermic reaction, the products end up with less energy than the reactants, so ΔH is negative, and the released heat can be written as a product. The burning of methane has a ΔH of −890.4 kJ:

CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(ℓ)    ΔH = −890.4 kJ

It can also be written with “+ 890.4 kJ” on the product side. Note that the negative sign appears only in ΔH. We never write a negative sign on a heat term in the equation itself.

Endothermic Reaction – reaction which absorbs energy from its surroundings.

Exothermic Reaction – reaction which releases energy to its surroundings.

The value of ΔH applies to the equation exactly as written, with its coefficients and phases. The −890.4 kJ above is the heat released when 1 mole of methane reacts with 2 moles of oxygen to produce liquid water. If the water were produced as a gas, less heat would be released, since some of the energy would remain in the water vapor instead of being released by condensation. When we report or use a value of ΔH, we always keep it attached to its balanced equation.

  • Every chemical reaction is accompanied by a change in energy due to the breaking and forming of bonds. We call the energy absorbed or released during a chemical reaction the enthalpy, or heat of reaction.
    • Enthalpy (Heat) of Reaction (ΔH) – the difference in energy between the products and the reactants in a chemical reaction at constant pressure. It is the energy absorbed or released during the reaction.
    • Endothermic Reaction – reaction which absorbs energy from its surroundings.
    • Exothermic Reaction – reaction which releases energy to its surroundings.
  • The enthalpy change is found by comparing the energy of the products with the energy of the reactants (see the box below).
  • If ΔH is positive (+), the reaction is endothermic and heat is a reactant.
  • If ΔH is negative (−), the reaction is exothermic and heat is a product.
    • The negative sign only appears in ΔH. We do not write a negative sign on the heat term in the equation itself.
  • The ΔH value applies to the equation exactly as written, with those coefficients and phases.

Calculating ΔH from Bond Energies

Chemists cannot always measure a heat of reaction in a calorimeter. Some reactions are too slow, some are too dangerous and some produce a mixture of products. In these cases, we can estimate ΔH from bond energies, using the same energy budget of bonds broken and bonds formed. Breaking bonds in the reactants absorbs energy, so those bond energies are added. Forming bonds in the products releases energy, so those bond energies are subtracted.

Since bond energies are given per mole of bonds, each bond energy must be multiplied by the number of moles of that bond in the balanced equation. The coefficient tells us how many molecules there are, and the structure of each molecule tells us how many of each bond it has. 2HCl contains 2 moles of H–Cl bonds, and CH4 contains 4 moles of C–H bonds. Drawing the Lewis structure of each molecule is the most reliable way to count bonds, especially when double or triple bonds are involved.

If more energy is absorbed breaking bonds than is released forming bonds, ΔH is positive. If more is released, ΔH is negative. The steps for calculating ΔH from bond energies are:

  1. Determine the type and number of bonds broken (all of the bonds in the reactants).
  2. Multiply the number of each bond broken by its bond energy, and add the results.
  3. Determine the type and number of bonds formed (all of the bonds in the products).
  4. Multiply the number of each bond formed by its bond energy, and add the results.
  5. Subtract: ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed).

Keep in mind that the answer is only an estimate. A bond energy is an average taken over many different molecules, and the C–H bond in methane is not exactly as strong as a C–H bond in ethanol. For the reaction 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(g), bond energies give about −509 kJ. The measured value is −484 kJ. Measured heats of reaction are more exact, and they are used whenever they are available.

  • The enthalpy (heat) of reaction is equal to the sum of the bond energies of the bonds broken minus the sum of the bond energies of the bonds formed.
    • Breaking bonds in the reactants absorbs energy, so these bond energies are added.
    • Forming bonds in the products releases energy, so these bond energies are subtracted.
  • Bond energies are given per mole of bonds, so multiply each bond energy by the number of moles of that bond in the balanced equation.
    • Ex: 2HCl contains 2 moles of H–Cl bonds. CH4 contains 4 moles of C–H bonds.
  • If more energy is absorbed breaking bonds than is released forming bonds, ΔH is positive and the reaction is endothermic. If more energy is released, ΔH is negative and the reaction is exothermic.

    4. Multiply the number of each bond formed by its bond energy, and add the results.

Selected Heats of Reaction

Measured heats of reaction allow engineers to compare fuels and predict how much fuel a job will need. Many homes are heated with natural gas, which is mostly methane. Homes in the country often burn propane, C3H8, from a tank. Burning 1 mole of propane, about 44 g, releases 2,219.2 kJ of heat. Burning 1 mole of methane releases only 890.4 kJ. Part of the reason is size. A propane molecule has more bonds to break and form, so more energy is released for each molecule that burns.

Notice that the combustion reactions in the table all have large negative values, which is the whole reason fuels are burned. The reaction of glucose with oxygen, which releases 2,804 kJ per mole, is the overall reaction your cells use to get energy from food.

Dissolving can also absorb or release energy. Ammonium nitrate dissolving in water has a positive ΔH and absorbs heat. Sodium hydroxide dissolving has a negative ΔH (−44.51 kJ/mol) and releases heat, so a concentrated solution of it can become hot enough to be dangerous.

Manipulating Reactions and Their Enthalpies

Manipulating Reactions and Their Enthalpies

When enthalpies of reaction are provided, they apply to the reaction exactly as it is written stoichiometrically and for the states of matter stated in the equation. For instance, consider the reaction:

H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g)    ΔH = −185 kJ

This means that when 1 mole of hydrogen gas combines with 1 mole of chlorine gas to form 2 moles of hydrogen chloride gas, 185 kJ of energy is released. If the reaction is altered in any way, this enthalpy will change.

Changing the stoichiometric coefficients in a chemical equation will cause the enthalpy to change proportionally. That is, multiplying or dividing the coefficients by a certain factor will cause the enthalpy of the reaction to be multiplied or divided by the same factor. As an example, if we take the reaction from before and double each of the amounts being reacted or produced, the enthalpy will be doubled as well:

2H2(g) + 2Cl2(g) → 4HCl(g)    ΔH = −370 kJ

When 2 moles of hydrogen gas combine with 2 moles of chlorine gas to produce 4 moles of hydrogen chloride gas, 370 kJ of energy is released instead. Since you are breaking and forming twice the number of bonds, the energy change will be twice as great.

Remember that a reaction can also be written in reverse, meaning that the reactants and products are swapped. When a reaction is reversed, the magnitude of the enthalpy stays the same but its sign will change. An exothermic reaction will become endothermic and vice versa. For example:

2HCl(g) → H2(g) + Cl2(g)    ΔH = +185 kJ

You can see how the enthalpy of the reaction is still 185 kJ, but now it is positive (+) rather than negative (−). This should make sense, as breaking the original bonds and forming the bonds of hydrogen chloride releases 185 kJ of energy, so breaking those same bonds and forming the original bonds again should absorb the same amount of energy. If it absorbed any less, we could form and break hydrogen chloride over and over and create energy from nothing, which the law of conservation of energy does not allow.

These two rules allow us to combine several reactions, reversing and multiplying them as needed, and add up their enthalpies. The steps to finding the ΔH of a changed equation are:

  • The enthalpy of a reaction applies to the reaction exactly as it is written. Changing the coefficients or reversing the reaction changes its enthalpy.
    • Multiplying or dividing – if all of the coefficients are multiplied or divided by a number, ΔH is multiplied or divided by the same number.
    • Reversing the reaction – if a reaction is reversed, the sign of ΔH is switched, but its size stays the same. An endothermic reaction becomes exothermic, and an exothermic reaction becomes endothermic.
  • Steps to finding the ΔH of a changed equation:
    1. Compare the new equation with the given equation. Was it reversed? Were the coefficients multiplied or divided?
    2. If the equation was reversed, switch the sign of ΔH.
    3. If the coefficients were multiplied or divided, multiply or divide ΔH by the same number.
    4. Write the new ΔH with units.
