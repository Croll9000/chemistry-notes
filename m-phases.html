Solids, Liquids and Gases

The phase of a substance is about how its particles are arranged and how they move.

Phases of Matter

Water is one of the few substances found naturally on Earth as a solid, a liquid and a gas: in glaciers, in oceans and as vapor in the atmosphere. Though these three forms look and behave very differently, they are all made of the same H2O molecules.

  • Phase of Matter – the form that matter can take.
    • A substance’s phase can be determined by looking at the arrangement of its particles.
  • There are 3 phases of matter: solid (s), liquid (l) and gas (g).

Part2

What Decides the Phase?

Three things decide the phase of a substance: attractions between particles, temperature, and pressure.

Determining Phase of Matter

At room temperature, oxygen is a gas, water is a liquid and iron is a solid. Every substance has a phase under a given set of conditions, so it is important to understand what determines it.

The phase of matter that a specific substance will take is dependent on 3 things:

  1. Intermolecular forces (attractions between particles)
  2. Temperature
  3. Pressure

Phases of Matter and Intermolecular Forces

Particles in a sample of matter are not completely independent of one another. They are attracted to the particles around them, and the strength of these attractions helps explain why some substances are solids at room temperature while others are gases.

  • Intermolecular Forces (of attraction) – attractive forces between particles in a sample of matter
    • Intermolecular forces arise due to small charges possessed by particles.
      • Opposite charges attract.
      • The slightly positive end of one particle is attracted to the slightly negative end of another.
      • The stronger the attraction, the harder the particles are to pull apart.
    • If a substance has weak intermolecular forces, its particles will be very spread out as in a gas.
    • If a substance has strong intermolecular forces, its particles will be tightly packed as in a solid.
  • Stronger attractions take more energy to overcome, so substances with stronger intermolecular forces have higher melting points and boiling points.
  • The types of intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces) are covered in detail in Unit 4: Intermolecular Forces of Attraction.

Temperature

  • Temperature – measurement of the average kinetic energy of particles in a sample
    • Unit – degrees Celsius (°C) and kelvin (K)
    • Measuring device – thermometer
    • Kinetic Energy – energy possessed by particles in motion.
    • The faster particles move, the more kinetic energy they have.
      • Particles at a high temperature move fast.
      • Particles at a low temperature move slowly.
  • Heat – a form of energy that flows from a hotter object to a colder one.
    • Unit – joules (J) and kilojoules (kJ)
    • Heat and temperature are not the same thing.
      • Heat is energy.
      • Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles.
    • Adding heat usually raises the temperature.
  • In chemistry we measure temperature in degrees Celsius (°C) or kelvin (K).
    • Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C.
    • The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero (0 K), the lowest possible temperature.
    • The Kelvin scale has no negative numbers.
  • Your Reference Table converts between them: K = °C + 273.15

Temperature

Phases of Matter and Temperature

  • Temperature affects phase.
    • As temperature increases, a substance changes from solid to liquid to gas.
    • As temperature decreases, a substance changes from gas to liquid to solid.
  • Temperature is a measurement of average kinetic energy.
    • Particles of a substance in the solid phase have low kinetic energy.
    • Particles of the same substance in the gas phase have high kinetic energy.

Phases of Matter and Pressure

Water does not always boil at 100 °C. In Denver, Colorado, about 1.6 km above sea level, water boils at about 95 °C because the air pressure is lower. Pressure cookers work the opposite way, raising the pressure so that water boils at a higher temperature and food cooks faster.

  • Pressure – the amount of force per unit area pushing down on a substance
    • Unit – atmospheres (atm) and kilopascals (kPa)
    • Measuring device – barometer (air pressure) or pressure gauge
  • The higher the pressure pushing down on a substance, the closer together the particles are pushed.
    • The substance is more likely to be in a condensed phase (liquid or solid).
  • The lower the pressure pushing down on a substance, the farther apart the particles will be.
    • The substance is more likely to be a gas.
  • Ex: the boiling point of water changes with pressure.
    • 100 °C at 1 atm
    • About 81 °C at 0.5 atm (high in the mountains)
    • About 120 °C at 2 atm (inside a pressure cooker)
Part3

Phase Changes

Adding or removing heat changes the phase. Some changes absorb energy; others release it.

Phase Changes

Frost forming on a windshield, dew collecting on grass and a puddle drying in the sun are all examples of matter changing from one phase to another. Each of these changes involves heat being added to or removed from a substance.

  • For a given substance at a given pressure, its phase can be changed by adding or removing heat from it.
    • Melting Point – the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It is the same temperature at which the liquid freezes.
    • Boiling Point – the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid. It is the same temperature at which the gas condenses.
  • While a substance is changing phase, its temperature stays the same.
    • The added (or removed) energy goes into pulling the particles apart (or letting them come together), not into changing how fast they move.
    • Ex: ice stays at 0 °C until it has all melted.
    • Ex: boiling water stays at 100 °C until it has all boiled away.
  • Comparing the temperature to a substance’s melting and boiling points tells its phase.
    • Below the melting point: solid
    • Between the melting point and the boiling point: liquid
    • Above the boiling point: gas

Endothermic vs. Exothermic

When sweat evaporates from your skin, it carries energy away and cools you down. A burn from steam is often more severe than one from boiling water because steam releases additional energy as it condenses on the skin. Every phase change involves energy moving into or out of a substance.

  • During phase changes energy is either absorbed or released.
    • Endothermic – a change in which energy is absorbed by a substance (melting, vaporization, sublimation)
    • Exothermic – a change in which energy is released by a substance (freezing, condensation, deposition)

Phase Change Equations

  • We can write phase changes as equations, just like reactions.
    • The substance on the left is the reactant (what you start with).
    • The substance on the right is the product (what you end with).
    • If heat is a reactant (left side), the change is endothermic
    • If heat is a product (right side), the change is exothermic
H2O(s) + heat → H2O(l)
A solid is changing into a liquid: melting (fusion). Since heat is on the reactants (left) side, it is endothermic.
NH3(g) → NH3(l) + heat
A gas is changing into a liquid: condensation. Since heat is on the products (right) side, it is exothermic.

Both of these are physical changes: the identity of the substance does not change.
