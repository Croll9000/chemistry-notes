Part1

Solids, Liquids and Gases

Phases of Matter

Water is one of the very few substances found naturally on Earth as a solid, a liquid and a gas all at once: ice in glaciers, liquid in the oceans and invisible water vapor in the air. A single water molecule is identical in all three. What differs is how the molecules are arranged and how they move.

Each of these forms is referred to as a phase of matter. There are 3 phases of matter: solid (s), liquid (l) and gas (g). We can determine a substance’s phase by looking at the arrangement of its particles.

Phase of Matter – the form that matter can take.

In a solid, the particles are packed tightly in fixed positions. They vibrate in place but cannot move past one another, so a solid keeps its own shape and volume.

In a liquid, the particles are still close together, but they can slide past one another. A liquid keeps its volume, but it takes the shape of its container.

In a gas, the particles are far apart and move freely in all directions. A gas has no shape or volume of its own and spreads out to fill whatever container it is in.

Since the particles of a gas are so far apart, a gas takes up far more space than the same mass of liquid. One gram of liquid water fills about 1 mL. As steam at 100 °C, the same gram fills about 1.7 L, more than a thousand times as much space. This is what made the steam engine possible. Water boiling inside a closed cylinder expands so forcefully that it can push a piston, and that push drove the trains, ships and factories of the 1800s.

  • Phase of Matter – the form that matter can take.
    • A substance’s phase can be determined by looking at the arrangement of its particles.
  • There are 3 phases of matter: solid (s), liquid (l) and gas (g).

Part2

What Decides the Phase?

Determining Phase of Matter

At room temperature, oxygen is a gas, water is a liquid and iron is a solid. Yet oxygen can be cooled until it becomes a pale blue liquid, and iron can be heated until it melts and pours like water. A substance does not have one permanent phase. Its phase depends on the conditions it is in.

You can think of the phase of a substance as the result of a tug of war. The attractions between particles pull them together, while the motion of the particles pushes them apart. Whichever side is winning decides whether the substance is a solid, a liquid or a gas, and the outcome depends on three factors:

  1. Intermolecular forces (attractions between particles)
  2. Temperature
  3. Pressure

Phases of Matter and Intermolecular Forces

Oxygen and water molecules are about the same size, yet at room temperature oxygen is a gas and water is a liquid. Oxygen has to be cooled to −183 °C before it condenses into a liquid. Water stays liquid all the way up to 100 °C. Since the molecules are about the same size, the difference must come from something else: how strongly the molecules of each substance attract one another.

These attractions are known as intermolecular forces (of attraction). They arise since particles carry small charges, and opposite charges attract. The slightly positive end of one particle is pulled toward the slightly negative end of another.

Intermolecular Forces (of attraction) – attractive forces between particles in a sample of matter

The strength of these forces decides how the particles are arranged. If a substance has weak intermolecular forces, its particles will be very spread out, as in a gas. If a substance has strong intermolecular forces, its particles will be tightly packed, as in a solid.

Stronger attractions take more energy to overcome, so it makes sense that substances with stronger intermolecular forces have higher melting points and boiling points. Water molecules attract one another far more strongly than oxygen molecules do. More energy must therefore be added to pull water molecules apart, and water boils at a much higher temperature.

Temperature

In everyday speech, “heat” and “temperature” are used as if they meant the same thing. In chemistry, they do not, and the difference starts with motion.

Every particle of matter is always moving, and the energy an object has due to its motion is called kinetic energy. The faster particles move, the more kinetic energy they have.

Kinetic Energy – energy possessed by an object due to its motion.

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample. It is measured with a thermometer, in degrees Celsius (°C) or kelvins (K). Particles at a high temperature move fast, and particles at a low temperature move slowly.

Temperature – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample.

Heat, on the other hand, is energy transferred from one object to another. It is measured in joules (J) or kilojoules (kJ), and it always flows from a hotter object to a colder one.

Heat – energy transferred from a high temperature object to a low temperature object.

A sparkler shows the difference clearly. Its sparks are hotter than 1000 °C, yet a spark that lands on your hand feels like a tiny pinprick and is gone. A pot of water at only 100 °C can cause a serious burn. The sparks have a far higher temperature, but each one is so small that it can transfer very little heat. The pot of water holds a great deal of energy that can flow into your skin.

Adding heat usually raises the temperature, since the energy makes the particles move faster. On the Celsius scale, water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero (0 K), the lowest possible temperature, where particles have the least possible motion. For this reason, the Kelvin scale has no negative numbers. A change of one kelvin is the same size as a change of one degree Celsius, so converting between them only requires adding 273.15:

K = °C + 273.15

Temperature

Phases of Matter and Temperature

A pot of snow placed on a stove melts into water, and if the stove is left on, the water eventually boils away into steam. In a freezer, the process runs the other way, and water vapor in the air becomes frost on the walls. The only thing being changed is the temperature, yet the substance moves through all three phases.

To see why, recall that temperature is a measurement of average kinetic energy. Particles of a substance in the solid phase have low kinetic energy, so the attractions between them hold them in fixed positions. As the solid is heated, its particles move faster and faster. Eventually they move fast enough to slide past one another, and the solid becomes a liquid. With even more heating, they move fast enough to escape their neighbors entirely, and the liquid becomes a gas. Thus, particles of the same substance in the gas phase have high kinetic energy.

Putting this together, as temperature increases, a substance changes from solid to liquid to gas. As temperature decreases, a substance changes from gas to liquid to solid.

Factories that make medical oxygen use this idea in reverse. Air is cooled to below −190 °C until it becomes a liquid. As the liquid air is slowly warmed, nitrogen boils off first, at −196 °C, and nearly pure oxygen is left behind to be bottled for hospitals.

  • Temperature affects phase.
    • As temperature increases, a substance changes from solid to liquid to gas.
    • As temperature decreases, a substance changes from gas to liquid to solid.
  • Temperature is a measurement of average kinetic energy.
    • Particles of a substance in the solid phase have low kinetic energy.
    • Particles of the same substance in the gas phase have high kinetic energy.

Phases of Matter and Pressure

Temperature is not the only condition that affects phase. The air above us presses down on everything at the Earth’s surface, and this push is an example of pressure. Pressure is measured in atmospheres (atm) or kilopascals (kPa), using a barometer for air pressure or a pressure gauge for a closed container.

Pressure – the amount of force per unit area pushing down on a substance

Pressure works against the motion of particles. The higher the pressure pushing down on a substance, the closer together the particles are pushed, so the substance is more likely to be in a condensed phase (liquid or solid). The lower the pressure pushing down on a substance, the farther apart the particles can be, so the substance is more likely to be a gas.

La Paz, Bolivia, sits about 3600 m above sea level, where the air pressure is only about two-thirds of the pressure at sea level. With less pressure holding them in, water molecules escape into the gas phase more easily, and water boils at about 88 °C. Pasta and beans take noticeably longer to cook there, since the boiling water is never as hot as it is at the coast.

Raising the pressure does the opposite. Hospitals sterilize surgical tools in an autoclave, a sealed steel chamber filled with steam. The steam is trapped, so the pressure inside rises to about twice normal air pressure. The higher pressure makes it harder for water molecules to escape, and the water stays liquid until about 121 °C. That temperature kills bacteria and their spores, which can survive ordinary boiling water.

  • Pressure – the amount of force per unit area pushing down on a substance
    • Unit – atmospheres (atm) and kilopascals (kPa)
    • Measuring device – barometer (air pressure) or pressure gauge
  • The higher the pressure pushing down on a substance, the closer together the particles are pushed.
    • The substance is more likely to be in a condensed phase (liquid or solid).
  • The lower the pressure pushing down on a substance, the farther apart the particles will be.
    • The substance is more likely to be a gas.
Example
The boiling point of water changes with pressure.

  • About 81 °C at 0.5 atm (high in the mountains)
  • About 120 °C at 2 atm (inside a pressure cooker)

Phase Changes

Phase Changes

Consider what happens when an ice cube is dropped into a glass of water and stirred. A thermometer in the glass reads 0 °C. As the ice melts, the thermometer still reads 0 °C, even though the glass is absorbing heat from the warm room the whole time. Only when the last bit of ice has melted does the temperature begin to rise. The energy being absorbed is clearly going somewhere, but it is not making the water any warmer.

While a substance is changing phase, its temperature stays the same. The added energy goes into pulling the particles apart, overcoming the attractions between them, rather than making them move faster. The same is true in reverse. When a liquid freezes, the energy it releases comes from the particles settling into place, not from the particles slowing down.

For a given substance at a given pressure, these changes always happen at the same temperatures. The temperature at which ice melts, 0 °C, is its melting point. The temperature at which water boils, 100 °C, is its boiling point.

Melting Point – the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It is the same temperature at which the liquid freezes.

Boiling Point – the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid. It is the same temperature at which the gas condenses.

Since these temperatures are fixed, we can compare the temperature of a sample with its melting and boiling points to tell its phase. Below the melting point, it is a solid. Between the melting point and the boiling point, it is a liquid. Above the boiling point, it is a gas.

  • For a given substance at a given pressure, its phase can be changed by adding or removing heat from it.
    • Melting Point – the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It is the same temperature at which the liquid freezes.
    • Boiling Point – the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid. It is the same temperature at which the gas condenses.
  • While a substance is changing phase, its temperature stays the same.
    • The added (or removed) energy goes into pulling the particles apart (or letting them come together), not into changing how fast they move.

Phase Forecast

Endothermic vs. Exothermic

Recall that the particles of a substance attract one another. To pull particles farther apart, against that attraction, energy has to be added. When particles come closer together, the attraction pulls them in, and energy is given off. Every phase change moves particles either apart or together, so every phase change either absorbs or releases energy.

Melting, vaporization (boiling or evaporation) and sublimation all move particles farther apart. They absorb energy, and a change in which a substance absorbs energy is endothermic.

Endothermic – a change in which energy is absorbed by a substance (melting, vaporization, sublimation)

Freezing, condensation and deposition all bring particles closer together. They release energy, and a change in which a substance releases energy is exothermic.

Exothermic – a change in which energy is released by a substance (freezing, condensation, deposition)

Since each change moves the same particles the same distance in opposite directions, each endothermic change has an exothermic change that is its exact reverse, with the same amount of energy. The energy absorbed when 1 g of ice melts is exactly the energy released when 1 g of water freezes.

Both directions are put to use. As sweat evaporates from your skin, it absorbs energy from your body and cools you down. On a cold night when frost is expected, Florida orange growers spray their trees with water. As the water freezes on the oranges, it releases energy, and that energy keeps the fruit itself at about 0 °C, warm enough to avoid serious damage.

  • During phase changes energy is either absorbed or released.
    • Endothermic – a change in which energy is absorbed by a substance (melting, vaporization, sublimation)
    • Exothermic – a change in which energy is released by a substance (freezing, condensation, deposition)

Phase Change Equations

A phase change can be written as an equation, just like a chemical reaction. The substance on the left is what we start with, and the substance on the right is what we end with. The phase symbols after each formula show what kind of change is happening. Since energy is part of every phase change, the word heat can be written into the equation as well, on whichever side matches the direction energy flows.

Melting ice absorbs energy, so heat must be put in, like a reactant:

H2O(s) + heat → H2O(l)

Thus, if heat is a reactant (left side), the change is endothermic. Freezing water releases energy, so heat comes out, like a product:

H2O(l) → H2O(s) + heat

Thus, if heat is a product (right side), the change is exothermic. Notice that in both equations the formula stays H2O. A phase change is a physical change, so the identity of the substance does not change, only its phase.

  • We can write phase changes as equations, just like reactions.
    • The substance on the left is the reactant (what you start with).
    • The substance on the right is the product (what you end with).
    • If heat is a reactant (left side), the change is endothermic
    • If heat is a product (right side), the change is exothermic
