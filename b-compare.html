Bonding Comparison

Bonding Comparison

Whether a substance is ionic, metallic, molecular or network, its properties depend on what must be pulled apart and what is free to move. This means that once we know the type of bonding in a substance, we can predict how it will behave before it is ever tested.

For instance, ice melts at 0 °C, table salt at 801 °C and iron at 1538 °C, while diamond does not melt until well above 3500 °C. Each of these melting points reflects what must be pulled apart to melt the solid:

  • Melting ice only overcomes the weak attractions between water molecules.
  • Melting salt breaks ionic bonds between ions.
  • Melting iron weakens the metallic bonds between iron ions and their sea of electrons.
  • Melting diamond breaks covalent bonds throughout the whole crystal.

Substances whose particles are held together by bonds throughout the whole solid (ionic, metallic and network) have high melting points. Molecular substances, which are held together only by intermolecular forces, have low melting points. In the same way, substances with charged particles that are free to move, such as metals and melted or dissolved ionic compounds, conduct electricity, while substances without them do not.

Choosing Materials

Choosing a Material

Every engineered object starts with a list of what it must do. The requirements a design must meet are called criteria. The limits on a design are referred to as constraints.

Criteria – the requirements a design must meet to succeed, such as “must conduct electricity” or “must stay solid at 1500 °C.”

Constraints – limits on a design, such as cost, safety, weight or the temperature at which a material can be shaped.

Since a material’s properties come from its particles and the forces between them, we can use the type of substance to predict which materials will work before any testing begins. Choosing a material means:

  • listing the criteria and constraints
  • identifying the property each criterion requires
  • deciding which type of substance has those properties
  • choosing the material that meets every criterion and constraint, and supporting the choice with property data

A material that meets most of the criteria but fails one is not a good choice, since a single failure can make the whole design fail.

The tiles on the underside of NASA’s Space Shuttle show this reasoning. They had to protect the shuttle from temperatures above 1200 °C as it plunged back through the atmosphere, and they had to be light enough to launch. Engineers chose silica fibers, a network solid that does not melt at those temperatures and conducts heat very poorly. A metal could have survived the heat, but it would have failed one criterion: it would have conducted the heat straight into the shuttle.

  • Criteria – the requirements a design must meet to succeed, such as “must conduct electricity” or “must stay solid at 1500 °C.”
  • Constraints – limits on a design, such as cost, safety, weight or the temperature at which a material can be shaped.
  • To choose a material:
    1. List the criteria and constraints.
    2. Identify the property each criterion requires (melting point, conductivity, hardness, malleability or solubility).
    3. Decide which type of substance (ionic, metallic, molecular or network) has those properties.
    4. Choose the material that meets every criterion and constraint, and support the choice with property data.
Example
A wire must conduct electricity as a solid and bend without breaking. Only metallic substances conduct as solids and are malleable, so copper (metallic) meets both criteria. Sodium chloride does not conduct as a solid and is brittle.
