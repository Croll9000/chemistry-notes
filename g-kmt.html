Kinetic Molecular Theory

Kinetic Molecular Theory

Gases as different as the helium in a balloon and the carbon dioxide in a soda follow the same gas laws. This suggests that the behavior of a gas depends mostly on the motion of its particles and very little on what the particles are. Scientists describe this behavior with a model called the kinetic molecular theory (KMT).

Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) – a model describing all matter as composed of tiny particles that are always in motion.

For gases, the kinetic molecular theory makes five assumptions:

  1. The particles are very far apart compared with their own size, so the volume of the particles themselves is negligible.
  2. The particles move in constant, random, straight-line motion.
  3. Collisions between particles are perfectly elastic, meaning kinetic energy can be passed from one particle to another, but none is lost.
  4. There are no intermolecular forces between the particles.
  5. The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the Kelvin temperature.

The first assumption is closer to the truth than it might seem. At 0 °C and 1 atm, a single cubic centimeter of air contains about 2.7 × 1019 molecules, yet the molecules themselves fill less than 0.1% of that space.

These assumptions explain the properties of gases. The particles are far apart, so a gas can be compressed. They move constantly and randomly, so a gas fills its container and mixes completely with other gases. They collide with the walls of the container, so a gas exerts pressure.

  • Scientists describe the behavior of gases with a set of principles known as the kinetic molecular theory. These assumptions describe the way gas particles would behave under perfect (ideal) conditions.
  • Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) – a model describing all matter as composed of tiny particles that are always in motion.
  • The kinetic molecular theory makes five assumptions about gases:
    1. The distance between gas particles is very large compared to the size of a single particle. Gas particle size is negligible.
    2. Gas particles move in constant, random, straight-line motion.
    3. Collisions between gas particles are perfectly elastic: no kinetic energy is lost in collisions. Momentum can be transferred, but the total kinetic energy stays the same.
    4. There are no intermolecular forces between gas particles.
    5. The average kinetic energy of a gas is proportional to its Kelvin temperature.
  • The postulates of the kinetic molecular theory explain the properties of gases:
    • Gas particles are far apart and have negligible volume, so gases can be compressed.
    • Gas particles are in constant, random motion, so gases fill their containers and mix completely.
    • Gas particles collide with the walls of their container, so gases exert pressure.

Ideal and Real Gases

Ideal Gases and Real Gases

The kinetic molecular theory describes how gas particles would behave under perfect conditions. A gas that follows all of its assumptions is called an ideal gas. Its particles are treated as points with no size. They feel no intermolecular forces, they move in straight lines and they collide without losing kinetic energy.

Ideal Gas – a theoretical gas made up of a large number of randomly moving particles that are not subject to intermolecular forces.

However, no gas in the real world meets every one of these conditions. Real gas particles do take up some space, and they do attract one another a little. We call these real gases.

Real Gas – a non-ideal gas whose particles occupy space and exert intermolecular forces on one another.

The carbon dioxide inside a fire extinguisher shows how large the difference can become. At room temperature, it is stored under a pressure of about 57 atm. That pressure pushes the molecules so close together that their attractions hold them in the liquid phase, something an ideal gas could never do. Under most everyday conditions, however, these effects are so small that the ideal model describes real gases very well.

  • Under real conditions, the behavior of gases differs from the behavior predicted by the kinetic molecular theory. Gases that behave according to its principles are called ideal gases.
  • Ideal Gas – a theoretical gas made up of a large number of randomly moving particles that are not subject to intermolecular forces.
    • Ideal gas particles have negligible size: they are treated as “point particles.”
    • Ideal gas particles experience no intermolecular forces.
    • Ideal gas particles undergo perfectly elastic collisions in which no kinetic energy is lost.
    • Ideal gas particles move in constant, random, straight-line motion.
  • Real Gas – a non-ideal gas whose particles occupy space and exert intermolecular forces on one another.

Example
At 25 °C and 1 atm, 1 mol of an ideal gas occupies 24.5 L. One mole of carbon dioxide occupies slightly less, about 24.3 L, because its molecules attract one another and pull slightly closer together.

Conditions for Ideal Gases

Conditions That Favor Ideal Behavior

Recall that real gases differ from an ideal gas in two ways: their particles take up space, and they attract one another. Any condition that makes these two effects less important makes a real gas behave more like an ideal gas.

At high temperature, the particles have a high kinetic energy and move so quickly that they have little time to interact as they pass one another. At low pressure, the particles are spread far apart, so their own size is negligible compared with the volume of the container, and the attractions between them are weak. Gases made of small, nonpolar particles, such as helium and hydrogen, have the weakest intermolecular forces of all and behave most like ideal gases.

Under the opposite conditions, low temperature and high pressure, the particles are slow and crowded together. This is where a real gas behaves least like an ideal gas, and where it can condense into a liquid. Helium in a weather balloon high in the thin upper atmosphere behaves almost exactly like an ideal gas. The propane in a grill tank is squeezed so hard that much of it condenses into a liquid.

  • Depending on the conditions of the environment, gas particles can behave more or less like ideal gases.
  • Conditions that favor ideal gas behavior:
    • High temperature – particles with high kinetic energy move fast and have little time to interact with each other.
    • Low pressure – the particles spread far apart, so their size is negligible compared to the volume of their container and the intermolecular forces between them are weaker.
    • Small, nonpolar particles – small atoms and molecules such as helium (He) and hydrogen (H2) have the weakest intermolecular forces, so they behave most like ideal gases.
  • High temperatures and low pressures favor ideal gas behavior because the effects of intermolecular forces and particle size are minimized.
  • Real gases behave least like ideal gases at low temperature and high pressure, where the particles are slow and crowded together. Under these conditions, a real gas can condense into a liquid.
Example
Of H2, CO2, HCl and Xe at STP, H2 behaves most like an ideal gas. Its molecules are the smallest and are nonpolar, so its intermolecular forces are the weakest.
