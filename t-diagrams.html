Phase Diagrams

Phase Diagrams

Mountaineers on the summit of Mount Everest cannot make a proper cup of tea. At that altitude the air pressure is about one-third of the pressure at sea level, so water boils at only about 70 °C. Changing either the temperature or the pressure around a substance can change its phase. A phase diagram shows the conditions at which a substance exists as a solid, a liquid or a gas, with temperature on the x-axis and pressure on the y-axis, so it can be read like a map. At high temperatures and low pressures, substances exist as gases, and at low temperatures and high pressures, they exist as solids.

The lines on a phase diagram separate the phases. When the temperature and pressure fall on a line, the substance is in phase equilibrium, and the two phases on either side of the line exist together. When a change in temperature or pressure crosses a line, a phase change occurs. The normal melting point and normal boiling point are read where the lines cross a pressure of 1 atm. Two points have special names. The triple point is the temperature and pressure at which a substance exists in all three phases at the same time. The critical point is the temperature and pressure above which the liquid and gas phases can no longer be told apart. A substance above both its critical temperature and critical pressure is a supercritical fluid, which is dense like a liquid but fills its container like a gas.

  • A phase diagram shows the conditions at which a substance exists as a solid, a liquid or a gas.
    • x-axis: temperature. y-axis: pressure.
    • At high temperatures and low pressures, substances exist as gases.
    • At low temperatures and high pressures, substances exist as solids.
  • When the temperature and pressure fall on a line, the substance is in phase equilibrium: the two phases on either side of the line exist together.
  • When a change in temperature or pressure crosses a line, a phase change occurs.
  • Triple Point – the temperature and pressure at which a substance exists in all three phases at the same time.
  • Critical Point – the temperature and pressure above which the liquid and gas phases become indistinguishable.
    • Supercritical Fluid – a substance above its critical temperature and critical pressure. It is dense like a liquid but fills its container like a gas.
    Supercritical carbon dioxide (above 31 °C and 73 atm) is used to remove caffeine from coffee beans.
  • The normal melting point and normal boiling point are read where the solid–liquid line and the liquid–gas line cross the pressure of 1 atm.

Phase Changes of Water and Pressure

Phase Changes of Water and Pressure

In winter, lakes in upstate New York freeze from the top down, and the layer of ice insulates the water below so that fish survive until spring. This happens because ice is less dense than liquid water, which is unusual; for almost every other substance, the solid sinks in its own liquid. For most substances, the solid–liquid line on a phase diagram leans to the right, because raising the pressure raises the melting point. Water is an exception. Since ice is less dense than liquid water, increasing the pressure favors the liquid, where the molecules are closer together. Water’s solid–liquid line leans to the left, which means that higher pressure lowers the melting point of ice. At the base of a glacier 1 km thick, the weight of the ice lowers its melting point by about 0.7 °C.

At 1 atm, water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. Its triple point is 0.01 °C at 0.006 atm, and its critical point is 374 °C at 218 atm. Carbon dioxide is different: its triple point is at 5.1 atm, above normal air pressure, so at 1 atm solid carbon dioxide (dry ice) cannot melt. Instead it sublimes directly into a gas at −78 °C.
