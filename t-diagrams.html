Phase Diagrams

A phase diagram maps the phase of a substance at every temperature and pressure. Lines mark phase equilibrium; crossing a line means a phase change.

Part1

Phase Diagrams

Liquid carbon dioxide cannot exist at normal air pressure: below 5.1 atm, solid carbon dioxide turns directly into a gas. Whether a substance is a solid, liquid or gas depends on both its temperature and its pressure, and a phase diagram maps the phase at every combination of the two. In this section, we will read phase diagrams, including their triple points and critical points.

Phase Diagrams

Mountaineers on the summit of Mount Everest cannot make a proper cup of tea. At that altitude the air pressure is about one-third of the pressure at sea level, so water boils at only about 70 °C. Changing either the temperature or the pressure around a substance can change its phase. A phase diagram puts both on one graph, like a map, so you can read the phase of a substance under any set of conditions.

  • A phase diagram shows the conditions at which a substance exists as a solid, a liquid or a gas.
    • x-axis: temperature. y-axis: pressure.
    • At high temperatures and low pressures, substances exist as gases.
    • At low temperatures and high pressures, substances exist as solids.
  • When the temperature and pressure fall on a line, the substance is in phase equilibrium: the two phases on either side of the line exist together.
  • When a change in temperature or pressure crosses a line, a phase change occurs.
  • Triple Point – the temperature and pressure at which a substance exists in all three phases at the same time.
  • Critical Point – the temperature and pressure above which the liquid and gas phases become indistinguishable.
    • Supercritical Fluid – a substance above its critical temperature and critical pressure. It is dense like a liquid but fills its container like a gas.
    Example
    Supercritical carbon dioxide (above 31 °C and 73 atm) is used to remove caffeine from coffee beans.
  • The normal melting point and normal boiling point are read where the solid–liquid line and the liquid–gas line cross the pressure of 1 atm.
Part2

Phase Changes of Water and Pressure

At the base of a glacier 1 km thick, the weight of the ice lowers the melting point of ice by about 0.7 °C. Because ice is less dense than liquid water, increasing the pressure on ice favors the denser liquid phase, so the solid–liquid line on water’s phase diagram tilts to the left. In this section, we will discuss why water is an exception to the pattern followed by most substances.

Phase Changes of Water and Pressure

In winter, lakes in upstate New York freeze from the top down, and the layer of ice insulates the liquid water below so that fish survive until spring. This happens because ice is less dense than liquid water, which is unusual: for almost every other substance, the solid sinks in its own liquid. This unusual property appears on water’s phase diagram as a solid–liquid line that leans the opposite way.

  • For most substances, the solid–liquid line leans to the right: raising the pressure raises the melting point, so high pressure favors the solid.
  • Water is an exception. Ice is less dense than liquid water, so increasing the pressure favors the liquid, where the molecules are closer together. Water’s solid–liquid line leans to the left: higher pressure lowers the melting point of ice.
  • Water’s triple point is 0.01 °C at 0.006 atm. Its critical point is 374 °C at 218 atm.
  • At 1 atm, water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C.
  • Carbon dioxide is different: its triple point is at 5.1 atm, above normal air pressure.
    • At 1 atm, solid CO2 (dry ice) cannot melt.
    • It sublimes directly into a gas at −78 °C.
