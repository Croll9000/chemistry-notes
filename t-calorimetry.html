Calorimetry

We cannot measure the energy of a reaction directly, but we can measure the temperature change of the water around it. In a calorimeter, the heat released or absorbed by a reaction equals the heat gained or lost by the water.

Calorimetry

How do you measure heat?

Heat itself cannot be poured into a beaker or placed on a balance. However, we can measure its effect on a substance we understand very well. In 1782, Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace placed a live guinea pig inside a container packed with ice and collected the water that dripped out over the next ten hours. Heat from the animal’s body melted the ice. Every gram of ice needs the same amount of heat to melt, so the mass of water told them how much heat the guinea pig had given off. They concluded that breathing works like a slow, flameless burning of food. Measuring heat by its effect on another substance is called calorimetry.

Calorimetry – process of measuring the heat absorbed or released during a chemical reaction or physical change.

Since the energy stored in a sample of matter cannot be measured directly, we measure energy changes indirectly, through changes in temperature. The change is carried out inside a calorimeter, next to a substance whose specific heat capacity is known, usually water. Water warms by a known amount for every joule it absorbs, so a thermometer placed in the water works as an energy meter. A simple calorimeter can be made from two nested foam cups with a lid, a thermometer and a stirrer.

Calorimeter – an insulated device used to measure the heat absorbed or released during a chemical reaction or physical change.

Every calorimetry experiment rests on one assumption: all of the heat absorbed or released by the reaction is transferred to or from the water, and none escapes to the room. That is why the calorimeter is insulated and has a lid.

Consider a reaction between substances dissolved in the water of a foam-cup calorimeter. The dissolved substances are the system, and the water around them is the surroundings, so the temperature change we measure is the temperature change of the surroundings. If the water’s temperature rises, the water gained heat, which means the reaction released heat and is exothermic. If the water’s temperature falls, the water lost heat, so the reaction absorbed heat and is endothermic.

We calculate the heat gained or lost by the water with q = mcΔT, using the mass, specific heat capacity and temperature change of the water. The heat of the reaction is equal in size but opposite in sign to the heat of the water, since whatever the water gained, the reaction lost:

When 2.5 g of a snack is burned under a can holding 200 g of water, for instance, the water warms by about 15 °C. The water absorbed (200 g)(4.18 J/g·K)(15 K) ≈ 12,500 J, so the burning snack released at least that much heat. The steps for solving calorimetry problems are:

  1. Identify which equation to use: q = mcΔT for the water.
  2. List the variables and the unknown, with units. The mass and ΔT are those of the water.
  3. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation.
  4. Solve for qwater algebraically.
  5. Write the final answer with units, then use qreaction = −qwater to decide whether the reaction is endothermic or exothermic.

Food Calories and the Bomb Calorimeter

How does a food company know that a granola bar has 200 Calories?

In the 1890s, the American chemist Wilbur Atwater burned thousands of food samples in sealed steel containers to find out how much energy each one held. He found that a gram of protein or carbohydrate provides about 4 Calories and a gram of fat about 9. Today the Calories on most nutrition labels are calculated from these numbers and the amount of protein, carbohydrate and fat in the food. The numbers themselves came from burning food in a bomb calorimeter.

A bomb calorimeter is a sealed, heavy steel container, the “bomb,” that sits in a measured amount of water inside an insulated jacket. A weighed sample of food is placed inside the bomb, the bomb is filled with pure oxygen and sealed, and an electric spark ignites the sample. The food burns completely, and the heat released flows through the steel walls into the surrounding water. The rest of the experiment works just like a foam-cup calorimeter. The temperature rise of the water is used to calculate the heat released.

Consider 1.00 g of peanut butter burned in a bomb calorimeter that holds 2000. g of water. If the water warms by 2.95 °C, it absorbed:

q = (2000. g)(4.18 J/g·K)(2.95 K) = 24,700 J

Food energy is reported in food Calories, where 1 Calorie = 1 kcal = 4184 J, so the peanut butter released 24,700 J × 1 Calorie4184 J = 5.90 Calories per gram. A 32 g serving, about two tablespoons, therefore holds roughly 190 Calories.

  • A bomb calorimeter is a sealed, heavy steel container (the “bomb”) that sits in a measured amount of water inside an insulated jacket.
    • The food is ignited with an electric spark, and the heat released by combustion flows into the water.
    • The temperature rise of the water is used to calculate the heat released, just like in a foam-cup calorimeter.
  • The result is reported in food Calories: 1 Calorie = 1 kcal = 4184 J (see the Energy page).

Calorimetry and Phase Changes

Calorimetry and Phase Changes

Why does ice cool a drink so well?

When 20 g of ice is dropped into 200 g of water at 30 °C, the ice melts completely and the water cools to about 20 °C. While it melts, the ice stays at 0 °C, so it is clearly absorbing energy without getting any warmer. That energy goes into separating the water molecules in the ice, and it comes from the warm water around it.

Since the heat the warm water loses is exactly the heat the ice gains, a calorimeter can be used to measure the heat of fusion (Hf) or heat of vaporization (Hv) of a substance. The heat absorbed by the melting substance equals the heat lost by the water: qice = −qwater. Since the heat needed to melt the ice is qice = mHf, the two equations can be combined:

The experiment must be planned so that the melted ice does not warm up, or some of the heat would go into raising its temperature instead of melting it. Ice is added to warm water, and the water is stirred until it cools exactly to 0 °C. At that point the melted ice is also at 0 °C, so all of the heat lost by the water went into melting ice. The leftover ice is then removed, and the mass of ice that melted is found by weighing: the mass of the water at the end minus the mass of the warm water at the start. With the mass of the water, its temperature change and the mass of ice that melted, we can calculate the heat of fusion.

  • The heat of fusion (Hf) or heat of vaporization (Hv) of a substance can be measured with calorimetry:
    • The heat absorbed by the melting substance equals the heat lost by the water.
  • For ice: if the warm water is cooled exactly to 0 °C, the melted ice stays at 0 °C, so all of the heat lost by the water went into melting the ice. The heat absorbed by the ice is qice = −qwater, and qice = mice meltedHf.
