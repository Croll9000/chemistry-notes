Calorimetry

We cannot measure the energy of a reaction directly, but we can measure the temperature change of the water around it. In a calorimeter, the heat released or absorbed by a reaction equals the heat gained or lost by the water.

Calorimetry

The energy contained in a sample of matter cannot be measured directly. Instead, chemists measure energy changes indirectly through the temperature change of a known mass of water.

Calorimetry

Heat cannot be poured into a beaker and weighed, so how do chemists measure it? They let it flow into something they understand very well: water. Water warms by a known amount for every joule it absorbs, so a thermometer in water becomes an energy meter. The Calories on a food label were measured this way, by burning the food inside a sealed steel container surrounded by water and recording how much the water warmed up.

  • The energy in a sample of matter cannot be measured directly. We can only measure energy changes indirectly, through changes in temperature.
  • In calorimetry, a reaction or change is carried out inside an insulated container alongside a substance whose specific heat capacity is known (usually water).
    • By measuring the temperature change of the water, we can calculate the heat absorbed or released by the reaction.
    • Calorimetry – process of measuring the heat absorbed or released during a chemical reaction or physical change.
    • Calorimeter – an insulated device used to measure the heat absorbed or released during a chemical reaction or physical change.
  • Calorimetry assumes that all of the heat absorbed or released by the reaction is transferred to (or from) the water, and none escapes to the surroundings. That is why the calorimeter is insulated and has a lid.
  • The heat of the reaction is equal in size and opposite in sign to the heat of the water (shown in the box below).
    • If the water’s temperature rises, the water gained heat, so the reaction released heat: it is exothermic.
    • If the water’s temperature falls, the water lost heat, so the reaction absorbed heat: it is endothermic.
qreaction=−qwater=−mwatercwaterΔTwater
q – heat (J)mwater – mass of the water (g)cwater – specific heat capacity of water (4.18 J/g·K)ΔTwater – change in temperature of the water (K or °C)

Food Calories and the Bomb Calorimeter

How does a food company know a granola bar has 200 Calories? A sample of the food is burned completely in pure oxygen inside a sealed steel container, and the heat released warms the water around it.

  • A bomb calorimeter is a sealed, heavy steel container (the “bomb”) that sits in a measured amount of water inside an insulated jacket.
    • The food is ignited with an electric spark, and the heat released by combustion flows into the water.
    • The temperature rise of the water is used to calculate the heat released, just like in a foam-cup calorimeter.
  • The result is reported in food Calories: 1 Calorie = 1 kcal = 4184 J (see the Energy page).

Calorimetry and Phase Changes

The same technique can be used to determine the heat required for a phase change, such as the melting of ice.

Calorimetry and Phase Changes

Drop ice into a cup of warm water and the water cools. The ice does not get colder or warmer while it melts, yet it is clearly absorbing energy. The heat the water loses is exactly the heat the ice uses to melt, so a calorimeter can measure a heat of fusion.

  • The heat of fusion (Hf) or heat of vaporization (Hv) of a substance can be measured with calorimetry:
    • The heat absorbed by the melting substance equals the heat lost by the water.
  • For ice: if the warm water is cooled exactly to 0 °C, the melted ice stays at 0 °C, so all of the heat lost by the water went into melting the ice. Then Hf = −qwater ÷ mice melted.
  • The mass of ice that melted is found by weighing: (mass of water after the leftover ice is removed) − (mass of the warm water at the start).
mxHf=−mwatercwaterΔTwater
mx – mass of the substance that changes phase (g)Hf – heat of fusion (J/g)mwater – mass of the water (g)ΔTwater – change in temperature of the water
