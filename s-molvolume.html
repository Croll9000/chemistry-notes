Avogadro’s Law

Avogadro’s Law

Gases are hard to weigh: a balloon full of helium seems to have no mass at all. Fortunately, gases follow a pattern that solids and liquids do not. A balloon filled with 1 mole of helium and a balloon filled with 1 mole of carbon dioxide are the same size at the same temperature and pressure, even though the carbon dioxide is 11 times heavier. The particles of a gas are tiny compared with the empty space between them, so the volume of a gas depends on the number of particles, not on what the particles are. This idea is known as Avogadro’s law: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of particles. It means that the volume of a gas can be used to count its particles.

  • Since all gas particles are treated as identical to one another under the kinetic molecular theory, if 2 containers at the same pressure and temperature have the same volume, they will contain an equal number of particles. This is known as Avogadro’s Law.
    • Avogadro’s Law – equal volumes of gas (at the same pressure and temperature) must contain the same number of moles/molecules.

Molar Volume

Molar Volume

At STP, 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L, about the volume of eleven 2-liter soda bottles. Under the kinetic molecular theory, all particles of a gas are treated as identical to one another, regardless of their identity, so if two samples of gas are at the same temperature and pressure, equal numbers of moles take up the same volume. It is often helpful to know the volume a gas takes up at standard temperature and pressure (STP: 273 K and 1 atm). This value is called the molar volume of an ideal gas at STP, and it is 22.4 liters. 1 mole of an ideal gas takes up 22.4 L at STP, and the Reference Table lists this equivalence as 1 mole = 22.4 L.

  • Under the kinetic molecular theory, all particles of a gas are treated as identical to one another, regardless of their identity. If any two samples of gas exist under the same temperature and pressure, equal numbers of moles take up the same volume of space.
  • It is often helpful to know the volume that a gas will take up at standard temperature and pressure (STP: 273 K and 1 atm). This is known as the molar volume at STP.

    • 1 mole of an ideal gas will take up 22.4 liters of volume at STP.

Mole–Volume Conversions

Mole–Volume Conversions

A party balloon about 30 cm across holds roughly 14 L of helium, or about 0.63 mole at STP. Many reactions produce or use gases, from the oxygen we breathe to the carbon dioxide that makes bread dough rise, and converting between moles and volume lets chemists predict how much space a gas will fill. To convert from moles to volume at STP, we multiply by 22.4 L, and to convert from volume at STP to moles, we divide by 22.4 L. The steps for converting between liters and moles at STP are:
