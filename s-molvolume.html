Part1

Avogadro’s Law

A balloon filled with 1 mole of helium and a balloon filled with 1 mole of carbon dioxide are the same size at the same temperature and pressure, even though the carbon dioxide is 11 times heavier. The volume of a gas depends on the number of particles, not on their identity, because the particles are tiny compared with the empty space between them. In this section, we will discuss Avogadro’s law.

Avogadro’s Law

Gases are hard to weigh: a balloon full of helium seems to have no mass at all. Fortunately, gases follow a remarkable pattern that solids and liquids do not. The space a gas takes up depends on how many particles it has, not on what the particles are. This means the volume of a gas can be used to count its particles.

  • Since all gas particles are treated as identical to one another under the kinetic molecular theory, if 2 containers at the same pressure and temperature have the same volume, they will contain an equal number of particles. This is known as Avogadro’s Law.
    • Avogadro’s Law – equal volumes of gas (at the same pressure and temperature) must contain the same number of moles/molecules.
Part2

Molar Volume

At STP, 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L, about the volume of eleven 2-liter soda bottles. This volume is the same for every gas because equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of particles. In this section, we will use the molar volume, 22.4 L/mol at STP, as a conversion factor.

Molar Volume

Engineers who design airbags and scuba tanks need to know how much space a gas will take up. Because every gas follows Avogadro’s law, one number describes the volume of a mole of any gas at standard conditions.

  • Under the kinetic molecular theory, all particles of a gas are treated as identical to one another, regardless of their identity. If any two samples of gas exist under the same temperature and pressure, equal numbers of moles take up the same volume of space.
  • It is often helpful to know the volume that a gas will take up at standard temperature and pressure (STP: 273 K and 1 atm). This is known as the molar volume at STP.
    • Molar Volume of an Ideal Gas (at STP) – 22.4 liters
    • 1 mole of an ideal gas will take up 22.4 liters of volume at STP.
    • The Reference Table lists this equivalence: 1 mole = 22.4 L (ideal gas at STP).
Part3

Mole–Volume Conversions

A party balloon about 30 cm across holds roughly 14 L of helium, or about 0.63 mole at STP. Dividing the volume of a gas by the molar volume, 22.4 L/mol, converts it to moles. In this section, we will convert between the moles and the volume of a gas.

Mole–Volume Conversions

Many reactions produce or use gases, from the oxygen we breathe to the carbon dioxide that inflates bread dough. Converting between moles and volume lets chemists predict how much space a gas will fill.

  • To convert from moles to volume (at STP), multiply by 22.4 L.
  • To convert from volume (at STP) to moles, divide by 22.4 L.

Use 22.4 L as the conversion factor.
