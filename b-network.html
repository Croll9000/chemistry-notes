Molecular & Network Solids

Some covalent substances are made of separate molecules. Others are one giant web of covalent bonds. The difference explains why dry ice turns to gas at −78 °C while quartz stays solid past 1700 °C.

Molecular Covalent Substances

Separate molecules, weakly attracted to each other.

Molecular Covalent Substances

Most covalent substances you meet every day, like water, sugar, carbon dioxide and oxygen, are made of separate molecules. Each molecule is held together by strong covalent bonds, but the molecules are only weakly attracted to each other.

  • Molecular Covalent Substance – a substance made of small, individual units (molecules) of covalently bonded atoms.
    • Strong attractions (covalent bonds) between the atoms inside each molecule.
    • Weak attractions (intermolecular forces) between the molecules.
  • Properties:
    • Low melting and boiling points – melting or boiling only pulls whole molecules apart from each other. The covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break.
    • Many are gases or liquids at room temperature (O2, CO2, H2O). Those that are solids are usually soft (sugar, ice, iodine).
    • Poor conductors of electricity in every phase – the particles are neutral molecules, so there are no charged particles free to move.
    • Solubility in water varies: some dissolve (sugar), others do not dissolve well (iodine, oil).

Network Solids

Every atom bonded to its neighbors.

Network Solids

Diamond is the hardest natural material and is used to cut stone and glass, yet it is made entirely of carbon, the same element found in the soft graphite of a pencil. The difference lies in how far the covalent bonds extend.

  • While some covalent molecules are small individual units, atoms can also covalently bond to form large, interconnected systems. These are referred to as network solids.
  • Network Solid – large, 3-dimensional, covalently bonded system of atoms.
  • Properties:
    • Hard (compared to molecular substances) – due to strong covalent bonds within the lattice
    • High melting points – due to large numbers of bonds to break
    • Insoluble – difficult for water to penetrate the matrix
    • Usually poor conductors of electricity (graphite is the exception)
  • Examples: diamond (C), graphite (C), quartz (SiO2), silicon carbide (SiC)
Graphite and diamond are examples of network solids. Note how each consists of a large matrix of covalently bonded carbon atoms.

Graphite: The Exception

  • In graphite, each carbon atom is bonded to only 3 others, forming flat sheets of hexagons. The sheets are stacked on top of each other and held together only by weak attractions.
    • The sheets slide past each other easily, so graphite is soft and slippery. This is why it is used as pencil “lead.”
    • Each carbon has one valence electron that is not in a bond. These electrons can move along the sheets, so graphite conducts electricity.
  • In diamond, each carbon atom is bonded to 4 others in every direction. Diamond is the hardest natural substance and does not conduct electricity.

Molecular vs. Network Covalent Substances

Where are the weak attractions?

Molecular vs. Network Covalent Substances

  • Molecular Covalent Substances – small, individual groupings of covalently bonded atoms. Atoms within individual units (molecules) are held together tightly, but molecules have weak attractions for each other.
    • Strong attractions (covalent bonds) between atoms inside of molecules.
    • Weak attractions between molecules.
    • Low melting/boiling points.
  • Network Covalent Substances – large covalently bonded systems of atoms. All atoms are connected together via strong covalent bonds.
    • Strong attractions (covalent bonds) between all atoms in the network.
    • High melting/boiling points.
Note how molecular covalent substances exist as individual units known as “molecules.” Network covalent substances consist of all atoms covalently bonded together in a large repeating pattern.

Compare the two kinds of covalent substances

Cause: melting a molecular substance only pulls whole molecules away from each other. The covalent bonds inside each molecule stay intact. Melting a network solid means breaking covalent bonds, which takes much more energy.
