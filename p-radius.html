Part1

Measuring an Atom

Atomic Radius

Chemists often find it useful to know the size of an atom. Size decides how close another atom can get to the nucleus, and so it affects how strongly an atom holds its own electrons and how strongly it pulls on the electrons of others. The size of an atom is described by its atomic radius.

Measuring it is not as simple as it sounds. An atom has no hard outer edge, since its electrons form a fuzzy cloud. Instead, chemists measure the distance between the nuclei of two neighboring atoms that are bonded together and take half of that distance as the radius of each atom.

Atomic Radius – the distance from the nucleus to the outermost energy level in an atom.

Unit – picometers (pm)

Atoms are so small that a special unit is needed. One picometer (pm) is 10−12 m, one trillionth of a meter. About 350,000 gold atoms lined up side by side would be as thick as a sheet of paper. Even so, atoms are not all the same size. A cesium atom (265 pm) is roughly seven times as wide as a helium atom (37 pm).

  • Atomic Radius – the distance from the nucleus to the outermost energy level in an atom.
    • Unit – picometers (pm)
    • 1 pm = 10−12 m. About 300,000 sodium atoms lined up side by side would be as thick as one sheet of paper.
Part2

The Trends

Atomic Radius in Periods

As we move across period 2 from lithium to fluorine, each atom has one more proton and one more electron than the one before. It might seem that adding electrons should make an atom larger. However, the opposite happens. A lithium atom has a radius of 152 pm, and a fluorine atom only 60 pm.

The reason is the nuclear charge. Every element in period 2 has its valence electrons in the same second energy level, so the distance to the outer shell does not get any larger. At the same time, the number of protons rises from 3 to 9. The greater the nuclear charge, the stronger the attraction to electrons and the smaller the atomic radius. The stronger pull draws the same shell in closer to the nucleus.

Thus, as we move from left to right across a period, the atomic radius decreases.

  • Recall that the nucleus has an overall positive charge due to the protons inside.
    • This positive charge attracts electrons.
  • The greater the nuclear charge (# of protons), the stronger the attraction to electrons and the smaller the atomic radius.
  • As you move from left to right across a period, the atomic radius decreases.
    • The nuclear charge (# of protons) increases.
    • The number of shells stays the same, so the stronger pull draws the same shells in closer.
Cause: the nuclear charge increases across a period while the number of energy levels stays the same.

Atomic Radius in Groups

If you recall, each new period begins a new energy level. Moving down a group therefore means adding a shell with every step. Lithium has two energy levels, sodium three and potassium four, and their radii grow from 152 pm to 186 pm to 227 pm.

It is true that each step down a group also adds protons, which pull harder on the electrons. However, two effects outweigh it. The new shell places the valence electrons farther from the nucleus, and the extra inner electrons shield the valence electrons from the nucleus. Therefore, as we move down a group, the atomic radius increases.

  • Elements in the same group differ in their number of energy levels.
    • The start of each new period adds a new energy level.
  • As you move down a group, the atomic radius increases.
    • A new energy level is added to the atom.
    • More inner electrons shield the outer electrons, so they are held less tightly.
Cause: each step down a group adds an energy level, so the valence electrons are farther from the nucleus.

Atomic Radius Trends

Putting the two trends together gives a pattern across the whole periodic table. Atomic radius decreases from left to right across a period, due to the increasing nuclear charge. Atomic radius increases from top to bottom down a group, due to the added energy levels. The largest atoms are found at the bottom left of the table (cesium and francium), and the smallest at the top right (helium and hydrogen).

  • Atomic radius decreases from left to right across a period.
  • Atomic radius increases from top to bottom down a group.
  • The largest atoms are at the bottom left (francium, cesium). The smallest are at the top right (helium, hydrogen).
Across a period: increasing nuclear charge, same number of energy levels → radius decreases.
Down a group: more energy levels, greater distance from the nucleus → radius increases.

Why do some noble gases break the pattern?
Look closely at neon and argon. Each is slightly larger than the halogen just before it. An atom’s radius is found by measuring the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms. Since noble gases almost never form bonds, their radii can only be estimated, and different tables list different values for them. The small jump comes from the way the radius is estimated, not from a real change in the trend.

Why is gallium about the same size as aluminum?
Click on aluminum (121 pm) and then gallium (122 pm). Gallium has a whole extra energy level, so it should be noticeably larger. However, between aluminum and gallium sit the ten transition metals of period 4, which add ten protons to the nucleus. The inner electrons added along with them do a poor job of shielding the valence electrons, so gallium’s nucleus pulls its outer electrons in more tightly than expected. Below gallium, the trend picks up again.
