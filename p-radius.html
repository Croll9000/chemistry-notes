Atomic Radius

Atomic radius is a measure of the size of an atom. Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.

Measuring an Atom

The distance from the nucleus to the outermost energy level.

Atomic Radius

One of the most important factors in determining the physical and chemical properties of an element is the radius of its atoms. Trends in atomic radius exist in both the groups and periods of the Periodic Table.

  • Atomic Radius – the distance from the nucleus to the outermost energy level in an atom.
    • Unit – picometers (pm)
    • 1 pm = 10−12 m. About 300,000 sodium atoms lined up side by side would be as thick as one sheet of paper.
Part2

The Trends

Atomic radius across a period and down a group.

Atomic Radius in Periods

  • Recall that the nucleus has an overall positive charge due to the protons inside.
    • This positive charge attracts electrons.
  • The greater the nuclear charge (# of protons), the stronger the attraction to electrons and the smaller the atomic radius.
  • As you move from left to right across a period, the atomic radius decreases.
    • The nuclear charge (# of protons) increases.
    • The number of shells stays the same, so the stronger pull draws the same shells in closer.
Cause: the nuclear charge increases across a period while the number of energy levels stays the same.

Atomic Radius in Groups

  • Elements in the same group differ in their number of energy levels.
    • The start of each new period adds a new energy level.
  • As you move down a group, the atomic radius increases.
    • A new energy level is added to the atom.
    • More inner electrons shield the outer electrons, so they are held less tightly.
Cause: each step down a group adds an energy level, so the valence electrons are farther from the nucleus.

Atomic Radius Trends

  • Atomic radius decreases from left to right across a period.
  • Atomic radius increases from top to bottom down a group.
  • The largest atoms are at the bottom left (francium, cesium). The smallest are at the top right (helium, hydrogen).
  • The measured radii of some noble gases are slightly larger than those of the halogens before them (Ar 101 pm vs. Cl 100 pm).
    • Noble gases rarely form bonds, so their radii are measured differently and do not follow the trend exactly.
Across a period: increasing nuclear charge, same number of energy levels → radius decreases.
Down a group: more energy levels, greater distance from the nucleus → radius increases.
