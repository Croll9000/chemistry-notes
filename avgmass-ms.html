Mass Spectrometry

Every average atomic mass on the periodic table rests on a measurement: the mass of each isotope and how common it is. Isotopes of the same element behave almost identically in chemical reactions, so they cannot be separated by ordinary chemistry. The mass spectrometer separates them by mass instead, by turning atoms into ions and bending their paths with a magnet.

Measuring Isotopes: Mass Spectrometry

The Mass Spectrometer

To find the abundance of each isotope, chemists need to separate atoms that differ by as little as one neutron. A mass spectrometer does this by sorting the atoms of a sample by mass.

The atoms are first given a charge and sent flying through the instrument. A moving charged particle can be pushed sideways by a magnet, and a light particle is pushed off course more easily than a heavy one, just as a gust of wind moves a ping-pong ball more than a bowling ball. Thus, lighter ions bend more and heavier ions bend less, so each isotope follows its own curved path to a detector.

The detector counts the ions arriving along each path, so a larger signal means more atoms of that isotope. The result is a mass spectrum, a graph with one peak for each isotope. The position of a peak gives the isotope’s mass, and the height of the peak gives its percent abundance. It makes sense, then, that the average atomic mass is always closest to the tallest peak.

Mass spectrometers have traveled far beyond the chemistry lab. NASA’s Curiosity rover, exploring Mars since 2012, carries one about the size of a microwave oven, which it uses to identify the atoms and molecules in Martian rocks and air. Here on Earth, hospitals use them to screen newborn babies for rare inherited diseases from a single drop of blood. The steps are:

  • A mass spectrometer separates the atoms of a sample by mass, so scientists can measure the mass of each isotope and how common it is.
  1. Vaporize: the sample is turned into a gas so the atoms are separate.
  2. Ionize: a beam of fast electrons knocks an electron off each atom, making positive ions. (Ions can be pushed by electric and magnetic fields; neutral atoms can’t.)
  3. Accelerate: negatively charged plates attract the positive ions and speed them into a narrow beam.
  4. Deflect: a magnet bends the path of the moving ions into a curve.
    • Lighter ions bend more.
    • Heavier ions bend less.
    • Each isotope follows its own path.
  5. Detect: a detector counts the ions that arrive along each path.
    • More ions means a larger signal.
    • The signal shows the isotope’s percent abundance.
  • Mass Spectrum – a graph with one peak for each isotope.
    • The position of a peak is the isotope’s mass.
    • The height of the peak is its percent abundance.
  • The tallest peak is the most common isotope, and the average atomic mass is always closest to that peak.
