Ionic Bonds

A metal transfers electrons to a nonmetal.

Ionic Bonds

Table salt forms when sodium, a reactive metal, and chlorine, a toxic gas, react with one another. The product is safe enough to sprinkle on food because electrons have been transferred between the atoms, creating ions held together by their opposite charges.

  • Recall that metals have low electronegativities and nonmetals have high electronegativities.
    • Metals have a weak attraction for electrons.
    • Nonmetals have a strong attraction for electrons.
  • When a metal and a nonmetal are brought together, the metal will donate its valence electrons to the nonmetal.
    • This forms 2 ions: a positive metal ion and a negative nonmetal ion.
    • There is a strong attraction between these oppositely charged ions, which we call an ionic bond.
  • Ionic Bond – strong attraction between two oppositely charged ions that form when one atom donates electrons to another.
  • Ionic Compound – compound that contains ionic bonds.
Cause: the nonmetal’s nucleus attracts electrons much more strongly than the metal’s nucleus does, so the metal’s valence electrons move to the nonmetal. The ions then attract because opposite charges attract.
Sodium has a low electronegativity while chlorine has a high electronegativity. When brought together, sodium will donate an electron to chlorine.
After the transfer of electrons, a positive and a negative ion are produced. There is a strong attraction between them, which we call an ionic bond.

Drawing Lewis Structures for Ionic Compounds

Arrows show each electron moving from the metal to the nonmetal.

Drawing Lewis Structures (Ionic)

Steps for drawing Lewis structures for ionic compounds:

  1. Draw the Lewis dot diagram for each atom in the compound.
  2. Draw arrows to show the transfer of electrons from the metal atom to the nonmetal atom.
    • Arrows should always point to the nonmetal.
  3. If necessary, recruit additional atoms.
  4. Stop when each atom has a complete valence shell.
  5. Redraw the ions with brackets around them and charges in the top right-hand corner.

Part3

Ionic Compounds and Charge

The positive and negative charges always cancel.

Ionic Compounds and Charge

Every ionic compound, from table salt to the calcium phosphate in bones, is electrically neutral overall. The positive and negative charges of its ions must balance exactly, which determines how many of each ion the compound contains.

  • Although ionic compounds consist of ions with positive and negative charges, the overall, or net, charge of any ionic compound is neutral (0).
    • The total number of positive charges is equal to the total number of negative charges.
  • This is why some compounds need more than one of an ion: the charges must add up to zero.
Potassium phosphide (K3P) has 3 K+ ions for every 1 P3− ion. When the charges of all these ions are combined: 3(+1) + (−3) = 0. The overall charge of the compound is neutral.
