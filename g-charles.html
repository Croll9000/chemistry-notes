Charles’s Law

A balloon carried from a 25 °C room into a −18 °C freezer shrinks to about 86% of its volume, and it fills out again as it warms. Cooling slows the gas particles, so they push less on the inside of the balloon until the gas takes up less space. In this section, we will discuss Charles’s law, the relationship between the volume and the temperature of a gas.

Charles’s Law

Bread rises in the oven partly because the gas bubbles inside the dough expand as they are heated. In 1787, Jacques Charles found that many different gases expand by the same fraction for each degree of heating when the pressure is kept constant.

  • Charles’s Law – the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the temperature of the gas when pressure is held constant.
  • When the temperature increases, the volume increases by the same percentage. When the temperature decreases, the volume decreases by the same percentage (temperatures in kelvin).
  • Volume and Kelvin temperature are directly proportional: doubling the Kelvin temperature doubles the volume.
    • The volume divided by the temperature stays the same: VT = constant.
  • Explanation using particles: when a gas is cooled, its particles slow down and strike the walls of the container less often and less forcefully. Since the external pressure stays the same, the walls of a flexible container such as a balloon are pushed inward, and the container shrinks until the pressure inside equals the pressure outside again. When a gas is heated, the opposite happens: the faster particles strike the walls harder and the container expands.
  • Temperatures must be in kelvin. A graph of volume against Kelvin temperature is a straight line through the origin. On a Celsius axis, the line extended to zero volume reaches −273 °C, absolute zero.
    • Absolute Zero – the lowest possible temperature, 0 K (−273 °C), where particles have the least possible kinetic energy (see Unit 7: Temperature & Temperature Scales).
    • The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, so a Kelvin temperature is directly proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles. A Celsius temperature is not: 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C.

Example
Consider a balloon on a cold day. The gas particles inside slow down and push on the balloon less, so they move closer together and the volume of the balloon decreases. On the other hand, if the balloon is heated, the particles speed up and push harder, and the balloon grows as the gas particles move farther away from each other.

Solving Charles’s Law Problems

A dented table-tennis ball dropped into hot water often pops back into shape within seconds: heating the air sealed inside from about 20 °C to about 80 °C makes it expand by about 20%. Charles’s law predicts how much a gas expands for a given change in temperature. In this section, we will solve Charles’s law problems.

Solving Problems

Charles’s law problems compare the volume and temperature of the same gas before and after a change, at constant pressure. The temperatures must always be converted to kelvin: a gas at 20 °C heated to 40 °C does not double in volume.

  • Steps for solving gas law problems:
    1. Read the question and list all given variables and the unknown.
    2. If necessary, convert all temperatures to kelvin.
    3. Write down Charles’s Law.
    4. Substitute the variables into the equation and solve for the unknown algebraically.
    5. Write the final answer with units.
  • The two pressures must have the same unit, and so must the two volumes.
