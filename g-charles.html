Charles’s Law

Charles’s Law

What happens to a gas that is heated while the pressure on it stays the same? Recall that temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles. Heating the gas makes its particles move faster, so they strike the walls more often and with more force. If the walls can move, as the skin of a balloon can, they are pushed outward until the gas takes up more space and the collisions on each unit of area drop back to the outside pressure. We would therefore predict that when the temperature of a gas increases, its volume increases, and that when the temperature decreases, its volume decreases.

In the 1780s, the French scientist Jacques Charles, who had flown in one of the first balloons to carry a person, measured this effect. He found that many different gases expand by the same fraction for each degree they are heated, as long as the pressure stays the same. This relationship is called Charles’s law.

Charles’s Law – the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the temperature of the gas when pressure is held constant.

Since the relationship is direct, the volume and the Kelvin temperature change by the same factor in the same direction. Doubling the Kelvin temperature of a gas doubles its volume, and halving it halves the volume. As a result, the volume divided by the temperature stays the same:

VT = constant

A graph of volume vs. Kelvin temperature is a straight line with a positive slope that passes through the origin.

These temperatures must always be in kelvins. If the straight line on a graph of volume vs. Celsius temperature is extended back to zero volume, it reaches −273 °C, which is absolute zero. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, so a Kelvin temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles. A Celsius temperature is not, so doubling a Celsius temperature does not double the volume.

Absolute Zero – the lowest possible temperature, 0 K (−273 °C), where particles have the least possible kinetic energy.

A hot air balloon is Charles’s law at work. When the burner heats the air inside the envelope, the air expands and some of it is pushed out of the opening at the bottom. The air left inside is less dense than the cooler air outside, so the balloon rises.

  • Charles’s Law – the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the temperature of the gas when pressure is held constant.
  • When the temperature increases, the volume increases by the same factor. When the temperature decreases, the volume decreases by the same factor (temperatures in kelvin).
  • Volume and Kelvin temperature are directly proportional: doubling the Kelvin temperature doubles the volume.
    • The volume divided by the temperature stays the same: VT = constant.
  • Explanation using particles: when a gas is cooled, its particles slow down and strike the walls of the container less often and less forcefully. Since the external pressure stays the same, the walls of a flexible container such as a balloon are pushed inward, and the container shrinks until the pressure inside equals the pressure outside again. When a gas is heated, the opposite happens: the faster particles strike the walls harder and the container expands.
  • Temperatures must be in kelvin. A graph of volume against Kelvin temperature is a straight line with a positive slope that passes through the origin. On a Celsius axis, the line extended to zero volume reaches −273 °C, absolute zero.
    • Absolute Zero – the lowest possible temperature, 0 K (−273 °C), where particles have the least possible kinetic energy.
    • The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, so a Kelvin temperature is directly proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles. A Celsius temperature is not: 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C.
V1T1=V2T2
V1 – initial volume (L or mL)T1 – initial temperature (K)V2 – final volume (L or mL)T2 – final temperature (K)
Example
Consider a balloon on a cold day. The gas particles inside slow down and push on the balloon less, so they move closer together and the volume of the balloon decreases. On the other hand, if the balloon is heated, the particles speed up and push harder, and the balloon grows as the gas particles move farther away from each other.

Solving Charles’s Law Problems

Solving Problems

Charles’s law problems compare the volume and the temperature of the same gas before and after a change, at constant pressure. The temperatures must always be converted to kelvins first. A gas heated from 20 °C to 40 °C does not double in volume, since its Kelvin temperature only rises from 293 K to 313 K, an increase of less than 7%.

A dented table-tennis ball dropped into hot water often pops back into shape within seconds. The air sealed inside expands as it warms from about 20 °C to about 80 °C, pushing the dent back out. To solve a Charles’s law problem:

  1. List the variables and the unknown. Read the question and list every given variable, with its unit, and the unknown.
  2. Convert temperatures to kelvin. If a temperature is given in °C, add 273: K = °C + 273.
  3. Write down Charles’s law.
  4. Substitute and solve. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation, then solve for the unknown algebraically.
  5. Write the final answer with units.
