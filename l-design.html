Part1

Variables in an Experiment

Before a new medicine is approved in the United States, it is tested in clinical trials: some patients take the medicine, others do not, and the results of the two groups are compared. Experiments like these are planned carefully so that the results show what really causes an effect. In this section, we will discuss the variables in an experiment.

Variables in an Experiment

An experiment tests how one quantity affects another. To find out whether temperature changes how fast sugar dissolves, a student must change only the temperature and keep everything else the same. Otherwise, there is no way to tell which change caused the result.

  • Variable – a quantity that can change or be changed in an experiment.
  • Independent Variable – the variable that the experimenter changes on purpose.
    • It is the possible cause in the experiment.
  • Dependent Variable – the variable that is measured. Its value depends on the independent variable.
    • It is the effect, or the result, in the experiment.
  • Controlled Variable – a variable that is kept the same in every trial, so it cannot affect the results.
Example
A student dissolves 5.0 g of sugar in 100 mL of water at 20, 40, 60 and 80 °C and times how long each sample takes to dissolve. Independent variable: the temperature of the water. Dependent variable: the time to dissolve. Controlled variables: the mass of sugar, the volume of water and the amount of stirring.

Designing a Fair Test

In 1747, the ship’s surgeon James Lind tested six treatments for scurvy on twelve sick sailors. He gave every pair of sailors the same diet and the same care and changed only the treatment. The two sailors who ate oranges and lemons recovered quickly, so Lind could tell that the citrus fruit, and not anything else, made the difference. In this section, we will discuss how to design a fair test.

Fair Tests and Control Groups

An experiment is a fair test when the only thing that differs between the trials is the independent variable. Then any difference in the results must have been caused by that variable.

  • Hypothesis – a testable prediction about how the independent variable will affect the dependent variable.
    • It is often written as an “If … then …” statement, for example “If the water is warmer, then the sugar will dissolve faster.”
  • Change only one variable at a time. If two variables change at once, there is no way to tell which one caused the result.
  • Control Group – the group that does not receive the change being tested. It is used for comparison.
    • The results of the experimental groups are compared with the control group.
  • Experimental Group – a group that receives the change being tested (a value of the independent variable).
  • Repeat each trial several times. Every measurement has some error, so repeated trials (and their average) give more reliable results than a single trial.
Example
In the bean plant experiment above, the independent variable is the mass of fertilizer. The dependent variable is the height of each plant. The plant with no fertilizer is the control group. The type of plant, the pot, the soil, the amount of water and the amount of light are controlled variables.
