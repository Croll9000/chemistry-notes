Families of the Periodic Table

Groups of elements with similar properties are called families. Each family has a location on the periodic table, properties to know for the Regents, and some interesting facts.

Main-Group Families

Alkali Metals (Group 1)

The elements in group 1, except hydrogen, are called the alkali metals. Each one possesses 1 valence electron, and that single, loosely held electron explains nearly everything about them.

Since an alkali metal only has to lose one electron to reach a stable octet, it gives that electron up very easily and forms a 1+ ion. This makes the alkali metals the most reactive metals on the periodic table. They react with air and with water, so they are stored under oil and are never found uncombined in nature. They are soft enough to be cut with a butter knife and very shiny when freshly cut, although the cut surface dulls within seconds as it reacts with the air.

Their reactivity increases as you move down the group. Lithium fizzes gently in water. Sodium melts into a silvery ball that skates across the surface. Potassium bursts into a lilac flame. This should make sense, since each step down the group places the valence electron in a higher energy level, farther from the nucleus, where it is easier to remove.

The same easy loss of an electron makes lithium valuable. Lithium is the lightest metal, and its atoms give up electrons readily, so a lithium battery can store a great deal of energy in very little mass. This is the reason lithium-ion batteries power phones, laptops and electric cars.

  • The elements in group 1 (except hydrogen) are known as the alkali metals.
  • Highly reactive
    • Never found uncombined (as a pure element) in nature
  • Most metallic elements
    • Very shiny
    • Very soft
  • Possess 1 valence electron
  • Form 1+ ions
  • Interesting facts
    • They are so reactive with air and water that they are stored under oil.
    • They are soft enough to cut with a knife.
    • They react with water to make hydrogen gas. The reaction gets more violent down the group: lithium fizzes, sodium skates around, potassium bursts into flame.
    • Lithium powers the rechargeable batteries in phones and laptops.

Alkaline Earth Metals (Group 2)

The elements in group 2 are referred to as the alkaline earth metals. Each possesses 2 valence electrons, which it loses to form a 2+ ion. They are very reactive, but less reactive than the alkali metals beside them. Two electrons must be removed instead of one, and that takes more energy.

Magnesium and calcium are the most familiar members. A burning strip of magnesium gives off a light so bright and white that it is used in emergency flares and fireworks. Calcium compounds make up bones, teeth and seashells.

A heavier member of the family has an unusual use in medicine. Barium is a dense element that blocks X-rays. Before an X-ray of the stomach or intestines, a patient drinks a thick suspension of barium sulfate, and the barium outlines the digestive tract so doctors can see it on the image. Free barium ions are poisonous, but barium sulfate is so insoluble in water that it passes through the body without dissolving.

  • The elements in group 2 are known as the alkaline earth metals.
  • Very reactive, although not as reactive as group 1
  • Possess 2 valence electrons
  • Form 2+ ions
  • Interesting facts
    • Calcium compounds make up your bones and teeth, and seashells and chalk.
    • Magnesium burns with a blinding white light, so it is used in flares and fireworks.
    • Strontium compounds give fireworks their red color.

Halogens (Group 17)

The elements in group 17 are known as the halogens. Each possesses 7 valence electrons, one short of a full octet, so it readily gains one electron to form a 1− ion. This makes the halogens the most reactive nonmetals. Fluorine is the most reactive of all, and reactivity decreases as you move down the group, since a larger atom attracts an extra electron less strongly.

The halogens exist as diatomic molecules (F2, Cl2, Br2, I2). They are also the only group with elements in all three phases at room temperature. Fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid and iodine is a solid. The name “halogen” means “salt former,” since halogens combine with metals to form salts.

The reactivity that makes chlorine dangerous to breathe also makes it deadly to bacteria. In 1908, Jersey City, New Jersey, became the first city in the United States to add chlorine to its drinking water every day. Within a few decades, deaths from waterborne diseases such as typhoid and cholera fell sharply across American cities. Iodine is put to a similar use in hospitals, where a brown iodine solution is painted on the skin to kill bacteria before surgery.

  • The elements in group 17 are known as the halogens.
  • Highly reactive
  • Contain elements in all 3 phases of matter at room temperature
    • Gas: fluorine (F2) and chlorine (Cl2)
    • Liquid: bromine (Br2)
    • Solid: iodine (I2)
  • Diatomic: they exist as pairs of atoms (F2, Cl2, Br2, I2)
  • Possess 7 valence electrons
  • Form 1− ions
  • Interesting facts
    • “Halogen” means “salt former.” Halogens combine with metals to form salts (ionic compounds).
      Example
      Chlorine combines with sodium to form table salt, NaCl.
    • Fluorine is the most reactive nonmetal. Reactivity decreases down the group.
    • Chlorine is used to disinfect drinking water and swimming pools.
    • Fluoride in toothpaste helps prevent cavities.

Noble Gases (Group 18)

In 1894, the British scientists Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay ran into a small but stubborn puzzle. Nitrogen taken from the air was always slightly denser than nitrogen made by a chemical reaction in the lab. They traced the difference to a small amount of an unknown gas mixed into the air. Nothing they tried would make this gas react with anything, so they named it argon, from the Greek word for “lazy.” Within four years Ramsay had also found neon, krypton and xenon, an entire column of the periodic table that Mendeleev had never suspected.

The elements in group 18 are called the noble gases. Each possesses 8 valence electrons, except helium, which has 2. In both cases the valence shell is full, so the noble gases do not gain or lose electrons. They are gases at room temperature, and they are inert, or unreactive. Since they do not bond even to each other, they exist as single atoms.

Inert – unreactive; not readily forming chemical bonds with other atoms.

Their lack of reactivity makes them useful. Helium is less dense than air and cannot burn, so it fills balloons and airships. Neon glows red-orange when electricity passes through it, giving “neon” signs their name.

  • The elements in group 18 are known as the noble gases.
  • Inert – unreactive
  • Gases at room temperature
  • Possess 8 valence electrons (helium has 2)
    • Their valence shells are full.
  • Do not gain or lose electrons
  • Monatomic – found as single atoms under standard conditions
  • Interesting facts
    • Helium is less dense than air, so helium balloons float.
    • Neon glows red-orange in “neon” signs. Other noble gases glow other colors.
    • Argon makes up almost 1% of the air.
    • The larger noble gases (krypton and xenon) can react on rare occasions.

The Middle and the Bottom

Transition Metals (Groups 3–12)

The elements in groups 3–12 are referred to as the transition metals, since they form the transition between groups 1–2 and groups 13–18. Iron, copper, nickel, silver, gold and platinum, the metals we handle most often, are all transition metals.

Unlike the main-group metals, some transition metals have multiple oxidation states, meaning more than one possible ion charge. Iron forms both Fe2+ and Fe3+, and copper forms both Cu+ and Cu2+. Many of their compounds are also brightly colored and form brightly colored solutions in water, such as the deep blue of copper(II) sulfate. For this reason transition metal compounds are used as pigments in paints, glazes and stained glass.

The Statue of Liberty shows both properties at once. When it was dedicated in 1886, its copper skin was the reddish-brown of a new penny. Over about 30 years, the copper slowly reacted with air and rain and formed the pale green coating it has today.

Not all transition metals react so readily. Platinum, palladium and rhodium hardly corrode at all, but their surfaces help other reactions happen. Every car’s catalytic converter contains a thin coating of these metals, where poisonous carbon monoxide and unburned fuel in the exhaust are converted into carbon dioxide and water before they leave the tailpipe.

  • The elements in groups 3–12 are known as the transition metals.
    • They are the transition between the s-block (groups 1–2) and the p-block (groups 13–18).
  • Many form brightly colored solutions in water.
  • Some transition metals have multiple oxidation states (more than one possible ion charge).
    Example
    Iron forms Fe2+ and Fe3+; copper forms Cu+ and Cu2+.
  • Interesting facts
    • Familiar metals like iron, copper, silver, gold and nickel are transition metals.
    • Mercury is the only metal that is a liquid at room temperature.
    • Gold and platinum are so unreactive that they stay shiny for centuries, which is why they are used in jewelry.

Lanthanides and Actinides

Two rows of elements are set apart at the bottom of the periodic table. They really belong in periods 6 and 7, but placing them inside the table would make it too wide to fit on a page. The first row is the lanthanides, and the second is the actinides.

Lanthanides – elements with atomic numbers 57–71

Actinides – elements with atomic numbers 89–103

The lanthanides are metallic and reactive. Although they are sometimes called “rare earth elements,” several are found in modern technology. Neodymium is used to make the strongest permanent magnets known, so a magnet small enough to fit in an earbud can produce clear sound, and the motors of electric cars and the generators of wind turbines depend on them. Europium has an unfamiliar use. Compounds of europium glow red under ultraviolet light, and they are printed into euro banknotes so that a forged note can be spotted under a UV lamp.

The actinides are metallic, reactive and radioactive. Uranium and plutonium are fuels for nuclear power, and almost every element after uranium (92) exists only because scientists made it in a laboratory or a nuclear reactor.

  • Lanthanides – elements with atomic numbers 57–71
    • Metallic
    • Reactive
  • Actinides – elements with atomic numbers 89–103
    • Metallic
    • Reactive
    • Radioactive
  • They are placed below the table so it fits on a page. They really belong in periods 6 and 7.
  • Interesting facts
    • Neodymium (a lanthanide) makes the strongest permanent magnets, used in earbuds, electric cars and wind turbines.
    • Uranium and plutonium (actinides) are fuels for nuclear power.
    • Almost every element after uranium (92) exists only because scientists made it in a laboratory or nuclear reactor.

Hydrogen: A Family of Its Own

Hydrogen sits at the top of group 1 since it has 1 valence electron, just like lithium and sodium. However, hydrogen is not an alkali metal. It is a nonmetal and a diatomic gas (H2) at STP. Although it can lose its electron, it can also gain one, as the halogens do, or share it. For this reason, hydrogen is best thought of as a family of its own.

  • Hydrogen sits above group 1 because it has 1 valence electron.
  • Hydrogen is not an alkali metal.
    • It is a nonmetal and a diatomic gas (H2) at STP.
