Graham’s Law of Effusion

During the Second World War, scientists separated uranium-235 from uranium-238 by forcing uranium hexafluoride gas through thousands of porous barriers. Molecules containing the lighter isotope passed through only about 0.4% faster, so the gas had to pass through barrier after barrier. In this section, we will discuss Graham’s law, which predicts how quickly gases escape through tiny openings.

Effusion and Graham’s Law

Natural gas has no odor of its own, so gas companies add a small amount of a strong-smelling sulfur compound that lets people notice a leak as the gas spreads through a room. At the same temperature, all gas particles have the same average kinetic energy, so lighter particles must move faster. In 1846, Thomas Graham showed how the rate at which a gas escapes depends on the mass of its particles.

  • Gas particles are constantly in motion. If they are released in a small part of a container, they eventually spread out until every region of the container has an equal concentration of the gas.
    • Diffusion – the process of particles moving from areas of high concentration to areas of low concentration.
    • The rate of diffusion depends on the temperature and concentration of the gas, the volume of the container and the molar mass of the gas.
  • Gases can also move through small holes.
    • Effusion – the process in which a gas escapes a container through a small hole.
    • The rate of effusion depends on the temperature and concentration of the gas, the size of the hole and the molar mass of the particles.
  • At the same temperature, gases have the same average kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²), so lighter particles move faster and effuse faster.
  • This means that smaller particles effuse more quickly, while larger particles effuse more slowly.
  • Graham’s Law of Effusion – the rate of diffusion or effusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molar mass.
  • Graham’s law comes from the kinetic energy of the particles. Two gases at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy, so the lighter gas must move faster to have the same kinetic energy. The derivation is shown in the box.
  • The speed of the particles, and so the rate of effusion, is inversely proportional to the square root of the mass. A gas with 4 times the molar mass effuses at ½ the rate.
