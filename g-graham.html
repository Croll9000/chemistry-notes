Graham’s Law of Effusion

Effusion and Graham’s Law

Gas particles are constantly in motion, so if they are released in one part of a container, they eventually spread out until every region has the same concentration. This process is known as diffusion. Natural gas has no odor of its own, so gas companies add a small amount of a strong-smelling sulfur compound. When gas leaks, the smell diffuses through the room and warns the people in it. Gases can also escape through a tiny hole, a process called effusion, and a helium balloon slowly sags over a few days as helium effuses through the rubber.

Diffusion – the process of particles moving from areas of high concentration to areas of low concentration.

Effusion – the process in which a gas escapes a container through a small hole.

The rates of both processes depend on the temperature of the gas, its concentration and the molar mass of its particles. The molar mass matters because of kinetic energy. Two gases at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy, KE = ½mv², so the lighter particles must move faster to have the same kinetic energy as the heavier ones. Faster particles reach the hole more often, so lighter gases effuse more quickly and heavier gases effuse more slowly.

In 1846, Thomas Graham showed that the rate of effusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molar mass. This is called Graham’s law of effusion. A gas with 4 times the molar mass of another effuses at ½ the rate. A rate of effusion is measured as an amount of gas per unit of time, such as mol/s, and the derivation from the kinetic energy is shown in the box.

Graham’s Law of Effusion – the rate of diffusion or effusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molar mass.

Graham’s law helped build the first atomic bomb. Uranium-235 and uranium-238 were turned into a gas and forced through thousands of porous barriers in a vast factory in Oak Ridge, Tennessee.
