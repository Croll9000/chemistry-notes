Percent Composition

Percent Composition

Every formula unit of a compound contains the same atoms in the same ratio, so every compound has a fixed percent of each element by mass, whether the sample is a pebble or a mountain. This fixed percent is referred to as the percent composition, and it comes straight from the formula and the periodic table.

Percent Composition – the percent by mass of each element in a compound.

Like any percent, it compares a part with a whole:

% composition = mass of partmass of whole × 100

The mass of the part is the mass of one element in one mole of the compound: the number of atoms of that element times its atomic mass. The mass of the whole is the molar mass of the compound.

Mining companies rely on this calculation. Most of the iron used to make steel comes from an ore called hematite, Fe2O3. In hematite, the part is 2 × 55.85 = 111.70 g of iron, and the whole is 159.70 g, so:

% Fe = 111.70 g159.70 g × 100 = 69.9%

Before a company spends millions of dollars mining an ore body, this number tells it how much iron each ton can provide. The percents of all the elements in a compound always add up to 100%, which is a useful check. To calculate percent composition:

  1. Identify the part and the whole. The part is the element; the whole is the compound.
  2. Calculate the molar mass of the compound (the mass of the whole).
  3. Calculate the mass of the part: number of atoms of the element × its atomic mass.
  4. Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100.
  5. Write the final answer with a percent sign.

Hydrated Compounds

Hydrated Compounds

Gypsum, the mineral used to make the drywall in most homes, feels completely dry, yet about 21% of its mass is water. The water is not on its surface or soaked into its pores. It is part of the crystal itself.

Recall that ionic compounds exist as crystal lattices. Sometimes molecules of water become embedded in the lattice in fixed positions between the ions. The result is a hydrated compound (hydrate). A compound that does not contain water is known as an anhydrous compound.

Hydrated Compound (Hydrate) – compound (usually ionic) that contains water.

Anhydrous Compound – compound that does not contain water.

The trapped water makes drywall useful in a house fire. As the wall heats up, the water in the gypsum is released as steam, which absorbs energy and slows the spread of the fire.

The water in a hydrate is held by attractions between the polar water molecules and the ions, so it can change the properties of the crystal. Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate is a white powder. Its hydrate forms bright blue crystals, and adding a drop of water to the white powder turns it blue again. Some anhydrous compounds absorb water from the air so readily that they are used as drying agents, such as the small desiccant packets packed with shoes and electronics.

  • Ionic compounds exist as crystal lattices, geometric arrangements of alternating cations and anions.
    • Sometimes molecules of water become embedded in the crystal lattice structure.
    • These are known as hydrated compounds.
    • Ionic lattices without water are known as anhydrous compounds.
    • Hydrated Compound (Hydrate) – compound (usually ionic) that contains water.
    • Anhydrous Compound – compound that does not contain water.
  • Copper(II) chloride can become hydrated by absorbing water molecules into its lattice structure.

Naming Hydrated Compounds

Water joins the crystal lattice of a hydrate in a fixed ratio, since each water molecule occupies a particular position in the repeating pattern. Gypsum always holds exactly 2 water molecules for every formula unit of calcium sulfate, never 1.5 or 3. The name and formula of a hydrate must therefore show this ratio, and they do so with the same Greek prefixes used to name covalent compounds.

Gypsum is named calcium sulfate dihydrate and written CaSO4•2H2O. The dot in the formula shows that the water molecules are held in the crystal, not chemically bonded into the ionic compound. To name a hydrated compound:

  1. Name the ionic compound.
  2. Include a prefix to show the number of water molecules for every formula unit of the ionic compound.
  3. Attach the ending “-hydrate” after the prefix.

To write the formula of a hydrate:

  1. Write the formula for the ionic compound.
  2. Identify the number of water molecules per formula unit from the prefix, and write it after a dot: •nH2O.
  • When water becomes part of the crystal lattice of a hydrate, it does so in a fixed ratio. When naming hydrated compounds it is necessary to specify the ratio of ionic formula units to water molecules.

Percent of Water in a Hydrate

Since the water in a hydrate is held by attractions rather than by chemical bonds within the ionic compound, heating a hydrate drives the water out of the crystal as vapor and leaves the anhydrous compound behind. When blue copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate is heated in a crucible, it slowly turns into a white powder, and the crucible gets lighter as the water escapes. Weighing the sample before and after heating tells us exactly how much water it contained.

How do we know all of the water is gone? The sample is heated, cooled and weighed again, over and over, until two masses in a row are the same.

We can find the percent of water by mass from a lab measurement or from the formula of the hydrate. Both compare the mass of water, the part, with the mass of the hydrate, the whole:

From the lab: % water = mass of water lostmass of hydrate × 100

From the formula: % water = mass of water in one mole of the hydratemolar mass of the hydrate × 100

Comparing the two values is a good way to judge a lab result. If the measured percent is lower than the percent from the formula, some water was probably still trapped in the crystal. A measured percent that is too high suggests that the solid itself began to break down or spattered out of the crucible.
