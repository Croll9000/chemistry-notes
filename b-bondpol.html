Polar and Nonpolar Bonds

Electronegativity decides who pulls harder.

Polar Bonds

In a tug of war between two evenly matched teams, the rope stays in the middle. When one team is stronger, the rope moves toward it. Electrons shared between two atoms behave in a similar way.

  • Recall that different elements have different attractions for electrons. We call this property electronegativity.
    • A high electronegativity means that an atom has a strong attraction for electrons, and a low electronegativity denotes a weak attraction.
  • If a covalent bond is formed between two atoms with a difference in electronegativity, it will create an unequal sharing of electrons. This is known as a polar bond.
  • Polar Covalent Bond – unequal, or asymmetrical, sharing of electrons between two atoms.
Since chlorine (electronegativity 3.16) has a higher electronegativity than hydrogen (2.20), the electrons in the bond between them are pulled more towards the chlorine side of the molecule. This is known as a polar covalent bond.

Polar Bonds and Dipoles

  • Because the shared electrons spend more time near the more electronegative atom, that end of the bond has a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other end has a partial positive charge (δ+).
    • The symbol δ (lowercase Greek “delta”) means “partial.” These are not full charges like those on ions.
  • Dipole – a bond or molecule with a partially positive end and a partially negative end.
    • A dipole can be shown with an arrow that points toward the δ− end. The cross on the tail marks the δ+ end.

Nonpolar Bonds

  • If both of the atoms in a bond have similar or equal electronegativities, there will be an equal, or symmetrical, sharing of electrons between the two. This is known as a nonpolar covalent bond.
  • Nonpolar Covalent Bond – equal sharing of electrons between two atoms.
In this molecule of Cl2, both atoms have an equal electronegativity. Therefore, they share electron density equally. Neither side of the molecule possesses a net charge.

Calculating Bond Polarity

  • All bonds fall along a spectrum from nonpolar to ionic depending on whether there is a perfectly equal sharing of electrons (nonpolar covalent) or a complete transfer of electrons (ionic) between the atoms.
  • The difference in electronegativity between the atoms in the bond determines its character.
  • These cutoffs are guidelines. For example, H–F has a difference of 1.78 but is a polar covalent bond, because hydrogen and fluorine are both nonmetals.
