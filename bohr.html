Part1

Filling the Energy Levels

In the Bohr model, electrons sit in energy levels around the nucleus. Each level holds a limited number, and the lowest levels fill first.

Atomic Structure

The arrangement of electrons in an atom determines how that atom will behave in chemical reactions. Since electrons are far too small and fast to be observed directly, chemists use models to describe where they are found.

For our purposes we will be working with the Bohr Model of the Atom. In this model, the protons and neutrons are in the nucleus, and the electrons are found in energy levels (shells) at fixed distances around the nucleus.

Electron Configuration

Electrons are not scattered randomly around the nucleus. They are organized into energy levels, each able to hold only a certain number of electrons. Knowing how electrons are distributed among these levels allows us to predict how an atom will react.

According to the Bohr Model of the Atom, electrons are found in energy levels surrounding the nucleus.

  • Each energy level can only hold a specific maximum number of electrons.
  • Energy levels close to the nucleus have less energy than levels further from the nucleus.
  • Energy levels are filled starting with the lowest energy level before moving to the next highest although there are exceptions to this.
    • Note that this will only work for elements 1-18.
  • The 3rd energy level only needs 8 electrons before electrons start filling the 4th energy level. Even though n = 3 can hold up to 18, after it has 8 electrons the next 2 electrons go into the 4th energy level. That is why potassium (19) is 2-8-8-1 and calcium (20) is 2-8-8-2, not 2-8-9 or 2-8-10.

Placing Electrons

When placing electrons into energy levels, we must fill up the lowest energy levels first before moving onto the next. Lower energy levels are closer to the nucleus, where electrons are attracted more strongly (Coulomb’s law: smaller distance, stronger force), so an atom is most stable (lowest in energy) with its electrons as low as possible. Each level keeps getting filled until there are no electrons left to place.

  • There are many exceptions to this basic rule but for now this is the process we will follow.
Consider an atom of fluorine with 9 electrons. When constructing the atom, begin by placing 2 electrons in the 1st energy level, n=1. There are still 7 remaining electrons and they get placed in the 2nd energy level, n=2. No electrons need to go in the 3rd energy level since the 2nd is not completely full.
An atom of Argon has 18 protons, 18 electrons and 22 neutrons. When placing its electrons into energy levels, the 1st energy level is filled with 2 electrons. The 2nd energy level is then filled with 8. The 3rd energy level is also filled with 8.

Part2

Writing Shell Notation

Instead of drawing every electron, we can write how many are in each energy level.

Shell Notation

  • Shell Notation – way of describing how many electrons are found in each energy level
    • When writing the electron configuration of an atom, we list how many electrons are found in each energy level.
      • Start with the lowest level: 1 → 2 → 3 →…
      • Separate each number with a hyphen.
    • Ex: An atom of Magnesium with 12 electrons would have an electron configuration of 2-8-2 when written in shell notation form.
We can write Argon’s electron configuration in shell notation form as 2-8-8. It has 2 electrons in the 1st shell, 8 in the 2nd and 8 in the 3rd.
Element = ArgonProtons = 18Electrons = 18Neutrons = 22Electron Configuration: 2 − 8 − 8

Part4

Patterns on the Periodic Table

Atoms in the same group and in the same period share patterns in their energy levels and valence electrons.

Patterns on the Periodic Table

When elements are examined closely, we can see that patterns in atomic structure emerge along the periodic table. Certain similarities exist amongst atoms within the same rows, referred to as periods, and columns, referred to as groups. Understanding these patterns is essential to making predictions about how elements will react with one another.

  • Down a group:
    • Each element has one more energy level (shell) than the element above it.
    • Each element has the same number of valence electrons.
    • That is why elements in the same group have similar chemical properties.
  • Across a period: every element has the same number of energy levels (equal to the period number), and the number of valence electrons increases by 1 from left to right.
