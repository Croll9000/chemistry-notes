The Ideal Gas Law

A driver’s airbag fills with about 60 L of nitrogen gas in roughly 30 milliseconds, produced by a reaction inside the steering wheel. To know how much propellant the reaction needs, engineers must connect the amount of gas, in moles, to the volume, pressure and temperature it will have. In this section, we will discuss the ideal gas law, the one equation that relates all four gas variables.

The Ideal Gas Law

The combined gas law compares one sample of gas before and after a change, so it never needs to know how much gas there is. The ideal gas law describes a single state of a gas and includes the amount of gas, so it can answer questions such as how many moles of helium fill a balloon.

  • The state variables of a gas (pressure, volume, temperature and amount) are all linked: if one variable changes, at least one of the others changes as well.
  • For any ideal gas, the product of its pressure and volume divided by the product of its number of moles and temperature, PV ÷ nT, equals the same constant value, the ideal gas constant.
  • Ideal Gas Law – the equation relating the four state variables (pressure, volume, temperature and number of moles) for an ideal gas.
  • Ideal Gas Constant (R) – the constant of proportionality relating pressure, volume, temperature and number of moles in an ideal gas.
    • R = 8.314 L·kPa/(mol·K) when pressure is in kilopascals.
    • R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) when pressure is in atmospheres.
    • R can be written with other pressure units too: R = 62.36 L·mmHg/(mol·K) when pressure is in millimeters of mercury.
  • To use R, the units must match it: pressure in atm (or kPa), volume in liters, amount in moles and temperature in kelvin.
PV=nRT
P – pressure (atm or kPa)V – volume (L)n – number of moles (mol)R – ideal gas constantT – temperature (K)

The Meaning of R

For every ideal gas, under every set of conditions, the combination PV ÷ nT comes out to the same number. That number is R. Its value can be found from the molar volume at STP, which you learned in Unit 6.

  • Rearranging PV = nRT gives R = PV ÷ nT: R is the value of PV ÷ nT for any ideal gas.
  • At STP, 1 mole of an ideal gas occupies 22.4 L at 1 atm and 273 K:
    • R = (1 atm)(22.4 L) ÷ [(1 mol)(273 K)] = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K), which rounds the more precise value 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K)
    • With pressure in kilopascals: R = (101.3 kPa)(22.4 L) ÷ [(1 mol)(273 K)] = 8.31 L·kPa/(mol·K), which rounds the more precise value 8.314 L·kPa/(mol·K)
  • R is the same for every gas because, in the ideal model, the pressure of a gas depends only on how many particles there are and how fast they move, not on what the particles are.
  • The combined gas law comes from the ideal gas law: for a fixed amount of gas, PV ÷ T = nR, which is constant.

Solving Ideal Gas Law Problems

A small helium tank sold for party balloons releases about 250 L of helium at room pressure, enough to fill around 30 balloons. With the ideal gas law, the number of moles, and therefore the mass, of helium in any balloon can be calculated from its volume, pressure and temperature. In this section, we will solve ideal gas law problems.

Solving Problems

Ideal gas law problems give three of the four variables P, V, n and T and ask for the fourth. Most mistakes come from units: R fixes the units of every other quantity in the equation.

  • Steps for solving ideal gas law problems:
    1. Write down the given information.
    2. Choose R to match the pressure unit.
    3. Make sure the units are correct: K = °C + 273; volume in liters; grams to moles with n = m ÷ M.
    4. Substitute into PV = nRT and solve the equation algebraically.

Density of a Gas

A liter of carbon dioxide at room temperature has a mass of about 1.8 g, half again as much as a liter of air, which is why carbon dioxide collects in low places such as cellars and wells. The density of a gas depends on its molar mass, its pressure and its temperature. In this section, we will use the ideal gas law to calculate the density of a gas.

Ideal Gas Density

Substituting n = m ÷ M into PV = nRT and rearranging gives an equation for the density of a gas, D = m ÷ V. Heavier molecules, higher pressure and lower temperature all make a gas denser.

  • Gas density equation: PM = DRT, where M is the molar mass (g/mol) and D is the density (g/L).
  • At the same temperature and pressure, a gas with a larger molar mass is denser.
PM=DRT
P – pressure (atm)M – molar mass (g/mol)D – density (g/L)R – 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K)T – temperature (K)
