The Ideal Gas Law

The Ideal Gas Law

A driver’s airbag fills with about 60 L of nitrogen gas in roughly 30 milliseconds, produced by a chemical reaction inside the steering wheel. To know how much of the reacting chemical to pack into the airbag, engineers must connect the amount of gas, in moles, to the volume, pressure and temperature it will have. The combined gas law cannot do this, because it only compares one sample of gas before and after a change and never needs to know how much gas there is. The ideal gas law relates all four state variables of a gas, pressure, volume, temperature and number of moles, in a single state.

For any ideal gas, the pressure multiplied by the volume, divided by the number of moles multiplied by the Kelvin temperature, always gives the same value. This value is known as the ideal gas constant (R), and the relationship is written PV = nRT. The value of R depends on the units used for pressure: R = 8.314 L·kPa/(mol·K) when the pressure is in kilopascals, and R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) when the pressure is in atmospheres. Because R carries units, every other quantity in the equation must match it: volume in liters, amount in moles, temperature in kelvin, and pressure in the same unit as R.

  • The state variables of a gas (pressure, volume, temperature and amount) are all linked: if one variable changes, at least one of the others changes as well.
  • For any ideal gas, the product of its pressure and volume divided by the product of its number of moles and temperature, PVnT, equals the same constant value, the ideal gas constant.
  • Ideal Gas Law – the equation relating the four state variables (pressure, volume, temperature and number of moles) for an ideal gas.
  • Ideal Gas Constant (R) – the constant of proportionality relating pressure, volume, temperature and number of moles in an ideal gas.
    • R = 8.314 L·kPa/(mol·K) when pressure is in kilopascals.
    • R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) when pressure is in atmospheres.
    • R can be written with other pressure units too: R = 62.36 L·mmHg/(mol·K) when pressure is in millimeters of mercury.
  • To use R, the units must match it: pressure in atm (or kPa), volume in liters, amount in moles and temperature in kelvin.

The Meaning of R

Rearranging PV = nRT gives R = PVnT. For every ideal gas, under every set of conditions, this combination of pressure, volume, moles and temperature comes out to the same number. Its value can be calculated from the molar volume of a gas: at STP, one mole of an ideal gas occupies 22.4 L at 1 atm (101.3 kPa) and 273 K. Using 1 atm gives R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K), and using 101.3 kPa gives R = 8.31 L·kPa/(mol·K), each a rounded form of the more precise value.

R is the same for every gas because, in the ideal model, the pressure of a gas depends only on how many particles there are and how fast they move, not on what the particles are. The combined gas law also follows from the ideal gas law: for a fixed amount of gas, PVT = nR, and since n and R do not change, PVT is constant.

Solving Ideal Gas Law Problems

Solving Problems

A small helium tank sold for party balloons releases about 250 L of helium at room pressure, enough to fill around 30 balloons. With the ideal gas law, the number of moles of helium in any one balloon, and from it the mass of helium, can be calculated from the balloon’s volume, pressure and temperature. Ideal gas law problems give three of the four variables P, V, n and T and ask for the fourth. Most mistakes come from units, because the value of R fixes the unit of every other quantity in the equation. Thus, the value of R is chosen first, to match the unit of pressure. To solve an ideal gas law problem:

  1. Write down the given information.
  2. Choose R to match the pressure unit.
  3. Make sure the units are correct: K = °C + 273; volume in liters; grams converted to moles with n = mM.
  4. Substitute into PV = nRT and solve the equation algebraically.

Density of a Gas

Ideal Gas Density

A liter of carbon dioxide at room temperature has a mass of about 1.8 g, half again as much as a liter of air. This is why carbon dioxide released from dry ice or from fermentation collects in low places such as cellars and wells. The density of a gas is its mass per unit volume, D = mV, and gas densities are usually given in grams per liter (g/L). The ideal gas law can be used to find the density of any gas from its molar mass, its pressure and its temperature.

The number of moles of a gas is its mass divided by its molar mass, n = mM. Substituting this expression for n into PV = nRT gives PV = mMRT. Multiplying both sides by M and dividing both sides by V leaves mV on the right side, and since mV is the density, the result is PM = DRT, where M is the molar mass in g/mol and D is the density in g/L. The steps are shown in the box below.

Solving this equation for the density, D = PMRT, shows how each condition affects how dense a gas is. The molar mass and the pressure are on the top of the fraction, and the temperature is on the bottom:

  • A gas with a larger molar mass is denser at the same temperature and pressure. Equal volumes of gases hold equal numbers of particles, so heavier particles put more mass into each liter. This is why carbon dioxide (44.01 g/mol) sinks below air (about 29 g/mol).
  • Increasing the pressure increases the density, because more particles are packed into each liter.
  • Increasing the temperature decreases the density, because the gas expands and each liter holds fewer particles. This is why hot air rises.
