Energy: Kinetic & Potential

Every chemical and physical change comes with an energy change. Energy is stored in the positions of particles (potential energy) and in their motion (kinetic energy), and it is measured in joules or calories.

Part1

Energy

A person at rest uses about 7,000,000 J of energy (about 1,600 Calories) each day just to stay alive. That energy is stored in the chemical bonds of food and is released as the atoms rearrange during digestion and respiration. In this section, we will define energy and the units used to measure it.

Energy

In the previous unit, you learned that chemical reactions rearrange atoms into new substances. Every one of those reactions is also accompanied by a change in energy. A single AA battery, for example, stores about 9,000 joules of chemical energy, which is released as electrical energy when its reaction runs. Before we can describe these changes, we must define what energy is and the rule that governs every transfer of it.

  • Energy – the ability to do work or cause change.
    • Unit – joule (J) and kilojoule (kJ). 1 kJ = 1000 J.
    • Measuring device – energy cannot be measured directly. Chemists measure the temperature change it causes, using a calorimeter (see the Calorimetry page).
  • Energy comes in many forms, including light, heat, electrical, nuclear and chemical energy.
  • Law of Conservation of Energy – energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted into different forms.
    • When wood burns, the chemical energy stored in it is converted into heat and light. None of it disappears.
  • All forms of energy fall into two basic types:
    • Potential energy: stored energy, due to position.
    • Kinetic energy: energy of motion.

Units of Energy: Joules and Calories

Chemists measure energy in joules, but food labels report energy in Calories. A medium apple provides about 95 Calories, which is nearly 400,000 joules. The two units are linked through water: the calorie was originally defined as the energy needed to warm one gram of water by one degree Celsius. How are calories and joules related, and why is the food Calorie written with a capital C?

  • Joule (J) – the SI unit of energy.
    • The joule is the unit of energy used in chemistry, and in every calculation in this unit.
    • One joule is a small amount of energy: about the energy needed to lift a 100 g apple 1 meter.
    • Larger amounts are given in kilojoules: 1 kJ = 1000 J.
  • Some reference points for the size of a joule:
    • 4.184 J raises the temperature of 1 g of water by 1 °C.
    • A 10-watt LED light bulb uses 10 J of electrical energy every second.
    • Heating a 250 mL cup of water from 20 °C to 100 °C takes about 84 kJ.
    • A slice of bread provides about 335 kJ of chemical energy.
  • The calorie is an older unit of energy. It is still used today, mostly to describe the energy in food.
  • Calorie (cal) – the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of water by 1 °C.
    • 1 cal = 4.184 J (this is now the exact definition of the calorie).
    • 1 kilocalorie (kcal) = 1000 cal = 4184 J.
  • The Calorie on a food label (with a capital C) is really a kilocalorie: 1 Calorie = 1 kcal = 1000 cal.
    • Food Calories are measured by burning food in a bomb calorimeter (see the Calorimetry page).
  • To convert, use dimensional analysis with the conversion factor 4.184 J/1 cal.

Potential Energy

Water behind the Hoover Dam falls about 180 meters through turbines that generate electricity. The water’s potential energy, stored because of its height, becomes kinetic energy as it falls, and particles store potential energy in the same way when they are held apart against their attractions. In this section, we will discuss potential energy.

Potential Energy

A boulder resting at the top of a cliff is not moving, yet it clearly holds energy: if it falls, that energy becomes motion. This stored energy depends only on the boulder’s position. Particles store energy in the same way. Because neighboring particles attract one another, pulling them apart stores energy in the system, and allowing them to come back together releases it.

  • Energy is required to move an object against a force like gravity or the electrostatic force. This energy is stored in the system as potential energy.
    • Potential Energy – stored energy of an object due to its position in a system.
  • Particles are attracted to one another. Separating them requires energy, which is stored as potential energy because of where the particles are relative to one another.
    • The farther apart particles are, the more potential energy they have.
    • The closer together particles are, the less potential energy they have.
  • We can think of particles as though they are connected by a spring.
    • Pulling the particles apart takes energy, which is stored in the stretched spring.
    • When the particles come back together, that stored energy is released, usually as kinetic energy or heat.
  • Chemical energy is potential energy stored in the arrangement of atoms and electrons in chemical bonds. Fuels, food and batteries all store chemical energy.

Kinetic Energy

At room temperature, the oxygen molecules in the air move at about 480 meters per second, almost twice as fast as a passenger jet. Every moving particle has kinetic energy, and the faster it moves, the more kinetic energy it has. In this section, we will discuss kinetic energy and how it relates to temperature.

Kinetic Energy

A baseball thrown at 90 miles per hour can shatter a window, while the same ball rolled gently across the floor cannot. The difference is its speed. Particles are never truly still: at room temperature, the nitrogen molecules in the air around you travel at roughly 500 meters per second. The energy that every moving object and particle possesses because of its motion is known as kinetic energy.

  • To put an object into motion, energy must be given to it. Once it is moving, this energy is called kinetic energy.
    • Kinetic Energy – energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • When the speed of particles increases, their kinetic energy increases.
  • When the speed of particles decreases, their kinetic energy decreases.
  • Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample (see the Temperature page).

Converting Between Potential and Kinetic Energy

On the first drop of a large roller coaster, the car may fall more than 60 meters with no engine pushing it. The energy that carries it through the rest of the ride was stored as potential energy while a chain lifted it to the top. As the car falls, potential energy is converted into kinetic energy, and as it climbs the next hill, the conversion runs in reverse. The same exchange between stored energy and motion occurs between particles during every phase change and chemical reaction.

  • Potential energy and kinetic energy are constantly converted into one another.
    • As a roller coaster car rolls downhill, its potential energy decreases and its kinetic energy increases.
    • As it climbs, kinetic energy is converted back into potential energy.
    • The total amount of energy stays the same (the law of conservation of energy). Friction converts some of it into heat, but none is destroyed.
