Energy: Kinetic & Potential

Every chemical and physical change comes with an energy change. Energy is stored in the positions of particles (potential energy) and in their motion (kinetic energy), and it is measured in joules or calories.

Energy

Energy

Recall that energy is the ability to do work or cause change, and it is measured in joules (J) or kilojoules (kJ). Energy itself cannot be seen or measured directly. We only detect it through the changes it causes: an object speeding up, a sample getting warmer, a bulb giving off light. Chemists measure energy changes by the temperature changes they cause, a technique called calorimetry.

Energy – the ability to do work or cause change.

In the 1840s, the English brewer and scientist James Prescott Joule set out to show that heat and motion were connected. He hung weights from strings wound around a paddle wheel sitting in a tank of water. As the weights fell, the paddles churned the water, and Joule measured its temperature very carefully. The water warmed slightly, and the warming was always the same for the same fall of the weights. Joule concluded that the energy of the falling weights had not vanished. It had become the energy of the warmer water. The unit of energy is named after him.

Experiments like Joule’s convinced scientists that heat, motion, light and electricity are all forms of the same thing. Energy can be converted from one form into another, but the total amount never changes. This is the law of conservation of energy.

Law of Conservation of Energy – energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted into different forms.

For instance, consider a campfire. The wood and the oxygen around it store chemical energy, and as the wood burns, that energy is released as heat and light. If we could collect all of the heat and light and add it up, it would equal exactly the chemical energy lost by the wood and oxygen. This means that energy is never “used up.” It is only changed into a form that may be less useful to us, such as heat that spreads into the night air.

Although energy takes many forms, every one of them falls into one of two basic types. Potential energy is energy stored due to where something is. Kinetic energy is energy that something has because it is moving. The chemical energy in a sandwich is potential energy stored in the arrangement of its atoms. The heat from a campfire is the kinetic energy of fast-moving particles. Nearly every energy change in chemistry comes down to tracking which of these two types is increasing and which is decreasing.

  • Energy – the ability to do work or cause change.
    • Unit – joule (J) and kilojoule (kJ). 1 kJ = 1000 J.
    • Measuring device – energy cannot be measured directly. Chemists measure the temperature change it causes, using a calorimeter (see the Calorimetry page).
  • Energy comes in many forms, including light, heat, electrical, nuclear and chemical energy.
  • Law of Conservation of Energy – energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted into different forms.
    • When wood burns, the chemical energy stored in it is converted into heat and light. None of it disappears.
  • All forms of energy fall into two basic types:
    • Potential energy: stored energy, due to position.
    • Kinetic energy: energy of motion.

Units of Energy: Joules and Calories

Chemists and nutrition labels use different units for energy, and each has its own history. The joule (J) is the unit used for energy in chemistry. One joule is a small amount of energy, about the energy needed to lift a 100 g apple 1 meter off the ground. Since a joule is so small, energy changes in chemistry are often reported in kilojoules. Burning a single gram of natural gas, for instance, releases about 55 kJ.

Joule (J) – the SI unit of energy.

The calorie (cal) is an older unit, invented before scientists understood what heat really was. It was based on warming water, so it was convenient for anyone measuring heat with water and a thermometer. Joule’s paddle-wheel experiments showed how the two units are connected, and today the calorie is defined exactly as 1 cal = 4.184 J. It makes sense, then, that 4.184 J is also the energy needed to warm 1 g of water by 1 °C.

Calorie (cal) – the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of water by 1 °C.

Be careful with the Calorie printed on a food label. It is spelled with a capital C, and it is actually a kilocalorie:

1 Calorie = 1 kcal = 1000 cal = 4184 J

A 200-Calorie granola bar therefore provides 200,000 calories, or about 837 kJ, of chemical energy. Say, for instance, that a teenager eats 2,400 Calories in a day. That is about 10,000 kJ, roughly the energy needed to bring 30 liters of room-temperature water to a boil. Converting between these units is a dimensional analysis problem. The steps for converting energy units are:

  1. Write down the given quantity with its unit.
  2. Write down the desired unit.
  3. Set up the conversion factor with the given unit on the bottom and the desired unit on top.
  4. Cancel units, then multiply and divide.
  5. Write the final answer with units.

Potential Energy

Potential Energy

Energy can be stored in an object because of where it is. We call this potential energy. For instance, a boulder perched at the edge of a cliff is not moving, yet it could do a great deal of damage. The boulder holds energy because of its position, and that energy will appear as motion the moment it starts to fall.

Potential Energy – stored energy of an object due to its position in a system.

The boulder’s energy came from whatever lifted it there. Energy had to be supplied to move it upward against the pull of gravity, and that energy stays stored for as long as the boulder stays in its new position. The higher it is lifted, the more energy it took to get it there and the more potential energy it has.

Particles store energy in exactly the same way, except that the force involved is not gravity but the attraction between opposite charges. Neighboring particles attract one another, so pulling them apart is like lifting the boulder. It takes energy, and that energy is stored. Thus, the farther apart particles are, the more potential energy they have, and the closer together particles are, the less potential energy they have.

This can be modeled with two particles joined by a rubber band. Stretching the band takes energy, and when it is released, the particles snap back together and the stored energy is given off, usually as heat. The model is not perfect, since a rubber band pulls harder the more it is stretched, while the attraction between particles weakens with distance. Even so, in both cases separating takes energy and coming together releases it.

This idea explains a great deal. During a phase change, such as ice melting, the added energy goes into pulling particles farther apart, so it is stored as potential energy rather than making the particles move faster. In a chemical reaction, atoms are separated from some partners and joined to new ones, so the potential energy stored in the bonds changes. The potential energy stored in the arrangement of atoms and electrons in chemical bonds is called chemical energy. Gasoline, food and the chemicals in a battery are valuable because their atoms are arranged in a way that stores a large amount of potential energy, which is released when the atoms are rearranged.

  • Energy is required to move an object against a force like gravity or the electrostatic force. This energy is stored in the system as potential energy.
    • Potential Energy – stored energy of an object due to its position in a system.
  • Particles are attracted to one another. Separating them requires energy, which is stored as potential energy because of where the particles are relative to one another.
    • The farther apart particles are, the more potential energy they have.
    • The closer together particles are, the less potential energy they have.
  • We can think of particles as though they are connected by a spring.
    • Pulling the particles apart takes energy, which is stored in the stretched spring.
    • When the particles come back together, that stored energy is released, usually as kinetic energy or heat.
  • Chemical energy is potential energy stored in the arrangement of atoms and electrons in chemical bonds. Fuels, food and batteries all store chemical energy.

Kinetic Energy

Kinetic Energy

Recall that the energy an object has due to its motion is its kinetic energy. In order to set an object in motion, energy must be given to it, and once the object is moving, it carries that energy with it.

Kinetic Energy – energy possessed by an object due to its motion.

The kinetic energy of an object depends on its mass and its speed. For instance, a bowling ball and a tennis ball rolling at the same speed do not have the same kinetic energy, since the heavier bowling ball has more. Speed matters even more than mass. Doubling the speed of an object multiplies its kinetic energy by four, which is why a car crash at 60 miles per hour is far more destructive than one at 30.

For particles, the important relationship is the one between speed and kinetic energy. When the speed of particles increases, their kinetic energy increases. When the speed of particles decreases, their kinetic energy decreases. Since temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample, heating a sample makes its particles move faster.

The particles around us move much faster than you might expect. The air in a classroom seems perfectly still, but the oxygen molecules in it move at an average speed of about 480 meters per second, almost twice the speed of a passenger jet. We feel no wind because each molecule travels only a tiny distance before it collides with another molecule and changes direction. At the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy, so lighter molecules must move faster to make up for their smaller mass. Hydrogen molecules in the same room move at an average of about 1,900 meters per second, four times faster than the much heavier oxygen molecules.

  • To put an object into motion, energy must be given to it. Once it is moving, this energy is called kinetic energy.
    • Kinetic Energy – energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • When the speed of particles increases, their kinetic energy increases.
  • When the speed of particles decreases, their kinetic energy decreases.
  • Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample (see the Temperature page).

Converting Between Potential and Kinetic Energy

Potential energy and kinetic energy can be converted back and forth. For instance, a roller coaster has no engine on its cars. A chain pulls the train slowly to the top of the first hill, and from then on the ride runs entirely on the energy stored during that climb. At the top of the hill, the train has its greatest potential energy and very little kinetic energy. As it plunges down, its potential energy decreases and its kinetic energy increases, so the train reaches its top speed at the bottom of the drop. As it climbs the next hill, the exchange runs in reverse.

According to the law of conservation of energy, the total of the two stays the same, so the potential energy lost on the way down is exactly the kinetic energy gained. It makes sense, then, that the taller the first hill, the faster the train is moving at the bottom. The train can never climb a hill taller than the one it started from, since it would need more potential energy than it was ever given. In a real coaster, friction and air resistance convert some of the energy into heat along the way. That energy is not destroyed, but it no longer moves the train, so each hill on a coaster must be a little lower than the one before it.

The same back-and-forth exchange takes place between particles. When a substance freezes, its particles move closer together and lose potential energy, and that energy is released as heat. In a chemical reaction, potential energy stored in bonds can be released as the kinetic energy of the products, so a burning fuel produces hot gases. Whenever a change involves energy, ask which kind of energy is changing. A change in kinetic energy shows up as a change in temperature. A change in potential energy shows up as a change in the arrangement of particles.

  • Potential energy and kinetic energy are constantly converted into one another.
    • As a roller coaster car rolls downhill, its potential energy decreases and its kinetic energy increases.
    • As it climbs, kinetic energy is converted back into potential energy.
    • The total amount of energy stays the same (the law of conservation of energy). Friction converts some of it into heat, but none is destroyed.
