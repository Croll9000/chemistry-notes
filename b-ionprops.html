Properties of Ionic Compounds

Ionic compounds are held together by the attraction between oppositely charged ions. That one fact explains their high melting points, their brittleness and when they conduct electricity.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

Properties of Ionic Compounds

Calcium oxide, also known as lime, melts at 2613 °C; in the 1800s, blocks of it were heated until they glowed to produce the bright “limelight” used in theaters. The calcium carbonate in seashells and the sodium fluoride in toothpaste look very different from lime, but as ionic compounds they share a set of properties that come from the way their ions are arranged and held together. Ionic bonds form between a metal and a nonmetal through a transfer of electrons. As a result, ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points and are solids at room temperature, they conduct electricity when dissolved in water or melted, and they are brittle, shattering or crumbling when struck.

  • Ionic compounds differ from covalent compounds in a number of ways.
    • Ionic bonds take place between a metal and a nonmetal.
    • Ionic bonds involve a transfer of electrons.
    • High melting and boiling points. Ionic compounds are solids at room temperature.
    • Can conduct electricity when dissolved in water or melted.
    • Brittle: they shatter or crumble when struck.
Cause: each ion is attracted to all of the oppositely charged ions around it. Separating the ions means overcoming a very large number of these strong attractions, so a lot of energy (a high temperature) is needed to melt or boil an ionic compound.
Both sodium chloride (NaCl) and magnesium chloride (MgCl2) are examples of ionic compounds. They are formed from a metal and a nonmetal. They are solids at room temperature, and when added to water, the resulting solutions can conduct electricity.
Part2

Crystal Lattice

Crystal Lattice

A grain of table salt half a millimeter across contains more than 5 × 1018 ions, and its shape is a nearly perfect cube. Ionic compounds do not exist as individual molecules. Instead, they form large, repeating, geometric arrangements of ions, in which every ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge. A crystal lattice is a large, three-dimensional, repeating arrangement of ions. The chemical formula of an ionic compound therefore does not show an exact number of atoms; it shows the simplest ratio of ions in the crystal lattice. MgCl2, for instance, means 1 Mg2+ ion for every 2 Cl− ions. This simplest ratio is called a formula unit. Because ionic compounds do not form molecules, we say “formula unit” instead of “molecule,” so 3NaCl means 3 formula units of NaCl.

  • Ionic compounds do not exist as individual, discrete molecules.
    • Instead, they form large, repeating, geometric arrangements of ions.
    • This arrangement is known as a crystal lattice.
  • Crystal Lattice – large, 3-dimensional, repeating arrangement of ions.
  • When the chemical formula for an ionic compound is given (ex: NaCl, MgCl2), it does not represent the exact number of atoms in the compound but rather the simplest ratio of ions in the crystal lattice.
    • NaCl: 1 Na+ ion for every 1 Cl− ion.
    • MgCl2: 1 Mg2+ ion for every 2 Cl− ions.
  • Formula unit – the simplest ratio of ions in an ionic compound, as shown by its chemical formula. One formula unit of NaCl is 1 Na+ ion and 1 Cl− ion.
    • Ionic compounds do not form molecules, so "formula unit" is used instead of "molecule."
      Example
      3NaCl means 3 formula units of NaCl.
Sodium chloride (NaCl) consists of many Na+ and Cl− ions arranged in an alternating, 3-dimensional pattern known as a crystal lattice. The cube shape of a salt grain comes from this cube-shaped arrangement of ions.

Brittleness

Ionic Compound Brittleness

A ceramic plate dropped onto a tile floor usually shatters, while an aluminum pan dropped from the same height only dents. The crystal lattice of an ionic compound is very stable as long as every positive ion is surrounded by negative ions and every negative ion by positive ions. However, ionic compounds are also very brittle. When a force shifts one layer of ions past another, ions with the same charge are placed next to each other and repel, so the layers push away from each other and the solid splits apart. Ceramics are made largely of ionic compounds, which is why they shatter.

  • The crystal lattice structure of an ionic compound is very stable as long as positive ions are surrounded by negative ions and vice versa.
  • However, ionic compounds are also very brittle.
    • When layers of ions try to slide past each other, like-charged ions (positive + positive, negative + negative) are placed next to each other and repel.
    • This causes the layers to push away from each other and break apart.
When the layers of an ionic compound are shifted over, same-charged ions begin to repel each other, causing the crystal lattice to crumble. This is why ionic compounds are brittle.

Electrolytes

Electrolytes

Solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity, but at 801 °C it melts into a liquid that conducts well enough to be used in factories that produce sodium metal. Electricity is the flow of charged particles from one point to another. In a metal wire these particles are electrons, but they can also be ions. Substances that conduct electricity through the movement of ions are called electrolytes, and there are three types: ionic compounds (salts), acids and bases. To conduct electricity, an electrolyte must be dissolved in a liquid such as water or melted, so that its ions are free to move. In a solid, the ions are locked in place in the crystal lattice, so solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity.

  • Electricity is the flow of charged particles from one point to another.
    • Usually these particles are electrons, as is the case in a metal wire.
    • However, these particles can also take the form of ions.
  • Some substances, when added to water, allow the resulting solution to conduct electricity. These are known as electrolytes.
  • Electrolytes – substances that conduct electricity through the movement of charged ions.
  • There are 3 types of electrolytes:
    • Ionic compounds (salts)
    • Acids
    • Bases
  • In order to conduct electricity, these substances must be dissolved in a liquid like water or melted to allow for the movement of ions.
    • In a solid, the ions are locked in place in the crystal lattice and cannot move, so solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity.
An aqueous solution of NaCl will conduct electricity. If a battery is connected to electrodes placed in the solution, Na+ ions will migrate to the negative electrode and Cl− ions will migrate to the positive electrode. Although different from electrons moving through a wire, this movement of charged ions is also an electric current.
