Properties of Ionic Compounds

Ionic compounds are held together by the attraction between oppositely charged ions. That one fact explains their high melting points, their brittleness and when they conduct electricity.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

Strong attractions between ions lead to high melting and boiling points.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

Table salt, the calcium carbonate in seashells and the sodium fluoride in toothpaste are all ionic compounds. Although they look very different, they share a set of properties that come from the way their ions are arranged and held together.

  • Ionic compounds differ from covalent compounds in a number of ways.
    • Ionic bonds take place between a metal and a nonmetal.
    • Ionic bonds involve a transfer of electrons.
    • High melting and boiling points. Ionic compounds are solids at room temperature.
    • Can conduct electricity when dissolved in water or melted.
    • Brittle: they shatter or crumble when struck.
Cause: each ion is attracted to all of the oppositely charged ions around it. Separating the ions means overcoming a very large number of these strong attractions, so a lot of energy (a high temperature) is needed to melt or boil an ionic compound.
Both sodium chloride (NaCl) and magnesium chloride (MgCl2) are examples of ionic compounds. They are formed from a metal and a nonmetal. They are solids at room temperature, and when added to water, the resulting solutions can conduct electricity.
Part2

Crystal Lattice

Ionic compounds are not made of separate molecules.

Crystal Lattice

A grain of table salt is a nearly perfect cube. Its shape is a clue to how the particles inside it are arranged: in a regular, repeating pattern that extends in every direction.

  • Ionic compounds do not exist as individual, discrete molecules. Instead, they form large, repeating, geometric arrangements of ions. This arrangement is known as a crystal lattice.
  • Crystal Lattice – large, 3-dimensional, repeating arrangement of ions.
  • When the chemical formula for an ionic compound is given (ex: NaCl, MgCl2), it does not represent the exact number of atoms in the compound but rather the simplest ratio of ions in the crystal lattice.
    • NaCl: 1 Na+ ion for every 1 Cl− ion.
    • MgCl2: 1 Mg2+ ion for every 2 Cl− ions.
Sodium chloride (NaCl) consists of many Na+ and Cl− ions arranged in an alternating, 3-dimensional pattern known as a crystal lattice. The cube shape of a salt grain comes from this cube-shaped arrangement of ions.

Part3

Brittleness

Shift the layers and like charges meet.

Ionic Compound Brittleness

A block of salt shatters when struck with a hammer, while a piece of copper simply bends. The difference comes down to what happens to the ions when their layers are forced to shift.

  • The crystal lattice structure of an ionic compound is very stable as long as positive ions are surrounded by negative ions and vice versa.
  • However, ionic compounds are also very brittle.
    • When layers of ions try to slide past each other, like-charged ions (positive + positive, negative + negative) are placed next to each other and repel.
    • This causes the layers to push away from each other and break apart.
When the layers of an ionic compound are shifted over, same-charged ions begin to repel each other, causing the crystal lattice to crumble. This is why ionic compounds are brittle.

Electrolytes

Moving ions can carry an electric current.

Electrolytes

Sports drinks advertise that they replace the electrolytes lost in sweat, and the body relies on dissolved ions to carry nerve signals. Pure water barely conducts electricity, but dissolving even a small amount of an ionic compound in it changes that.

  • Electricity is the flow of charged particles from one point to another.
    • Usually these particles are electrons, as is the case in a metal wire.
    • However, these particles can also take the form of ions.
  • Some substances, when added to water, allow the resulting solution to conduct electricity. These are known as electrolytes.
  • Electrolytes – substances that conduct electricity through the movement of charged ions.
  • There are 3 types of electrolytes:
    • Ionic compounds (salts)
    • Acids
    • Bases
  • In order to conduct electricity, these substances must be dissolved in a liquid like water or melted to allow for the movement of ions.
    • In a solid, the ions are locked in place in the crystal lattice and cannot move, so solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity.
An aqueous solution of NaCl will conduct electricity. If a battery is connected to electrodes placed in the solution, Na+ ions will migrate to the negative electrode and Cl− ions will migrate to the positive electrode. Although different from electrons moving through a wire, this movement of charged ions is also an electric current.
