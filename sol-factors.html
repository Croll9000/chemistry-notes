Polarity and Solubility

Polarity: “Like Dissolves Like”

A bottle of oil-and-vinegar salad dressing can be shaken until it looks evenly mixed, but within a few minutes it separates into two layers again, with the oil floating on top. Vinegar is mostly water, a polar substance, while oil is made of large nonpolar molecules. Shaking breaks the oil into tiny droplets, but it cannot make the oil dissolve, and the droplets soon find one another again. The dressing shows that a solvent cannot dissolve just anything placed in it.

The amount of a substance that a solvent can dissolve is known as its solubility: the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature and pressure. The solubility of a solute depends on three things: the nature of the solute and solvent, the temperature, and, for gases, the pressure.

The nature of the solute and solvent comes down to polarity, which is summed up in the rule “like dissolves like.” Polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents such as water, while nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents such as hexane (C6H14) or benzene (C6H6).

If we analyze the attractions involved, it becomes clear why this rule works. For a solute to dissolve, the solute particles must be pulled apart from one another, and the solvent particles must make room for them. A solute dissolves when the new attractions between solute and solvent particles are similar in strength to the attractions they replace. Water molecules attract one another strongly through hydrogen bonding, and they attract ions and other polar molecules strongly as well, so polar and ionic solutes can take a place among them. Nonpolar oil molecules, however, offer water almost nothing to attract. The water molecules stay attracted to one another and squeeze the oil out, which is why the oil collects into its own layer.

Nonpolar solvents follow the same logic in reverse. Iodine, a nonpolar molecule, barely dissolves in water but dissolves readily in hexane, turning it purple, because the weak attractions between iodine and hexane molecules are similar to the weak attractions each already has for its own kind. This is also why grease stains will not wash out with water alone, and why dry cleaners use nonpolar solvents to remove them.

  • Solubility – the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature and pressure.
  • The solubility of a solute depends on:
    • the nature of the solute and solvent (polarity),
    • the temperature,
    • the pressure (for gases).
  • “Like dissolves like”:
    • Polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents, such as water.
    • Nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents, such as hexane (C6H14) or benzene (C6H6).
  • A solute dissolves when the attractions between solute and solvent particles are similar to the attractions they replace. The partial charges of water attract ions and polar molecules, but they attract nonpolar molecules only weakly, so nonpolar substances do not dissolve in water.

Temperature and Solubility

Temperature

Trout, a fish common in the cold streams of upstate New York, cannot survive for long in warm water, and the reason has nothing to do with the heat itself. Water at 0 °C can hold about 14.6 mg of dissolved oxygen per liter, but water at 30 °C can hold only about 7.6 mg per liter, roughly half as much. On a hot summer day, a shallow stream warmed by the sun simply cannot hold enough dissolved oxygen for the fish that live in it. Power plants that release warm water into rivers cause the same problem, which is why their discharges are regulated.

The oxygen in a stream shows that as the temperature increases, the solubility of gases in water decreases. Raising the temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the particles. A dissolved gas molecule held among water molecules only by weak attractions is more likely to have enough energy to break free and escape from the surface of the liquid. This is also why a warm soda goes flat faster than a cold one.

Solids generally behave the opposite way. A glass of iced tea can dissolve only a spoonful or two of sugar before grains collect on the bottom, while the same tea served hot dissolves far more. Faster-moving water molecules strike the surface of a solid more often and with more force, pulling more of its particles away. Thus, as the temperature increases, the solubility of most solids in water increases. The word “most” matters: a few solids, such as cerium(III) sulfate, become less soluble as they are heated, so the effect of temperature on a particular solid must always be checked with data such as a solubility curve.

  • As temperature increases, the solubility of most solids in water increases.
  • As temperature increases, the solubility of gases in water decreases.
    • Dissolved gas molecules gain kinetic energy and escape from the liquid.
    • This is why a warm soda goes flat faster than a cold one, and why warm water holds less dissolved oxygen for fish.

Pressure and Solubility

Pressure

A sealed bottle of soda shows no bubbles at all, yet the moment the cap is twisted off, a stream of bubbles rises from the bottom. Soda is bottled with carbon dioxide gas held above the liquid at a pressure several times greater than the air around us. Opening the bottle releases this gas, the pressure above the liquid drops to ordinary air pressure, and the extra carbon dioxide that the soda can no longer hold comes out of solution.

The gas particles above a liquid are constantly colliding with its surface, and some of them enter the liquid with each collision, while some dissolved particles escape back out. When the pressure of the gas is increased, the particles are packed more closely, more of them strike the surface each second, and more of them enter the liquid. The dissolved gas soon reaches a new balance, with more gas particles in the liquid than before. Therefore, as the pressure of a gas above a liquid increases, the solubility of the gas increases.

The same idea matters to scuba divers. Deep under water, a diver breathes air at a high pressure, so extra nitrogen dissolves in the blood. If the diver rises to the surface too quickly, the pressure drops and the nitrogen comes out of solution as bubbles in the body, a dangerous condition known as “the bends.” Divers avoid it by rising slowly, giving the extra gas time to leave the blood through the lungs.

Solids and liquids are not pushed into a solvent this way, because their particles are not free-moving gas particles colliding with the surface. Thus, pressure has almost no effect on the solubility of solids and liquids. Combining the effects of temperature and pressure, it makes sense that gases are most soluble in water at low temperature and high pressure, the exact conditions inside a cold, sealed bottle of soda.

  • As the pressure of a gas above a liquid increases, the solubility of the gas increases.
  • Pressure has almost no effect on the solubility of solids and liquids.
  • Gases are most soluble in water at low temperature and high pressure.
