Factors Affecting Solubility

How much solute dissolves depends on polarity (“like dissolves like”), temperature and, for gases, pressure.

Part1

Polarity and Solubility

A bottle of oil-and-vinegar salad dressing separates into two layers within minutes of being shaken. Vinegar is mostly water, a polar substance, and oil is made of large nonpolar molecules. The water molecules attract one another far more strongly than they attract the oil, so the two never form a solution. In this section we will discuss how the polarity of the solute and solvent decides whether a solution forms.

Polarity: “Like Dissolves Like”

Every solvent can dissolve only a certain amount of a given solute. How much dissolves depends on the nature of the solute and solvent, the temperature and the pressure. The first of these, the nature of the substances, comes down to polarity.

  • Solubility – the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature and pressure.
  • The solubility of a solute depends on:
    • the nature of the solute and solvent (polarity),
    • the temperature,
    • the pressure (for gases).
  • “Like dissolves like”:
    • Polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents, such as water.
    • Nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents, such as hexane (C6H14) or benzene (C6H6).
  • A solute dissolves when the attractions between solute and solvent particles are similar to the attractions they replace. The partial charges of water attract ions and polar molecules, but they attract nonpolar molecules only weakly, so nonpolar substances do not dissolve in water.

Part2

Temperature and Solubility

A glass of iced tea can dissolve only a spoonful or two of sugar before grains collect on the bottom, while the same tea served hot dissolves far more. A can of soda behaves the opposite way: a warm soda goes flat faster than a cold one, because carbon dioxide escapes from warm water more easily. In this section we will discuss how temperature affects the solubility of solids and gases in water.

Temperature

Raising the temperature gives every particle in a solution more kinetic energy. For a solid solute, the faster water molecules pull more particles away from the crystal. For a gas, the faster dissolved gas molecules escape from the liquid.

  • As temperature increases, the solubility of most solids in water increases.
  • As temperature increases, the solubility of gases in water decreases.
    • Dissolved gas molecules gain kinetic energy and escape from the liquid.
    • This is why a warm soda goes flat faster than a cold one, and why warm water holds less dissolved oxygen for fish.
Part3

Pressure and Solubility

Soda is bottled under carbon dioxide at a pressure several times greater than the air around us, which forces extra carbon dioxide into the liquid. When the cap is twisted off, the pressure above the liquid drops and the extra gas comes out of solution as a stream of bubbles. In this section we will discuss how pressure affects the solubility of gases.

Pressure

Pressure pushes gas molecules into a liquid. The more gas molecules strike the surface of the liquid, the more of them enter it and stay dissolved. Solids and liquids are not compressed in this way, so pressure has almost no effect on them.

  • As the pressure of a gas above a liquid increases, the solubility of the gas increases.
  • Pressure has almost no effect on the solubility of solids and liquids.
  • Gases are most soluble in water at low temperature and high pressure.
