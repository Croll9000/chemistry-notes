Density & Identifying Substances

Every piece of matter has mass and volume. Divide one by the other and you get density, a property that helps identify substances.

PS1.AHS-PS1-3
Part1

Mass and Volume

Mass is the amount of matter. Volume is the amount of space it occupies.

Mass

  • Mass – the amount of matter (“stuff”) in an object.
    • Unit – gram (g) or kilogram (kg)
      • 1 kg = 1000 g
    • Measuring device – electronic balance, triple beam balance
At a fundamental level, the mass of an object depends on the number and type of particles that make up the object.
We can use an electronic balance to measure the amount of mass in an object. In chemistry we usually measure mass in grams (g).

Volume

  • Volume – measurement of how much space an object takes up
    • Unit – cubic meter (m³), cubic centimeter (cm³), milliliter (mL), liter (L)
      • 1 L = 1000 mL
    • Measuring device – ruler or meter stick (regular solids), graduated cylinder (liquids)
  • Generally, m³ and cm³ are used for solids while L and mL are used for liquids.
    • 1 cm³ = 1 mL
  • For a rectangular solid: V = l × w × h
We can use a graduated cylinder to measure the volume of a liquid. Read the bottom of the curved surface (the meniscus) at eye level.
A cube 1 cm on each side has a volume of 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm = 1 cm³, which is the same as 1 mL.

Displacement Method

The volume of a box can be found by measuring its length, width and height. Objects such as a rock, a key or a ring do not have regular shapes, so a different method is needed.

  • Displacement Method for Determining Volume – objects submerged in water will “displace” (push aside) a volume of water equal to their own volume.
    • Use it for objects with irregular shapes.
    1. Measure out a known volume of liquid in a graduated cylinder.
    2. Add an object to the liquid.
      • Water will be displaced and the water level will rise.
    3. Record the new volume on the graduated cylinder.
    4. Subtract: final volume − initial volume = the volume of the object.
After adding an object to the liquid, the water level rose from 8 mL to 12 mL. Therefore the volume of the object is 12 mL − 8 mL = 4 mL.
Part2

Density

Density compares mass to volume: how tightly packed the matter is.

Density

A piece of wood floats in water while a steel bolt of the same size sinks. Oil spilled on the ocean spreads across the surface instead of mixing in. Each of these observations can be explained by comparing how much mass is packed into a given amount of space.

  • Density – the amount of mass per unit volume in a substance.
    • Unit – grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³) or grams per milliliter (g/mL)
    • Think of density as “how tightly packed” matter is within a given space.
    • Density is linked to particle spacing.
      • Solids and liquids have particles packed close together, so they usually have high densities.
      • Gas particles are very far apart, so gases have very low densities.
      • Ex: hydrogen and oxygen in the table below

Calculating Density

Since density is the amount of mass per unit volume of an object, we can calculate density by taking an object’s mass and dividing it by its volume.

D=mV
D – density (g/cm³ or g/mL)m – mass (g)V – volume (cm³ or mL)

The density formula is not on the Reference Table, so memorize it. You can rearrange it: m = D × V, and V = m ÷ D.

Example: An object with a mass of 2.5 g and a volume of 8.0 cm³ would have a density of:
D = mV = 2.5 g8.0 cm³ = 0.31 g/cm³

Density Is a Property of a Substance

  • Every pure substance has its own unique density.
    • Knowing the density of an unknown object lets you identify what substance it is made of (Part 3 of this page).
  • Density does not depend on the size of the sample.
    • Ex: a small piece and a large piece of the same substance have the same density.
  • Density decides whether an object floats or sinks.
    • An object floats in a liquid if it is less dense than the liquid.
    • An object sinks if it is more dense than the liquid.
    • Water has a density of about 1.00 g/mL.

Aluminum2.70
Iron7.87
Hydrogen8.2 × 10−5
Carbon (graphite)2.25

Note: Only pure substances have a characteristic density, since their proportions are fixed. Mixtures do not have a characteristic density, since their proportions can vary.

Part3

Identifying Substances

Use intensive properties like density, melting point and boiling point to find out what an unknown is.

Identifying Substances

Jewelers and investigators must sometimes determine whether a piece of gold is genuine. Pure gold has a density of 19.3 g/cm3, so a fake made of a cheaper metal with a gold coating will have a noticeably different density. Properties that are unique to a substance allow us to determine its identity.

  • Certain properties are unique to each substance.
    • They can be used to determine the identity of a given substance.
  • Two types of properties we can use to identify substances are:
    • Intensive Physical Properties – properties that do not change with the size of the substance. Ex: density, melting point, boiling point
    • Chemical Properties – properties that describe the way that a substance reacts. Ex: flammable, corrosive, oxidizer

Mass or volume alone can’t identify a substance: a big piece of aluminum and a small piece of gold could have the same mass.

Identifying Substances Using Density

Density is an intensive physical property, meaning it does not change with the size of a sample. That makes it a good choice for identifying an unknown substance.

Ex: A student discovers an unknown substance in the lab. The substance has a mass of 4.44 grams and a volume of 2.40 cm³. By themselves, the mass and volume are not enough to identify the unknown. However, density is the same for a substance regardless of sample size:

Identifying Substances Using Melting Point and Boiling Point

The melting point and boiling point of a substance are intensive physical properties, meaning they do not change with the size of the sample. That makes them a good choice for determining the identity of an unknown substance.

Ex: A student discovers an unknown substance in the lab. They measure its melting point to be 54.36 K and its boiling point to be 90.2 K. Comparing to the table, the unknown is oxygen.

Notice that oxygen and fluorine have very similar melting points. Using two properties (melting point and boiling point) makes the identification certain.
