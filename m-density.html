Density & Identifying Substances

Every piece of matter has mass and volume. Divide one by the other and you get density, a property that helps identify substances.

Part1

Mass and Volume

Mass

Mass is the amount of matter in an object. Its unit is the gram (g) or kilogram (kg), where 1 kg = 1000 g, and it is measured with an electronic balance or a triple beam balance.

Mass – the amount of matter (“stuff”) in an object.

Weight, on the other hand, is the pull of gravity on an object. For instance, an astronaut standing on the Moon weighs only about one-sixth as much as on Earth, because the Moon’s gravity is weaker. However, the astronaut has not lost any matter. The same bones, muscles and spacesuit are still there, made of the same atoms, so the astronaut’s mass has not changed at all.

This is why a triple beam balance would give the same reading on the Moon as on Earth. The balance compares an object with known masses on the other side of a beam, so gravity pulls equally on both sides. A bathroom scale, which measures the pull of gravity, would give a much lower reading on the Moon.

Since mass measures the matter itself, it is the quantity that chemists rely on. The mass of a sample stays the same whether it is hot or cold, solid or melted, here on Earth or out in space. This is why Lavoisier measured mass so carefully in his sealed containers when he wanted to know what happened to matter during a reaction.

  • Mass – the amount of matter (“stuff”) in an object.
    • Unit – gram (g) or kilogram (kg)
      • 1 kg = 1000 g
    • Measuring device – electronic balance, triple beam balance
At a fundamental level, the mass of an object depends on the number and type of particles that make up the object.
We can use an electronic balance to measure the amount of mass in an object. In chemistry we usually measure mass in grams (g).

Volume

The amount of space an object takes up is its volume. Its units are the cubic meter (m³), cubic centimeter (cm³), milliliter (mL) and liter (L), where 1 L = 1000 mL. Generally, m³ and cm³ are used for solids, and L and mL are used for liquids.

Volume – the amount of space an object takes up.

The two kinds of units are connected. The liter was originally defined as the volume of a cube 10 cm on each side. That cube holds 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 cubic centimeters. A liter also equals 1000 milliliters, so each milliliter must be exactly the same as one cubic centimeter. Thus, 1 cm³ = 1 mL, and the two kinds of units can be used interchangeably.

The method for measuring volume depends on the shape of the object. A solid with a regular shape, such as a rectangular block, is measured with a ruler, and its volume is calculated by multiplying its three dimensions:

V = l × w × h

For example, a block 2.0 cm long, 3.0 cm wide and 1.5 cm high has a volume of 2.0 × 3.0 × 1.5 = 9.0 cm³. Liquids are measured with a graduated cylinder, read at the bottom of the curved surface of the liquid.

  • Volume – the amount of space an object takes up.
    • Unit – cubic meter (m³), cubic centimeter (cm³), milliliter (mL), liter (L)
      • 1 L = 1000 mL
    • Measuring device – ruler or meter stick (regular solids), graduated cylinder (liquids)
  • Generally, m³ and cm³ are used for solids while L and mL are used for liquids.
    • 1 cm³ = 1 mL
  • For a rectangular solid: V = l × w × h
We can use a graduated cylinder to measure the volume of a liquid. Read the bottom of the curved surface (the meniscus) at eye level.
A cube 1 cm on each side has a volume of 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm = 1 cm³, which is the same as 1 mL.

Displacement Method

A rock, a key or a crown has no simple shape, so its volume cannot be calculated from a few ruler measurements. According to a story told for over two thousand years, the Greek scientist Archimedes faced exactly this problem. A king wanted to know whether his new crown was pure gold, and Archimedes was asked to find out without damaging it.

As he stepped into a full bathtub, Archimedes noticed water spilling over the side. He reasoned that his body was pushing aside an amount of water equal to its own volume, and he concluded that he could measure the crown the same way. Legend says he was so excited that he ran through the streets shouting “Eureka!,” Greek for “I have found it!”

Archimedes’ idea is now a standard laboratory technique called the displacement method. An object submerged in water displaces (pushes aside) a volume of water equal to its own volume.

To use the method, a known volume of water is measured in a graduated cylinder. The object is then lowered into the water, the water level rises, and the new volume is recorded. The volume of the object is the difference between the two readings:

volume of object = final volume − initial volume

If the water rises from 20.0 mL to 26.5 mL, the object has a volume of 6.5 mL, which is the same as 6.5 cm³. Be careful that the object is completely under the water. Any part sticking out above the surface is not displacing water, and the volume you calculate will be too small.

  • Displacement Method for Determining Volume – objects submerged in water will “displace” (push aside) a volume of water equal to their own volume.
    • Use it for objects with irregular shapes.
    1. Measure out a known volume of liquid in a graduated cylinder.
    2. Add an object to the liquid.
      • Water will be displaced and the water level will rise.
    3. Record the new volume on the graduated cylinder.
    4. Subtract: final volume − initial volume = the volume of the object.
After adding an object to the liquid, the water level rose from 8 mL to 12 mL. Therefore the volume of the object is 12 mL − 8 mL = 4 mL.
Part2

Density

Density

We now have the two measurements needed to describe how tightly matter is packed. How much mass is packed into a given volume is referred to as density. Its unit is grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³) or grams per milliliter (g/mL).

Density – the amount of mass per unit volume in a substance.

An object with a low density has a small amount of matter spread through a large space. An object with a high density has a large amount of matter packed into a small space. For the same volume, the denser object always has the greater mass.

Consider a can of regular soda and a can of diet soda placed in a cooler of ice water. The regular soda sinks, and the diet soda floats. The cans are the same size and made of the same aluminum, so the difference must be inside. Regular soda contains about 39 g of dissolved sugar. Diet soda, on the other hand, uses a tiny amount of a far sweeter substitute. The sugar adds mass without adding much volume, so the regular soda has the higher density.

Density is also linked to how far apart particles are. Solids and liquids have particles packed close together, so they usually have high densities. Gas particles are very far apart, so gases have very low densities. Liquid water is about 1.00 g/mL. The air in the same room is only about 0.0012 g/mL, around 800 times less dense. For this reason, a liter bottle of water feels heavy, and a liter of air seems to weigh nothing at all.

  • Density – the amount of mass per unit volume in a substance.
    • Unit – grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³) or grams per milliliter (g/mL)
    • Think of density as “how tightly packed” matter is within a given space.
    • Density is linked to particle spacing.
      • Solids and liquids have particles packed close together, so they usually have high densities.
      • Gas particles are very far apart, so gases have very low densities.

Calculating Density

Since density is the amount of mass per unit volume of an object, we can calculate density by taking an object’s mass and dividing it by its volume.

D=mV
D – density (g/cm³ or g/mL)m – mass (g)V – volume (cm³ or mL)

Memorize the density formula. You can rearrange it: m = D × V, and V = mD.

Example
An object with a mass of 2.5 g and a volume of 8.0 cm³ would have a density of:
D = mV = 2.5 g8.0 cm³ = 0.31 g/cm³

Density Is a Property of a Substance

Every pure substance has its own density at a given temperature. Why should that be? The density of a substance depends on two things: how heavy its particles are and how tightly they are packed. Both are set by what the substance is. Cutting a sample in half does not change either one, so it does not change the density. Thus, density does not depend on the size of the sample. A small chip of ice and an enormous iceberg have the same density, and we can use the density of an unknown object to help identify what it is made of.

Density also decides whether an object floats or sinks, and we can see why by comparing the object with the liquid it pushes aside. When an object is placed in water, it displaces some of the water. If the object is less dense than the water, it weighs less than an equal volume of water. The upward push of the water is greater than the object’s weight, so the object floats before it is fully under the surface. If the object is denser, it weighs more than the water it displaces, and it sinks.

  • An object floats in a liquid if it is less dense than the liquid.
  • An object sinks if it is more dense than the liquid.

Water has a density of about 1.00 g/mL, so wood (about 0.5 g/cm³) floats high in water and an iron bolt (7.87 g/cm³) sinks straight to the bottom.

The closer the two densities are, the deeper a floating object sits. Ice has a density of about 0.92 g/cm³, and seawater is about 1.03 g/cm³. Ice floats, but only barely, so about nine-tenths of an iceberg sits below the water. This is the hidden danger that sank the Titanic in 1912.

Water is unusual in one way. For almost every other substance, the solid is denser than the liquid and sinks in it. Since ice floats, lakes freeze from the top down, and the layer of ice on the surface insulates the water below, allowing fish to survive the winter.

  • Every pure substance has its own unique density.
    • Knowing the density of an unknown object lets you identify what substance it is made of.
  • Density does not depend on the size of the sample.
    Example
    A small piece and a large piece of the same substance have the same density.
  • Density decides whether an object floats or sinks.
    • An object floats in a liquid if it is less dense than the liquid.
    • An object sinks if it is more dense than the liquid.
    • Water has a density of about 1.00 g/mL.

Identifying Substances

Identifying Substances

During the California Gold Rush, many miners were fooled by pyrite, a shiny, brass-yellow mineral that became known as “fool’s gold.” Color and shine could not tell the two apart. Other properties could.

Certain properties are unique to each substance, and we can use them to determine the identity of a given substance. Two kinds are especially useful. Intensive physical properties, such as density, melting point and boiling point, stay the same for any size of sample. Chemical properties, such as being flammable or corrosive, describe the way a substance reacts.

Gold has a density of 19.3 g/cm³, nearly four times that of pyrite, so a nugget of gold feels far heavier than a piece of pyrite the same size. Gold is also soft enough to be dented with a knife. Pyrite is hard and brittle, and it shatters. Jewelers still use a chemical property to test gold. A drop of nitric acid dissolves many other metals but leaves gold untouched, and this is the origin of the expression “the acid test.”

Using more than one property makes an identification much more reliable. Two different substances can share a similar color or even a similar melting point. The chance that they share several properties at once is very small.

  • Certain properties are unique to each substance.
    • They can be used to determine the identity of a given substance.
  • Two types of properties we can use to identify substances are:
    • Intensive Physical Properties – properties which do not change with the size of the sample.
      Example
      Density, melting point, boiling point
    • Chemical Properties – characteristics of a substance that describe the way it reacts.
      Example
      Flammable, corrosive, oxidizer

Mass or volume alone can’t identify a substance: a big piece of aluminum and a small piece of gold could have the same mass.

Density is an intensive physical property, meaning it does not change with the size of a sample. That makes it a good choice for identifying an unknown substance.

Identifying Substances Using Melting Point and Boiling Point

The melting point and boiling point of a substance are intensive physical properties, meaning they do not change with the size of the sample. That makes them a good choice for determining the identity of an unknown substance.

Example
A student discovers an unknown substance in the lab. They measure its melting point to be 54.36 K and its boiling point to be 90.2 K. Comparing to the table, the unknown is oxygen.
