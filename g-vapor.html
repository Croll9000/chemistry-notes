Vapor Pressure

Vapor pressure is the pressure of the vapor above a liquid. It increases with temperature and is higher for liquids with weaker intermolecular forces. A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the pressure on its surface.

Vapor Pressure

At 25 °C, propanone, the main ingredient in many nail polish removers, has a vapor pressure of about 30 kPa, almost ten times that of water at the same temperature (3.2 kPa). Molecules escape far more easily from a liquid whose particles attract one another weakly. In this section, we will discuss vapor pressure and the factors that change it.

Vapor Pressure

A glass of water left on a table slowly disappears, even though it never comes close to 100 °C. Some molecules at the surface always have enough kinetic energy to break away from their neighbors and enter the gas phase. In a sealed container, those molecules cannot escape, and they build up a pressure of their own.

  • Vapor – the gas phase of a substance that is usually a liquid or solid at room temperature.
  • Vapor Pressure – the pressure exerted by the vapor above a liquid that is in equilibrium with its condensed phase.
    • In a closed container, molecules evaporate from the surface and some of them return to the liquid (condense).
    • When evaporation and condensation happen at equal rates, the amount of vapor stays the same: the liquid and vapor are in phase equilibrium. The pressure of the vapor at that point is the vapor pressure.
  • As temperature increases, vapor pressure increases, because more molecules have enough kinetic energy to escape into the gas phase.
  • Substances with weaker intermolecular forces have higher vapor pressures at the same temperature, because their molecules escape more easily.
  • Vapor pressure depends only on the substance and its temperature, not on the amount of liquid. For example, a puddle and a lake of water at 20 °C have the same vapor pressure.
  • Stronger intermolecular forces hold molecules more tightly in the liquid phase, so fewer escape into the gas phase and the vapor pressure is lower. Weaker intermolecular forces hold molecules less tightly, so more escape and the vapor pressure is higher.

Vapor Pressure Curves

At sea level, ethanol boils at 78 °C. At that same temperature, water has a vapor pressure of only about 44 kPa, less than half of the air pressure, so it is still far from boiling. Each liquid’s vapor pressure rises with temperature along its own curve, and the curves can be read like a map. In this section, we will read vapor pressure curves and use them to compare intermolecular forces.

Reading Vapor Pressure Curves

A vapor pressure curve shows the vapor pressure of a liquid at every temperature. Placing the curves of several liquids on one graph shows at a glance which liquid evaporates most easily and which has the strongest attractions between its molecules.

  • A vapor pressure curve shows how the vapor pressure of a substance changes with temperature.
  • Steps to reading a vapor pressure curve:
    1. Find the given temperature on the x-axis.
    2. Move up to the curve of the substance.
    3. Move across to the y-axis.
    4. Read the vapor pressure and write it with units.
    • To find the temperature at a given vapor pressure, reverse the steps: start on the y-axis, move across to the curve, then down to the x-axis.
  • To compare liquids, compare their curves at the same temperature. The liquid with the higher vapor pressure has the weaker intermolecular forces.
  • For the four liquids below, intermolecular forces increase in this order: propanone < ethanol < water < ethanoic acid.

Vapor Pressure and Boiling

On the summit of Denali in Alaska, 6,190 m above sea level, the air pressure is less than half of its sea-level value, and water boils at only about 79 °C. A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the pressure pushing down on its surface, and with less air above, water reaches that point at a much lower temperature. In this section, we will discuss the connection between vapor pressure and boiling.

Vapor Pressure and Boiling

Bubbles in boiling water are pockets of water vapor. A bubble can only form inside the liquid if the vapor inside it pushes outward as hard as the air pushes down on the liquid’s surface. Raise the pressure on the surface and the liquid must get hotter before it can boil.

  • Boiling Point – the temperature at which the vapor pressure of a liquid equals the pressure on its surface (the atmospheric pressure).
    • On a vapor pressure curve, the boiling point is where the curve crosses the pressure on the surface.
  • Normal Boiling Point – the boiling point at standard pressure, 101.3 kPa (1 atm). Water’s normal boiling point is 100 °C.
  • As a liquid is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and more of them escape into the gas phase, so the vapor pressure increases. When the vapor pressure becomes equal to the atmospheric pressure pushing down on the liquid, the vapor can push back against the atmosphere and the liquid begins to boil.
  • When the pressure on the surface of a liquid is lower, its boiling point is lower. At high altitude, water boils below 100 °C, so food cooks more slowly.
  • When the pressure on the surface of a liquid is higher, its boiling point is higher. A pressure cooker raises the pressure, so the water stays liquid above 100 °C and food cooks faster.
  • Liquids with weaker intermolecular forces have higher vapor pressures, so they reach the surface pressure at lower temperatures. Therefore, liquids with weaker intermolecular forces have lower boiling points.
