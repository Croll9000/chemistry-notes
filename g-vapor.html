Vapor Pressure

Vapor pressure is the pressure of the vapor above a liquid. It increases with temperature and is higher for liquids with weaker intermolecular forces. A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the pressure on its surface.

Vapor Pressure

Vapor Pressure

Even below its boiling point, a liquid is always slowly turning into a gas. Some molecules at the surface always have enough kinetic energy to break away from their neighbors and enter the gas phase. The gas phase of a substance that is usually a liquid at room temperature is known as a vapor. For instance, a glass of water left on a table slowly disappears, even though it never comes close to 100 °C. In an open glass, the vapor drifts away. In a sealed container, however, it cannot escape, and it builds up a pressure of its own.

Vapor – the gas phase of a substance that is usually a liquid or solid at room temperature.

In a closed container, molecules evaporate from the surface of the liquid, and at the same time some of the vapor molecules strike the surface and return to the liquid, or condense. At first, evaporation is faster than condensation, so the amount of vapor grows. As more vapor collects, condensation speeds up until the two happen at equal rates. The liquid and its vapor are then in phase equilibrium, and the amount of vapor no longer changes. The pressure exerted by the vapor at this point is called the vapor pressure of the liquid.

Vapor Pressure – the pressure exerted by the vapor above a liquid that is in equilibrium with its condensed phase.

Vapor pressure depends only on the substance and its temperature, not on how much liquid there is. A puddle and a lake of water at 20 °C have the same vapor pressure.

  • Vapor – the gas phase of a substance that is usually a liquid or solid at room temperature.
  • In a closed container, molecules evaporate from the surface of the liquid, and some of the vapor molecules return to the liquid (condense).
  • At first, evaporation is faster than condensation, so the amount of vapor grows. As more vapor collects, condensation speeds up until the two happen at equal rates. The liquid and vapor are then in phase equilibrium.
  • Vapor Pressure – the pressure exerted by the vapor above a liquid that is in equilibrium with its condensed phase.
  • Vapor pressure depends only on the substance and its temperature, not on the amount of liquid. A puddle and a lake of water at 20 °C have the same vapor pressure.

Vapor Pressure and Temperature

Consider a pot of water warming on a stove. At 25 °C, the water vapor above it has a pressure of only 3.2 kPa. At 80 °C, it reaches about 47 kPa, and at 100 °C, it equals the full 101.3 kPa of the air.

The reason lies in the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. Raising the temperature increases the fraction of particles with high kinetic energy. In a liquid, these are the molecules that have enough energy to escape the attractions of their neighbors. At a higher temperature, a larger fraction of the molecules can escape into the vapor, so more vapor collects above the liquid before evaporation and condensation balance. More molecules in the vapor means more collisions with the walls of the container. Thus, as the temperature increases, the vapor pressure increases.

  • As temperature increases, vapor pressure increases.
    • At a higher temperature, the molecules have a higher average kinetic energy, so a larger fraction of them have enough energy to escape the attractions of their neighbors.
    • More molecules in the vapor means more collisions with the walls of the container, so the vapor pressure is higher.

Vapor Pressure and Intermolecular Forces

At 25 °C, propanone, the main ingredient in many nail polish removers, has a vapor pressure of about 30 kPa. Water at the same temperature has a vapor pressure of only 3.2 kPa, almost ten times lower. The difference comes from the attractions between the molecules.

Stronger intermolecular forces hold the molecules more tightly in the liquid, so fewer escape into the gas phase, and the vapor pressure is lower. Weaker intermolecular forces hold the molecules less tightly, so more of them escape. Thus, at the same temperature, a substance with weaker intermolecular forces has a higher vapor pressure. Water molecules form hydrogen bonds with one another, and propanone molecules do not, so water evaporates far more slowly.

  • Substances with weaker intermolecular forces have higher vapor pressures at the same temperature.
    • Stronger intermolecular forces hold molecules more tightly in the liquid phase, so fewer escape into the gas phase and the vapor pressure is lower.
    • Weaker intermolecular forces hold molecules less tightly, so more escape and the vapor pressure is higher.

Reading Vapor Pressure Curves

A vapor pressure curve shows how the vapor pressure of a substance changes with temperature. Placing the curves of several liquids on one graph shows at a glance which liquid evaporates most easily and which has the strongest attractions between its molecules. At 78 °C, for example, the vapor pressure of ethanol has already reached 101.3 kPa, but water’s is only about 44 kPa. To read the vapor pressure of a substance at a given temperature:

  1. Find the given temperature on the x-axis.
  2. Move up to the curve of the substance.
  3. Move across to the y-axis.
  4. Read the vapor pressure and write it with units.

To find the temperature at which a substance has a given vapor pressure, the same steps are followed in reverse:

  1. Find the given vapor pressure on the y-axis.
  2. Move across to the curve of the substance.
  3. Move down to the x-axis.
  4. Read the temperature and write it with units.

To compare liquids, compare their curves at the same temperature. The liquid with the higher vapor pressure has the weaker intermolecular forces. For the four liquids on the graph, the intermolecular forces increase in the order propanone < ethanol < water < ethanoic acid.

Vapor Pressure and Boiling

Vapor Pressure and Boiling

The bubbles that form in boiling water are pockets of water vapor. As a liquid is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy, more of them escape into the gas phase, and the vapor pressure rises. A bubble of vapor can only form inside the liquid when the vapor inside it pushes outward as hard as the air pushes down on the surface of the liquid. When the vapor pressure becomes equal to the pressure on the surface, bubbles form throughout the liquid and it begins to boil.

The boiling point can therefore be described in terms of vapor pressure. The boiling point at standard pressure is referred to as the normal boiling point.

Boiling Point – the temperature at which the vapor pressure of a liquid equals the pressure on its surface (the atmospheric pressure).

Normal Boiling Point – the boiling point at standard pressure, 101.3 kPa (1 atm). Water’s normal boiling point is 100 °C.

Since the boiling point depends on the pressure on the surface, it changes with altitude. On the summit of Denali in Alaska, 6,190 m above sea level, the air pressure is less than half of its sea-level value, and water boils at only about 79 °C. When the pressure on the surface is lower, the boiling point is lower.

Raising the pressure does the opposite. A car’s cooling system is sealed and kept under pressure, so the coolant does not boil until about 120 °C, well above the temperature of the hot engine. When the pressure on the surface is higher, the boiling point is higher. Mechanics warn never to open the radiator cap on a hot engine. The sudden drop in pressure lowers the boiling point below the coolant’s temperature, and it can boil violently out of the radiator.

The boiling point also depends on the intermolecular forces. Liquids with weaker intermolecular forces have higher vapor pressures, so they reach the pressure on their surface at lower temperatures. Thus, liquids with weaker intermolecular forces have lower boiling points. To find a boiling point from a vapor pressure curve:

  1. Find the pressure on the surface on the y-axis (101.3 kPa for the normal boiling point).
  2. Move across to the curve of the substance.
  3. Move down to the x-axis.
  4. Read the boiling point and write it with units.
  • As a liquid is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and more of them escape into the gas phase, so the vapor pressure increases.
  • When the vapor pressure becomes equal to the pressure pushing down on the liquid, bubbles of vapor can form throughout the liquid and push back against the atmosphere. The liquid begins to boil.
  • Boiling Point – the temperature at which the vapor pressure of a liquid equals the pressure on its surface (the atmospheric pressure).
    • On a vapor pressure curve, the boiling point is where the curve crosses the pressure on the surface.
  • Normal Boiling Point – the boiling point at standard pressure, 101.3 kPa (1 atm). Water’s normal boiling point is 100 °C.
  • When the pressure on the surface of a liquid is lower, its boiling point is lower. At high altitude, water boils below 100 °C, so food cooks more slowly.
  • When the pressure on the surface of a liquid is higher, its boiling point is higher. A pressure cooker raises the pressure, so the water stays liquid above 100 °C and food cooks faster.
  • Liquids with weaker intermolecular forces have higher vapor pressures, so they reach the surface pressure at lower temperatures. Therefore, liquids with weaker intermolecular forces have lower boiling points.
  • Steps to finding a boiling point from a vapor pressure curve:
    1. Find the pressure on the surface on the y-axis (101.3 kPa for the normal boiling point).
    2. Move across to the curve of the substance.
    3. Move down to the x-axis.
    4. Read the boiling point and write it with units.
