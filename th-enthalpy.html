System and Surroundings

System and Surroundings

Squeeze an instant cold pack, and it turns icy cold in seconds; snap a hand warmer, and it gets hot. In both cases, the chemicals inside the pack are the system, and your hand is part of the surroundings. Your hand feels cold when energy leaves it and flows into the system, and warm when energy flows out of the system into it.

The system is the part of the universe being studied, while the surroundings are everything else. In an open system, energy and matter can move in or out; in a closed system, energy can move in or out, but matter cannot; and in an isolated system, neither energy nor matter can move in or out.

Energy flow is always described from the point of view of the system. In an exothermic process, heat flows out of the system into the surroundings, so the surroundings get warmer, as with the hand warmer. In an endothermic process, heat flows from the surroundings into the system, so the surroundings get colder, as with the cold pack.

  • System – the part of the universe being studied. Surroundings – everything else.
  • Open system – energy and matter can move in or out. Closed system – energy can move in or out, but matter cannot. Isolated system – neither energy nor matter can move in or out.
  • In an exothermic process, heat flows out of the system into the surroundings, so the surroundings get warmer.
  • In an endothermic process, heat flows from the surroundings into the system, so the surroundings get colder.

Enthalpy

Enthalpy (H)

Engineers who design hand warmers choose a reaction that releases plenty of heat per gram, and to compare reactions, chemists measure the heat each one releases or absorbs. Most reactions in a lab, a kitchen or a living body happen open to the air, at constant pressure, so chemists use a quantity designed to track heat under exactly those conditions.

Enthalpy (H) is the total heat content of a system at constant pressure. The total enthalpy of a substance cannot be measured directly, but its change can. The heat of reaction (ΔH) is the change in enthalpy during a reaction: ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants. If ΔH < 0, the reaction is exothermic, and the system releases heat to the surroundings, while if ΔH > 0, it is endothermic, and the system absorbs heat from the surroundings.

Systems tend to move toward lower energy, much as a ball rolls downhill, so exothermic changes are favored. Energy is only one of the two factors that decide whether a change happens, however. Ice melts in a warm room, even though melting is endothermic, which shows that something else can outweigh energy. The other factor is entropy.

  • Enthalpy (H) – the total heat content of a system at constant pressure.
  • Heat of reaction (ΔH) – the change in enthalpy during a reaction: ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants.
  • ΔH < 0: exothermic. The system releases heat to the surroundings.
  • ΔH > 0: endothermic. The system absorbs heat from the surroundings.
  • Systems tend to move toward lower energy, so exothermic changes are favored, but energy is only one of the two factors that decide whether a change happens. The other is entropy.
