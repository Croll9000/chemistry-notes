Measurements & Measurable Quantities

Chemistry is a quantitative science. Before we can describe matter, we need to agree on what we measure, what units we use, and which tools we measure with.

Measurements

What does a number mean without a unit?

A nurse reading a doctor’s order for “0.5” of a medicine needs to know much more than the number. Half a milligram, half a gram and half a milliliter are completely different doses, and the difference between them could be a thousandfold. Hospitals require every dose to be written with its unit for exactly this reason. A number on its own describes nothing; it is the unit that tells what kind of quantity is being described and how large each counted piece is.

A measurement is a number-based description of something, made by comparing it to a standard unit. Every measurement has two parts. The magnitude is the number, which tells how much. The unit is the standard being used, which tells what kind of quantity it is and what size. In 36.7 mL, the magnitude is 36.7 and the unit is milliliters.

Thus, a number without a unit tells you nothing. Saying that a sample is 24 leaves the reader guessing: 24 grams, 24 milliliters or 24 degrees? In chemistry, every measured quantity is written with its unit, every time.

  • Measurement – a number-based description of something, made by comparing it to a standard unit.
  • Every measurement has 2 parts:
    • Magnitude – the number (how much)
    • Unit – the standard being used (what kind of quantity, and what size)

The SI System

In 1999, NASA’s Mars Climate Orbiter flew too close to Mars and was destroyed in the planet’s atmosphere. An investigation found that one team of engineers had calculated the thrust of the spacecraft’s engines in pound-force units, while another team’s software expected newtons, the metric unit. Every number was correct in its own system, but no one had converted between them, and a spacecraft that cost more than $100 million was lost.

To prevent mix-ups like this, scientists around the world use one agreed-upon set of units. The International System of Units (SI) is the set of units used by scientists around the world, and using the same units lets everyone’s measurements be compared. The SI is based on the metric system, so its units are related by factors of 10. A base unit is one of the basic SI units that all other units are built from, such as the meter for length, the kilogram for mass, the second for time, the kelvin for temperature and the mole for amount of substance. A derived unit is a unit made by combining base units: volume in cubic centimeters is length × length × length, and the joule, the unit of energy, combines mass, length and time.

Part2

Quantities We Measure in Chemistry

Length

In the 1790s, French scientists defined the meter as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, measured along the line passing through Paris. Two surveyors spent six years measuring part of that distance by hand. Today, the meter is defined far more precisely, by the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second, so any well-equipped laboratory in the world can reproduce it exactly.

Length is the distance between two points. Its SI unit is the meter (m), and smaller and larger lengths are measured in centimeters (cm), millimeters (mm) and kilometers (km). Length is measured with a ruler or a meter stick. A doorknob is about 1 m above the floor, while the width of a fingernail is about 1 cm.

  • Length – the distance between two points.
    • Unit – meter (m); also centimeter (cm), millimeter (mm) and kilometer (km)
    • Measuring device – ruler or meter stick

Mass

For 130 years, the kilogram was defined by a single metal cylinder about the size of a golf ball, kept under three glass bell jars in a vault near Paris. Every scale in the world was ultimately compared with it. The problem was that the cylinder, nicknamed “Le Grand K,” seemed to be changing very slightly over the decades, and if it changed, the kilogram changed with it. In 2019, scientists replaced it with a definition based on a constant of nature, which cannot change.

Mass is the amount of matter (“stuff”) in an object. Its SI base unit is the kilogram (kg), but in the chemistry lab, mass is usually measured in grams (g), and sometimes milligrams (mg), using an electronic or triple beam balance. A paperclip has a mass of about 1 g.

Mass is not the same as weight. Weight is the pull of gravity on an object, so it changes from place to place, while the mass of an object, the amount of matter in it, stays the same anywhere in the universe.

  • Mass – the amount of matter (“stuff”) in an object.
    • Unit – gram (g); also kilogram (kg) and milligram (mg)
    • Measuring device – balance (electronic or triple beam)

Volume

A syringe in a doctor’s office is often marked in “cc,” short for cubic centimeters, while a bottle of cough syrup is labeled in milliliters. The two units sound different, but they are exactly the same size. A cube 1 cm on each side holds exactly 1 mL, so a 5 cc dose and a 5 mL dose are identical.

Volume is the amount of space an object takes up. It is measured in liters (L), milliliters (mL) and cubic centimeters (cm³), and 1 mL = 1 cm³. The volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder, while the volume of a solid with a regular shape is calculated from its dimensions with a ruler: V = l × w × h. Since volume is length × length × length, its unit is a length unit cubed, which makes volume a derived unit.

  • Volume – the amount of space an object takes up.
    • Unit – liter (L), milliliter (mL) and cubic centimeter (cm³)
    • Measuring device – graduated cylinder (liquids); ruler (regular solids, V = l × w × h)
    • 1 mL = 1 cm³

Temperature

A thermometer can only ever measure its own temperature. When it is placed in a cup of hot water, energy flows from the water into the thermometer until the two are at the same temperature, and only then does the reading stop changing. This is why a thermometer must be left in a sample until its reading is steady: until then, it is measuring a temperature somewhere between its own and the sample’s.

Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample, the energy they have because of their motion. It is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius (°C) or kelvin (K), and the kelvin is the SI base unit.

  • Temperature – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample.
    • Unit – degrees Celsius (°C) and kelvin (K)
    • Measuring device – thermometer

Energy

Lifting an apple 1 meter off a table takes about 1 joule of energy, the SI unit of energy. A single joule is a small amount: heating a cup of water for tea takes about 80,000 J, which is why energy in chemistry is often measured in kilojoules. Energy is the ability to do work or cause change, and it is measured in joules (J) and kilojoules (kJ). Heat is a form of energy, the energy that flows from a hotter object to a cooler one, so it is measured in the same units.

  • Energy – the ability to do work or cause change.
    • Unit – joule (J) and kilojoule (kJ)
    • Heat is a form of energy.

Amount of Substance

Banks do not count coins one at a time. A bank teller pours a bag of pennies onto a scale, and since every penny has nearly the same mass, the mass of the pile tells how many pennies there are. Chemists count atoms the same way, because atoms are far too small and too numerous to count individually.

The mole (mol) is the SI unit for the amount of a substance, and it is used to count very small particles, like atoms and molecules. One mole is the number of atoms in exactly 12 grams of carbon-12. The number of particles in one mole is referred to as Avogadro’s number: 6.02 × 1023. Thus, weighing out 12.00 g of carbon is a way of counting out 6.02 × 1023 carbon atoms, just as weighing a pile of pennies counts the pennies.

  • Mole (mol) – the SI unit for the amount of a substance.
    • Unit – mole (mol)
    • It is used to count very small particles, like atoms and molecules.
  • One mole is the number of atoms in exactly 12 grams of carbon-12.
    • Avogadro’s Number – the number of particles in one mole: 6.02 × 1023
