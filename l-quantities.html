Measurements & Measurable Quantities

Chemistry is a quantitative science. Before we can describe matter, we need to agree on what we measure, what units we use, and which tools we measure with.

Part1

Measurements

Every measurement needs a number and a unit.

Measurements

Much of chemistry depends on careful observation, and the most useful observations are those that can be expressed with numbers. A doctor recording a patient’s temperature, a pharmacist preparing a dose of medicine and a chemist weighing out a sample all rely on measurements. For a measurement to be meaningful, it must be reported in a way that others can understand.

  • Measurement – a number-based description of something, made by comparing it to a standard unit.
  • Every measurement has 2 parts:
    • Magnitude – the number (how much)
    • Unit – the standard being used (what kind of quantity, and what size)
  • Ex: 24 g, 36.7 mL, 298 K
  • A number without a unit tells you nothing.
    • Ex: “the sample is 24”: 24 grams? 24 milliliters? 24 degrees?

The SI System

A measurement is only useful if others can understand and repeat it. In 1999, NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter because one engineering team reported its data in English units while another team expected metric units. To prevent errors like this, scientists around the world have agreed to use a single set of units.

  • International System of Units (SI) – the set of units used by scientists around the world.
    • Using the same units lets everyone’s measurements be compared.
    • The SI is based on the metric system (factors of 10).
  • Base Unit – one of the basic SI units that all other units are built from. These are the base units you will use in chemistry:

Part2

Quantities We Measure in Chemistry

What each quantity is, its units, the tool that measures it, and a real-world reference for its size.

Length

Chemists measure objects across an enormous range of sizes, from the length of a lab bench to the width of a single atom. Atoms are so small that their radii are measured in picometers, trillionths of a meter. Regardless of size, each of these is a measurement of length.

  • Length – the distance between two points.
    • Unit – meter (m); also centimeter (cm), millimeter (mm) and kilometer (km)
    • Measuring device – ruler or meter stick

Mass

Every object, from a grain of sand to a planet, is made of matter. The amount of that matter determines how heavy an object is and how difficult it is to move. In the lab, mass is one of the most common measurements a chemist will take.

  • Mass – the amount of matter in an object.
    • Unit – gram (g); also kilogram (kg) and milligram (mg)
    • Measuring device – balance (electronic or triple beam)

Volume

All matter takes up space. That space can be as large as the water in a swimming pool or as small as the few milliliters of medicine in a syringe. Liquids in particular are almost always measured by the amount of space they occupy.

  • Volume – the amount of space an object takes up.
    • Unit – liter (L), milliliter (mL) and cubic centimeter (cm³)
    • Measuring device – graduated cylinder (liquids); ruler (regular solids, V = l × w × h)
    • 1 mL = 1 cm³

Temperature

Temperature is one of the most important conditions in any chemistry lab. It determines whether a substance is a solid, liquid or gas, and it affects how quickly chemical reactions take place. This is why food is kept in a refrigerator: lower temperatures slow the reactions that cause food to spoil.

  • Temperature – a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample (how fast they are moving).
    • Unit – degrees Celsius (°C) and kelvin (K)
    • Measuring device – thermometer

Energy

Every change in matter, from ice melting to fuel burning, involves energy. The energy stored in food is listed on nutrition labels in Calories, and the energy released by burning gasoline is what moves a car.

  • Energy – the ability to do work or cause change.
    • Unit – joule (J) and kilojoule (kJ)
    • Heat is a form of energy.

Amount of Substance

Atoms and molecules are far too small to count one by one. A single drop of water contains roughly 1.7 × 1021 water molecules. In the same way that eggs are counted by the dozen and paper by the ream, chemists count particles in large groups.

  • Mole (mol) – the SI unit for the amount of a substance.
    • Unit – mole (mol)
    • It is used to count very small particles, like atoms and molecules.
  • One mole is the number of atoms in exactly 12 grams of carbon-12.
    • Avogadro’s number – the number of particles in one mole: 6.02 × 1023
