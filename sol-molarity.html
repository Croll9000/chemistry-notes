Molarity

The saline solution in a hospital IV bag contains 9.0 g of sodium chloride in every liter, a concentration that matches the salt content of blood. A much weaker solution would make red blood cells swell and burst, while a much stronger one would make them shrivel, so the concentration has to be exact. Pharmacists, nurses and chemists all need a way to describe a concentration that can be measured precisely and that tells them how many particles of solute are in a given volume.

For this reason, chemists describe the concentration of most laboratory solutions as molarity (M): the number of moles of solute per liter of solution. Its equation is

M = moles of soluteliters of solution

The unit of molarity is mol/L, written M and read “molar,” so a 2.0 M solution contains 2.0 moles of solute in every liter. The IV saline contains 9.0 g of NaCl, or 9.0 g × 1 mol58.5 g = 0.15 mol, in each liter, so it is a 0.15 M solution. Since molarity counts the solute in moles, solutions with the same molarity contain the same number of solute particles in the same volume, no matter which solute they contain. Rearranging the equation gives n = MV, which finds the moles of solute in any volume of a solution.

The volume in the equation is the volume of the whole solution, not of the water alone. Dissolving a solid changes the volume slightly, so dissolving 58.5 g of NaCl in exactly 1.00 L of water gives a little more than 1.00 L of solution, and a molarity a little lower than 1.00 M. To make a solution with an exact molarity, the solute is dissolved in a small amount of water first, and water is then added up to the mark on a volumetric flask. To solve a molarity problem:

  2. Convert units if needed: grams to moles with the molar mass, milliliters to liters.
  3. Write down the equation.
  4. Substitute and solve. Substitute the values and their units, then solve for the unknown algebraically.
  5. Write the final answer with units.
