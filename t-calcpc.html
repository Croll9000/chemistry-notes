Heat During Phase Changes

Heat During Phase Changes

A snowbank in early spring can sit at 0 °C through several days of sunshine, shrinking slowly but never getting any warmer. All of the energy it absorbs is going into melting, not into raising its temperature. We cannot use q = mcΔT here, since ΔT is zero and the equation would predict that no heat is needed at all. Instead of the particles’ kinetic energy changing, their potential energy changes, and the heat depends only on the mass that changes phase. We use q = mHf for melting or freezing and q = mHv for vaporizing or condensing.

The value Hf is called the heat of fusion (Hf), and Hv is the heat of vaporization (Hv). Both are measured in joules per gram. Since each one is a quantity of heat per gram, multiplying by the mass gives the total heat, which is exactly what the equations do.

Heat of Fusion (Hf) – the amount of heat needed to melt (or released when freezing) 1 gram of a substance at its melting point.

Heat of Vaporization (Hv) – the amount of heat needed to vaporize (or released when condensing) 1 gram of a substance at its boiling point.

Different substances need different amounts of heat to change phase, mostly because of differences in their intermolecular forces. The higher the heat of fusion or vaporization, the more energy must be added to change the phase, and the stronger the attractions being overcome.

For every substance, the heat of vaporization is much larger than the heat of fusion. Melting only loosens the particles enough to let them slide past one another, so most of the attractions remain. Vaporizing must pull the particles completely away from one another, overcoming nearly all of the attractions. For water, Hv is about 6.8 times Hf, so the boiling segment of water’s heating curve is far longer than the melting segment.

  • To calculate the heat added or removed during a phase change, we cannot rely on measurements of temperature, because the temperature remains constant during a phase change.
  • Instead of the particles’ kinetic energy changing, their potential energy changes. Therefore, we use different equations to calculate the heat added or removed during phase changes: q = mHf for melting or freezing, and q = mHv for vaporizing or condensing.

Melting vs. Boiling

Constants for Water

Water is so common in chemistry problems that its constants are worth getting to know rather than looking up each time. Notice how the numbers compare. Melting a gram of ice takes 334 J, as much heat as warming that gram of liquid water by about 80 °C. Vaporizing a gram of water takes 2,260 J, more than five times the heat needed to warm it all the way from 0 °C to 100 °C. Phase changes involve far more energy than ordinary temperature changes, and that is what makes them so useful for heating and cooling. Phase Changes in Everyday Life

Phase Changes in Everyday Life

Phase changes absorb or release large amounts of heat without any change in temperature, so they are used everywhere to heat and cool things.

Sweating cools the body. As sweat evaporates from your skin, every gram absorbs about 2,260 J of heat from your body. Evaporating just 100 g of sweat, less than half a cup, removes about 226,000 J. On a humid day, the air already holds so much water vapor that sweat evaporates slowly. It drips off instead of cooling you, and humid heat feels far worse than dry heat.

Ice keeps a cooler cold. Melting ice absorbs 334 J for every gram that melts, and the temperature stays at 0 °C the whole time, so a cooler holds a steady temperature until the ice is gone. A few ice cubes also cool a drink far better than the same mass of cold water would, since each gram absorbs 334 J just to melt before it starts absorbing heat to warm up.

Steam causes worse burns than boiling water at the same temperature. When steam touches your skin, it first condenses, releasing 2,260 J per gram. Only then does the hot water cool to body temperature, releasing about 263 J more. A gram of steam delivers nearly ten times as much heat as a gram of boiling water.

Phase changes can also move heat from one place to another. A refrigerator moves heat with two phase changes. A liquid refrigerant evaporates inside the coils within the refrigerator, absorbing heat from the food. It is then compressed and condensed in the coils on the back, releasing that heat into the kitchen. The cycle repeats as long as the refrigerator runs.

Solving Phase-Change Problems

Solving q = mH Problems

When performing heat calculations, it is always necessary to use the right tool for the problem. When a question involves a change in temperature, as we’ve seen, you use the equation q = mcΔT. However, phase changes require a different formula, either q = mHf or q = mHv. Figuring out which formula to use requires looking for key words or phrases in the question. If the question specifically names a phase change such as melting or freezing, or says that there is a transition between solid and liquid or vice versa, we use q = mHf. On the other hand, if the question names a phase change such as boiling or condensation, or mentions a transition between the liquid and gas phases or vice versa, we use q = mHv.

Be careful when a question mentions the melting point or boiling point. Often these are given in a question and students mistake them for a temperature change. However, in order to use our phase change equations, q = mHf or q = mHv, the substance must already be at its melting point or boiling point. Thus, that information is sometimes given even though there is no change in temperature during these transitions.

Also remember that the sign of q, the heat, changes based on whether energy is absorbed or released during the process. For endothermic phase changes like melting and boiling, the heat (q) is positive (+) since heat is absorbed by the substance. In exothermic phase changes like freezing and condensation, the heat (q) is negative (−) since heat is released by the substance.

It is not always the case that you will be solving for heat (q). Sometimes you will be asked to solve for the mass (m) of the substance or its heat of fusion (Hf) or heat of vaporization (Hv). Therefore, you should be prepared to algebraically rearrange the equation and solve for the correct variable in the question. The steps to solving for heat during phase changes are:

  1. Identify which equation to use. If the temperature changes, use q = mcΔT. If there is a solid to liquid transition or vice versa, use q = mHf. If there is a liquid to gas transition or vice versa, use q = mHv.
  2. List the variables and the unknown, each with its unit.
  3. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation.
  4. Solve for the unknown algebraically.
  5. Write the final answer with units.
