Limiting and Excess Reagents

Imagine that you are making hamburgers at a barbecue. Each burger requires 2 buns and 1 patty. Buns come in packages of 12, and patties come in packages of 3. Even with 12 buns on the table, you can only make 3 burgers, and 6 buns will be left over. The number of burgers is set by whichever ingredient runs out first, not by whichever ingredient you have the most of.

Chemical reactions work the same way. Substances must react in the mole ratios given by the balanced equation, so if random amounts of two reactants are combined, one will almost certainly be used up before the other. The reactant that runs out is the limiting reagent, while the reactant that is left over is the excess reagent. At the barbecue, the patties are the limiting reagent, since they are completely used up, and the buns are the excess reagent, since some are left over.

Limiting Reagent – reactant that is completely used up and therefore restricts how much product is formed.

Excess Reagent – reactant that is left over after the reaction is finished.

Chemists often use an excess reagent on purpose. When a reaction needs an expensive reactant and an inexpensive one, adding extra of the inexpensive reactant makes sure that every bit of the expensive one is used. Drug manufacturers do this constantly, since the starting material for a medicine may cost thousands of dollars per kilogram while the other reactants cost very little.

  • Since substances must react in specific mole ratios given by the balanced chemical equation, if random amounts of 2 reactants are combined, there is a high chance that one substance will be completely consumed before the other.
    • Limiting Reagent – reactant that is completely used up and therefore restricts how much product is formed.
    • Excess Reagent – reactant that is left over after the reaction is finished.

Determining Limiting and Excess Reagents

Consider 6 molecules of hydrogen mixed with 2 molecules of oxygen, reacting by:

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

Each oxygen molecule needs 2 hydrogen molecules. The 2 oxygen molecules therefore use up only 4 of the 6 hydrogen molecules and make 4 water molecules, and 2 hydrogen molecules are left over. Oxygen runs out first, so oxygen is the limiting reagent.

Notice why. Oxygen ran out because of the mole ratio. Each oxygen molecule needed two hydrogen molecules, so 2 oxygen molecules could only use 4 hydrogen. With a different equation, the reactant present in the smaller amount could just as easily be the one left over. Thus, the limiting reagent is not always the reactant with the smaller mass or the smaller number of moles, since the mole ratio also matters.

A reliable method is to ask how much product each reactant could make if the other reactant never ran out. Whichever reactant makes less product runs out first. It is the limiting reagent, and the smaller amount of product is the amount that actually forms. To determine the limiting and excess reagents:

  1. Make sure the chemical equation is balanced.
  2. Convert the starting amount of every reactant to moles.
  3. Using mole ratios, convert the moles of every reactant into moles of one of the products.
  4. Compare: the reactant that yields the smaller amount of product is the limiting reagent, and the reactant that yields the larger amount of product is the excess reagent.
  • Steps to Determine Limiting and Excess Reagents:
    1. Make sure the chemical equation is balanced.
    2. Convert the starting amount of every reactant to moles.
    3. Using mole ratios, convert the moles of every reactant into moles of one of the products.
    4. The reactant that yields the smaller amount of product is the limiting reagent. The reactant that yields the larger amount of product is the excess reagent.
