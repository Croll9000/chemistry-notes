Limiting and Excess Reagents

When 1.00 g of aluminum foil is placed in a solution containing 5.00 g of copper(II) sulfate, the blue color of the solution disappears while some aluminum is left over. The copper(II) sulfate, which gives the solution its blue color, is the limiting reactant: it runs out first and determines how much copper forms, while the aluminum is in excess. In this section, we will identify limiting and excess reagents.

Limiting and Excess Reagents

Car engines are tuned so that fuel and air mix in just the right ratio. Too little air, and some fuel goes unburned and pollutes; too much, and the engine loses power. In almost every real reaction, one reactant runs out before the other, and that reactant decides how much product can form.

  • Since substances must react in specific mole ratios given by the balanced chemical equation, if random amounts of 2 reactants are combined, there is a high chance that one substance will be completely consumed before the other.
    • Limiting Reagent – reactant that is completely used up and therefore restricts how much product is formed.
    • Excess Reagent – reactant that is left over after the reaction is finished.
  • Imagine that you are making hamburgers at a barbecue.
    • Each burger requires 2 buns and 1 patty.
    • Buns come in packages of 12 while patties come in packages of 3. You will only be able to make 3 burgers, leaving you with 6 leftover buns.
    • The patties are the limiting reagent, since they are completely used up.
    • The buns are the excess reagent, since some are left over.

Determining Limiting and Excess Reagents

  • Steps to Determine Limiting and Excess Reagents:
    1. Make sure the chemical equation is balanced.
    2. Convert the starting amount of every reactant to moles.
    3. Using mole ratios, convert the moles of every reactant into moles of one of the products.
    4. The reactant that yields the smaller amount of product is the limiting reagent. The reactant that yields the larger amount of product is the excess reagent.

Theoretical Yield, Leftover Excess and Percent Yield

Industrial ammonia plants convert only about 15% of their nitrogen and hydrogen into ammonia each time the gases pass through the reactor, so the unreacted gases are recycled. The amount of product actually collected is almost always less than the maximum calculated from the balanced equation, the theoretical yield. In this section, we will calculate theoretical yield, leftover excess reagent and percent yield.

Theoretical and Actual Yield

A company that makes aspirin is paid for the product it actually collects, not the product it calculated on paper. Side reactions, spills and incomplete reactions all mean that real reactions make less than the maximum. Comparing the two tells chemists how efficient their process is.

  • Not every chemical reaction will yield the amount of product that stoichiometric calculations predict. Side products can form and experimental errors can lead to less product than expected.
    • Theoretical Yield – maximum amount of product that can be created in a reaction.
    • Actual Yield – the amount of product that is truly produced in a reaction.
  • Steps to Calculating Theoretical Yield:
    1. Determine the limiting and excess reagents.
    2. Using mole ratios, convert moles of the limiting reagent into moles of each product.
    3. Using molar mass, convert moles of products into mass of products (if necessary).
  • Steps to Calculate the Leftover Excess Reagent:
    1. Determine the limiting and excess reagents.
    2. Using mole ratios, convert moles of the limiting reagent into moles of excess reagent consumed.
    3. Subtract the moles of excess reagent consumed from the starting moles of excess reagent. Using molar mass, convert moles of excess reagent into mass, if necessary.

Percent Yield

  • Percent Yield – the actual yield as a percent of the theoretical yield.
  • % yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
Example
A reaction has a theoretical yield of 20.0 g of product, but only 17.0 g is collected. % yield = (17.0 g ÷ 20.0 g) × 100 = 85.0%
