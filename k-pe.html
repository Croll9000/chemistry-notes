Part1

Heat of Reaction

A match does not light by itself in the box. Rubbing it on the strip gives it a small push of energy, and then the burning match releases far more energy than the push it needed. Every reaction either releases or absorbs energy, and most need some energy to get started. In this section, we will review the heat of reaction and see how a diagram can show the energy of a reaction from start to finish.

Heat of Reaction (ΔH)

Chemical bonds store potential energy. When bonds break and new bonds form, the potential energy of the substances changes.

  • Heat of reaction (ΔH) – the amount of heat released or absorbed during a reaction; it equals the potential energy of the products minus the potential energy of the reactants.
    • ΔH=PEproducts − PEreactants
  • Exothermic reactions release heat: the products have less potential energy than the reactants, and ΔH is negative. The energy term is written on the products side: N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g) + 91.8 kJ, ΔH = −91.8 kJ.
  • Endothermic reactions absorb heat: the products have more potential energy than the reactants, and ΔH is positive. The energy term is written on the reactants side: N2(g) + O2(g) + 182.6 kJ → 2NO(g), ΔH = +182.6 kJ.
  • The Reference Tables list the heats of reaction for many reactions.
Part2

Potential Energy Diagrams

A hiker crossing a mountain pass has to climb up before walking down into the next valley, even if that valley is lower than the starting point. Reactants also have to climb an energy hill before they can become products. In this section, we will discuss how a potential energy diagram shows the energy of a reaction as it happens.

Potential Energy Diagrams

A potential energy diagram follows the potential energy of the particles from reactants, over the energy hill, to products.

  • Potential energy diagram – a graph of the potential energy of the substances over the course of a reaction. The vertical axis is potential energy; the horizontal axis is the reaction coordinate, which shows the progress of the reaction.
  • Activated complex – the high-energy, unstable arrangement of atoms at the top of the curve, where old bonds are breaking and new bonds are forming.
  • The parts of a potential energy diagram:
    • PE of the reactants – the flat line on the left.
    • PE of the products – the flat line on the right.
    • Activation energy – the height from the reactants up to the activated complex. The activation energy of the reverse reaction is the height from the products up to the activated complex.
    • Heat of reaction (ΔH) – the difference between the products and the reactants.
  • In an exothermic diagram the products end lower than the reactants; in an endothermic diagram the products end higher.

Catalysts on a PE Diagram

An enzyme in your saliva breaks starch into sugar within minutes; without it, the same reaction would take years. On a potential energy diagram, the effect of a catalyst shows up in only one place. In this section, we will discuss how a catalyst changes a potential energy diagram.

Catalysts on a PE Diagram

Turn on the catalyst in the diagram above and compare the two curves.

  • A catalyst lowers the activation energy, so the hill on the diagram is lower. It lowers the activation energy of the forward and the reverse reactions by the same amount.
  • A catalyst does not change the PE of the reactants, the PE of the products, or the heat of reaction (ΔH).
