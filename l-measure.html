Measurements and Uncertainty

Taking Measurements

Two students measure the same line with the same ruler. One writes 2.81 cm, while the other writes 2.82 cm. Neither is wrong. The ruler is marked every millimeter, and the end of the line falls between the 2.8 and 2.9 cm marks, so both students can be certain of the first two digits, while the last digit is an estimate of where the line ends between the marks. What we see is that every measurement contains some uncertainty, and the way a measurement is written shows exactly where that uncertainty is.

No measurement is perfectly exact, since every measuring instrument has a limit to how finely it is marked. When taking measurements, we estimate one place beyond the smallest marking on the instrument. The digits read directly from the markings are the certain digits, while the last digit, estimated between two markings, is the estimated digit.

Certain Digits – the digits read directly from the markings on the instrument.

Estimated Digit – the last digit of a measurement, estimated between two markings.

The place value of the estimated digit is referred to as the place of uncertainty, and it is always one place value beyond the smallest marking on the instrument. A ruler marked in tenths of a centimeter, for instance, is read to the hundredths. Small differences in the estimated digit between people are expected, which is exactly why it is called an estimate.

Place of Uncertainty – the place value of the estimated digit.

  • No measurement is perfectly exact.
    • Every measuring instrument has a limit to how finely it is marked.
  • When taking measurements, you are allowed to estimate one place beyond the smallest marking (the smallest unit) on the instrument you are using.
    • Certain Digits – the digits you read directly from the markings on the instrument.
    • Estimated Digit – the last digit of a measurement, which you estimate between two markings.
  • Place of Uncertainty – the place value of the estimated digit.
    • It is always one place value beyond the smallest marking on the instrument.

Steps to Reading an Instrument

Every analog instrument, whether a ruler, a graduated cylinder or a thermometer, is read the same way. The first step is always to find the value of the smallest marking, by counting the spaces between two numbered lines, since that value decides which place is certain and which is estimated. The certain digits are the value of the last marking the object or liquid passes, and one more digit is then estimated by imagining the space between the two markings divided into 10 equal parts.

If the reading lands exactly on a marking, the estimated digit is 0, and it must still be written: 7.70 cm, not 7.7 cm. The zero is not a placeholder. It is a measured value that says the reading was checked to the hundredths place and found to be exactly on the line, while writing 7.7 cm would suggest a much less precise ruler.

  1. Find the value of the smallest marking.
    • Count the spaces between two numbered lines.
    Example
    10 spaces between 0 cm and 1 cm means each space is 0.1 cm.
  2. Write down the certain digits: the value of the last marking the object (or liquid) passes.
  3. Estimate one more digit by imagining the space between the two markings divided into 10 equal parts.
  4. If the reading lands exactly on a marking, the estimated digit is 0, and you must still write it: 7.70 cm, not 7.7 cm.

Reading Lab Equipment

Rulers and Meter Sticks

A carpenter measuring a board and a scientist measuring a crystal both start the same way: they line up one end of the object with the 0 mark and read the scale at the other end. Length is measured with a ruler for small objects or a meter stick for larger ones, and how far the reading can go depends on the markings. A ruler marked in millimeters (0.1 cm) is read to the hundredths of a centimeter (0.01 cm), while a meter stick marked every 1 cm is read to the tenths of a centimeter (0.1 cm). Note that lining up with the 0 mark, rather than the worn end of the ruler, avoids an error that would affect every reading.

  • Length is measured with a ruler (small objects) or a meter stick (larger objects).
  • Line up one end of the object with the 0 mark.
    • Then read the scale at the other end.
  • How far you read depends on the markings.
    • A ruler marked in millimeters (0.1 cm) is read to the hundredths of a centimeter (0.01 cm).
    • A meter stick marked every 1 cm is read to the tenths of a centimeter (0.1 cm).

Graduated Cylinders

We measure the volume of a liquid with a graduated cylinder. Water in a narrow glass tube, however, does not have a flat surface. It curves upward where it touches the glass, since water molecules are attracted to the glass. This curved surface is called a meniscus, and since it spans a range of heights, we need a rule for where to read it: for water, always read the bottom of the meniscus, and read the cylinder at eye level.

Meniscus – the curved surface of a liquid in a narrow container.

Reading at eye level prevents parallax error. Looking down from above makes the reading too high, while looking up from below makes it too low, much as the speedometer of a car looks different from the passenger seat than from the driver’s seat.

Parallax Error – a reading error caused by looking at a scale from an angle.

  • The volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder.
  • Meniscus – the curved surface of a liquid in a narrow container.
    • For water, always read the bottom of the meniscus.
  • Read the cylinder at eye level.
    • Parallax Error – a reading error caused by looking at a scale from an angle.
    • Looking from above makes the reading too high.
    • Looking from below makes the reading too low.

Thermometers

Temperature is measured with a thermometer, read at the top of the liquid column. The bulb should stay in the liquid being measured, without touching the bottom of the beaker, which may be hotter or colder than the liquid. A thermometer marked every 1 °C is read to the tenths place (0.1 °C).

Note that a thermometer placed in a beaker of warm water does not show the correct temperature right away. The liquid in its column rises for several seconds as the thermometer warms up to match the water, and a reading taken too early will be too low. Thus, the reading should be taken only after the liquid column stops moving.

  • Temperature is measured with a thermometer.
    • Read the scale at the top of the liquid column.
  • Keep the bulb in the liquid you are measuring (not touching the bottom of the beaker), and wait until the liquid column stops moving before you read it.
  • A thermometer marked every 1 °C is read to the tenths place (0.1 °C).
The smallest marking is 1 °C, so we record to the 0.1 °C (tenths) place.

Electronic Balances

Mass is measured with a balance, and an electronic (digital) balance shows the reading as numbers. With a digital instrument, there is no scale to estimate from, so every digit on the display is recorded, including zeros at the end. The last digit shown is the estimated digit, since it is the one that flickers when the balance is disturbed. A reading of 12.50 g is written as 12.50 g, not 12.5 g.

The TARE button resets the display to zero, which is used to subtract the mass of a container. In this case, the empty container is placed on the balance, TARE is pressed, and the sample is then added, so the display shows only the mass of the sample.

The most precise balances in a chemistry lab sit inside small glass boxes with sliding doors. They are so sensitive that a breath of air or the draft from someone walking past can change the last digit of the reading. With the doors closed, the air inside is still, and the reading settles.

  • Mass is measured with a balance.
    • An electronic (digital) balance shows the reading as numbers.
  • With a digital instrument, record every digit on the display.
    • This includes zeros at the end.
    • The last digit shown is the estimated digit.
  • The TARE button resets the display to zero.
    • Use it to subtract the mass of a container.
    • Put the empty container on, press TARE, then add the sample.
