Taking Measurements

A measurement is only as good as the instrument used to take it and the care of the person reading it. Two students reading the same graduated cylinder may record slightly different values. Reading instruments correctly allows scientists to report not only a value but also how certain they are of it.

  • No measurement is perfectly exact.
    • Every measuring instrument has a limit to how finely it is marked.
  • When taking measurements, you are allowed to estimate one place beyond the smallest marking (the smallest unit) on the instrument you are using.
    • Certain Digits – the digits you read directly from the markings on the instrument.
    • Estimated Digit – the last digit of a measurement, which you estimate between two markings.
  • Place of Uncertainty – the place value of the estimated digit.
    • It is always one place value beyond the smallest marking on the instrument.

Drag the magnifier along the ruler to see where the line ends.

The smallest marking on the ruler is 0.1 cm, so we must record one place beyond that, to the 0.01 cm (hundredths) place.
The line falls between 2.8 and 2.9 cm, so 2.8 are certain digits. The line goes about 1/10 of the way to 2.9, so we estimate the next digit as 1.
2.81 cm  ←  the 1 is in the place of uncertainty

It is alright if your estimated digit is a little different from someone else’s (2.81 cm or 2.82 cm), but your answer must include the place of uncertainty.

Steps to Reading an Instrument

  1. Find the value of the smallest marking.
    • Count the spaces between two numbered lines.
    • Ex: 10 spaces between 0 cm and 1 cm means each space is 0.1 cm.
  2. Write down the certain digits: the value of the last marking the object (or liquid) passes.
  3. Estimate one more digit by imagining the space between the two markings divided into 10 equal parts.
  4. If the reading lands exactly on a marking, the estimated digit is 0, and you must still write it: 7.70 cm, not 7.7 cm.
Ruler marked in millimeters (0.1 cm)Reading: 2.64 cm (to the hundredths place)
Ruler marked only in centimeters (1 cm)Reading: 2.6 cm (to the tenths place)
The same object gives a reading with more digits on the more finely marked ruler. More digits means a more precise measurement. (The number of digits we can trust is called the number of significant figures, the topic of the next page.)

Part2

Reading Lab Equipment

The same rule for every instrument: certain digits plus one estimated digit.

Rulers and Meter Sticks

  • Length is measured with a ruler (small objects) or a meter stick (larger objects).
  • Line up one end of the object with the 0 mark.
    • Then read the scale at the other end.
  • How far you read depends on the markings.
    • A ruler marked in millimeters (0.1 cm) is read to the hundredths of a centimeter (0.01 cm).
    • A meter stick marked every 1 cm is read to the tenths of a centimeter (0.1 cm).

Graduated Cylinders

  • The volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder.
  • Meniscus – the curved surface of a liquid in a narrow container.
    • For water, always read the bottom of the meniscus.
  • Read the cylinder at eye level.
    • Parallax Error – a reading error caused by looking at a scale from an angle.
    • Looking from above makes the reading too high.
    • Looking from below makes the reading too low.
Smallest marking: 1 mL → read to 0.1 mL. Reading: 36.7 mL

Thermometers

  • Temperature is measured with a thermometer.
    • Read the scale at the top of the liquid column.
  • Keep the bulb in the liquid you are measuring (not touching the bottom of the beaker), and wait until the liquid column stops moving before you read it.
  • A thermometer marked every 1 °C is read to the tenths place (0.1 °C).
The smallest marking is 1 °C, so we record to the 0.1 °C (tenths) place.
The top of the liquid is past the 24 °C line and a little more than halfway to 25 °C.
Reading: 24.6 °C

Electronic Balances

  • Mass is measured with a balance.
    • An electronic (digital) balance shows the reading as numbers.
  • With a digital instrument, record every digit on the display.
    • This includes zeros at the end.
    • The last digit shown is the estimated digit.
  • The TARE button resets the display to zero.
    • Use it to subtract the mass of a container.
    • Put the empty container on, press TARE, then add the sample.
