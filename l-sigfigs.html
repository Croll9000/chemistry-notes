Significant Figures

The digits in a measurement show its precision. Significant figures are the digits that count.

What Are Significant Figures?

Significant figures show the precision of a measurement.

Why Significant Figures Matter

  • Imagine a friend measures their height with a tape measure and says, “I am 6.01598 ft tall.”
  • That last digit is in the hundred-thousandths place.
    • 0.00001 ft is about 0.003 mm, much thinner than a human hair.
    • No tape measure can measure that finely.
    • Your height changes more than that between morning and night.
  • A tape measure marked every 0.1 ft lets you estimate one more place, to the hundredths.
    • Honest measurement: 6.02 ft
  • The digits you write down tell the reader how precisely you measured.
    • Writing extra digits claims precision you don’t have.
    • Leaving digits out throws away information you measured.

Significant Figures

  • Significant Figures (sig figs) – the digits in a measurement that are known with certainty plus the one estimated digit.
  • Significant figures show how precise a measurement is: more sig figs = more precision.
  • When you read an instrument correctly, every digit you write down is significant.
    • Ex: a ruler reading of 2.81 cm has 3 significant figures (2 and 8 are certain, 1 is estimated).
  • The tricky part is zeros.
    • Some zeros are measured digits.
    • Some zeros are only placeholders that show where the decimal point is.

Rules for Counting Significant Figures

Significant

  1. All nonzero digits.
  2. Zeros between nonzero digits (“sandwiched” zeros).
  3. Zeros at the end of a number when there is a decimal point (trailing zeros after a decimal).

Not significant

  1. Leading zeros (zeros in front of the first nonzero digit).
    • They only hold the decimal place.
  2. Zeros at the end of a number with no decimal point.
  • Scientific notation: every digit in the coefficient is significant.
    • This is useful for showing that zeros were measured.
    • Ex: 3.00 × 102 m has 3 sig figs, but 300 m has only 1.
  • A decimal point at the end of a whole number tells the reader the zeros were measured:

Numbers that are counted (12 eggs, 3 trials) or defined (1 km = 1000 m) are exact. They have unlimited significant figures and never limit a calculation.

The Atlantic-Pacific Rule

A quick way to count sig figs. Picture a map of the United States: the Pacific Ocean is on the left and the Atlantic Ocean is on the right.

Pacific: decimal Present
Start at the Pacific (left) side. Move right to the first nonzero digit, then count it and every digit after it.
= 2 sig figs
Atlantic: decimal Absent
Start at the Atlantic (right) side. Move left to the first nonzero digit, then count it and every digit after it (going left).
= 3 sig figs
