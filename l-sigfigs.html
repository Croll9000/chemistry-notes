Significant Figures

The digits in a measurement show its precision. Significant figures are the digits that count.

What Are Significant Figures?

Why Significant Figures Matter

Imagine a friend measures their height with a tape measure and says, “I am 6.01598 ft tall.” That last digit is in the hundred-thousandths place, and 0.00001 ft is about 0.003 mm, much thinner than a human hair. No tape measure can measure that finely, and your height changes by more than that between morning and night. A tape measure marked every 0.1 ft lets you estimate one more place, to the hundredths, so the honest measurement is 6.02 ft.

The digits written down in a measurement tell the reader how precisely it was measured. Writing extra digits claims a precision that was never measured, while leaving digits out throws away information that was. Thus, the number of digits is part of the information in a measurement, just as much as the digits themselves.

Significant Figures

A ruler reading of 2.81 cm has three digits that each carry meaning: the 2 and the 8 are certain, read from the markings, and the 1 is the estimated digit. Significant figures (sig figs) are the digits in a measurement that are known with certainty plus the one estimated digit. Significant figures show how precise a measurement is: more sig figs means more precision.

When an instrument is read correctly, every digit written down is significant. The tricky part is zeros. Some zeros are measured digits, like the 0 in 7.70 cm, while others are only placeholders that show where the decimal point is, like the zeros in 0.005 g. The rules for counting significant figures sort out which zeros are which.

Rules for Counting Significant Figures

The number 0.004050 has seven digits, but only four of them are significant. The three zeros at the front only show how small the number is: written in milligrams instead of grams, 0.004050 g becomes 4.050 mg, and the leading zeros disappear. The zero between the 4 and the 5, and the zero at the end after a decimal point, cannot be removed by changing units, because they were measured.

This is the idea behind every rule. A digit is significant if it was measured, and not significant if it only holds a place. All nonzero digits are significant, as are zeros between nonzero digits and zeros at the end of a number when there is a decimal point. Leading zeros are never significant, and zeros at the end of a number with no decimal point are not counted, since there is no way to tell whether they were measured. Scientific notation removes the doubt: every digit in the coefficient is significant, so 1.50 × 103 has three significant figures.

Significant

  1. All nonzero digits.
  2. Zeros between nonzero digits (“sandwiched” zeros).
  3. Zeros at the end of a number when there is a decimal point (trailing zeros after a decimal).

Not significant

  1. Leading zeros (zeros in front of the first nonzero digit).
    • They only hold the decimal place.
  2. Zeros at the end of a number with no decimal point.
  • Scientific notation: every digit in the coefficient is significant.
    • This is useful for showing that zeros were measured.
    Example
    3.00 × 102 m has 3 sig figs, but 300 m has only 1.

Numbers that are counted (12 eggs, 3 trials) or defined (1 km = 1000 m) are exact. They have unlimited significant figures and never limit a calculation.

The Atlantic-Pacific Rule

A quick way to count sig figs. Picture a map of the United States: the Pacific Ocean is on the left and the Atlantic Ocean is on the right.

Pacific: decimal Present
Start at the Pacific (left) side. Move right to the first nonzero digit, then count it and every digit after it.
= 2 sig figs
Atlantic: decimal Absent
Start at the Atlantic (right) side. Move left to the first nonzero digit, then count it and every digit after it (going left).
= 3 sig figs
