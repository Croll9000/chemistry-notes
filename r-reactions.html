Chemical Reactions

A loaf of bread turns brown in the oven once its crust passes about 140 °C, as sugars and proteins on its surface react to form hundreds of new, darker compounds. The color change is evidence that new substances have formed, because the atoms of the original substances have been rearranged. In this section, we will discuss chemical reactions and the evidence that one has taken place.

Chemical Reactions

When a gas stove is lit, methane and oxygen from the air turn into carbon dioxide and water vapor, and the flame gives off heat and light. No new atoms appear. The atoms that were already there are rearranged into new substances.

  • Recall that chemical reactions are processes in which one set of substances is changed into another.
  • Chemical reactions involve the breaking and forming of chemical bonds.
  • When a chemical reaction occurs, there will be an accompanying energy change.
  • Signs that a chemical reaction may have occurred:
    • A gas is produced (bubbles).
    • A solid forms in a liquid (a precipitate).
    • The temperature changes, or light is given off.
    • The color changes.
Watch the atoms: the bonds in the hydrogen and oxygen molecules break, and the same atoms form new bonds to make water molecules.

Chemical Equations

Green plants make sugar from carbon dioxide and water, a process summarized by the equation 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. In one line, this equation identifies every reactant and product and shows that 6 molecules of carbon dioxide are used for each molecule of glucose formed. In this section, we will discuss how to read and write chemical equations, starting from word equations.

Chemical Equations

Chemists around the world need a way to describe a reaction so that anyone can repeat it, whether they are making a medicine or testing a new battery. A chemical equation is that shared language. In one line it shows which substances react, which substances form and how many particles of each are involved, so it is the starting point for everything else in this unit.

  • We represent the change that takes place in a reaction using a chemical equation. The starting substances are known as reactants and the ending substances are known as products.
  • Chemical Equation – symbolic representation of a chemical reaction using formulas and numbers.
  • Reactants – the starting substances in a chemical reaction, written on the left side of the arrow.
  • Products – the ending substances in a chemical reaction, written on the right side of the arrow.
  • Arrow – represents “yields” or “forms.” Shows the direction in which a chemical reaction proceeds.
This equation can be read as “1 molecule of CH4 reacts with 2 molecules of O2 to yield 1 molecule of CO2 and 2 molecules of H2O.”

Word Equations

Before chemists had formulas, reactions were described in words, and many lab procedures still start that way: “iron reacts with oxygen to form iron(III) oxide.” Turning a description into a word equation, and then into a chemical equation, is the first step in predicting how much of each substance is needed or produced.

  • Word Equation – a description of a chemical reaction that uses the names of the reactants and products instead of their formulas.
    Example
    Methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
    • The “+” is read as “and” (or “reacts with”). The arrow is read as “yields.”
  • A word equation does not show the formulas, the coefficients or the phases. A chemical equation shows all three.

Coefficients and Subscripts

The equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O describes the reaction in the fuel cells that powered the Apollo spacecraft and produced drinking water for the astronauts. Its subscripts identify each substance (H₂O contains 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom), while its coefficients show that 2 molecules of hydrogen react with every 1 molecule of oxygen. In this section, we will distinguish between these two kinds of numbers and what each one tells us.

Coefficients and Subscripts

Changing one small number in an equation can change everything. H2O is water that we drink, but H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, which is used as a bleach. Knowing the difference between the numbers that describe what a substance is (subscripts) and how many particles react (coefficients) is the key to reading and balancing equations correctly.

  • When reading a chemical equation, there are two important numbers to take note of:
    • Coefficients – large numbers placed in front of a chemical formula. Indicate the number of molecules or formula units (or moles) of a substance taking part in the reaction.
    • Subscripts – small numbers placed to the bottom right of an element symbol. They indicate the number of atoms of that element in a molecule or compound.
  • No coefficient means 1. No subscript means 1 atom.
  • For ionic compounds, one unit of the formula is called a formula unit (ionic compounds do not form molecules).
    Example
    3NaCl means 3 formula units of NaCl.
  • A subscript after parentheses multiplies every atom inside the parentheses.
    Example
    Ca(OH)2 contains 1 Ca, 2 O and 2 H.
  • To count the atoms in a formula with a coefficient, multiply each subscript by the coefficient.
    Example
    3H2O contains 3 × 2 = 6 H atoms and 3 × 1 = 3 O atoms.
This equation can be read as “1 molecule of N2 reacts with 3 molecules of H2 to form 2 molecules of NH3.”
Why subscripts never change: changing a subscript changes the substance. H2O is water, but H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, a different compound. Changing a coefficient only changes how many molecules or formula units there are.
