Chemical Reactions

New substances form when atoms rearrange.

Chemical Reactions

When a gas stove is lit, methane and oxygen from the air turn into carbon dioxide and water vapor, and the flame gives off heat and light. No new atoms appear. The atoms that were already there are rearranged into new substances.

  • Recall that chemical reactions are processes in which one set of substances is changed into another.
  • Chemical reactions involve the breaking and forming of chemical bonds.
  • When a chemical reaction occurs, there will be an accompanying energy change.
  • Signs that a chemical reaction may have occurred:
    • A gas is produced (bubbles).
    • A solid forms in a liquid (a precipitate).
    • The temperature changes, or light is given off.
    • The color changes.
Watch the atoms: the bonds in the hydrogen and oxygen molecules break, and the same atoms form new bonds to make water molecules.

Chemical Equations

Formulas and numbers that describe a reaction.

Chemical Equations

  • We represent the change that takes place in a reaction using a chemical equation. The starting substances are known as reactants and the ending substances are known as products.
  • Chemical Equation – symbolic representation of a chemical reaction using formulas and numbers.
  • Reactants – the starting substances in a chemical reaction.
  • Products – the ending substances in a chemical reaction.
  • Arrow – represents “yields” or “forms.” Shows the direction in which a chemical reaction proceeds.
This equation can be read as “1 molecule of CH4 reacts with 2 molecules of O2 to yield 1 molecule of CO2 and 2 molecules of H2O.”

Word Equations

  • Word Equation – a description of a chemical reaction that uses the names of the reactants and products instead of their formulas.
    • Ex: methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
    • The “+” is read as “and” (or “reacts with”). The arrow is read as “yields.”
  • A word equation does not show the formulas, the coefficients or the phases. A chemical equation shows all three.

Coefficients and Subscripts

Two kinds of numbers, two different meanings.

Coefficients and Subscripts

  • When reading a chemical equation, there are two important numbers to take note of:
    • Coefficients – large numbers placed in front of a chemical formula. Indicate the number of molecules or formula units (or moles) of a substance taking part in the reaction.
    • Subscripts – small numbers placed to the bottom right of an element. Indicate the number of atoms of an element in a molecule or compound.
  • No coefficient means 1. No subscript means 1 atom.
  • For ionic compounds, one unit of the formula is called a formula unit (ionic compounds do not form molecules). Ex: 3NaCl means 3 formula units of NaCl.
  • A subscript after parentheses multiplies every atom inside the parentheses. Ex: Ca(OH)2 contains 1 Ca, 2 O and 2 H.
  • To count the atoms in a formula with a coefficient, multiply each subscript by the coefficient. Ex: 3H2O contains 3 × 2 = 6 H atoms and 3 × 1 = 3 O atoms.
This equation can be read as “1 molecule of N2 reacts with 3 molecules of H2 to form 2 molecules of NH3.”
Why subscripts never change: changing a subscript changes the substance. H2O is water, but H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, a different compound. Changing a coefficient only changes how many molecules or formula units there are.
