Part1

Gaining and Losing Electrons

Atoms can gain or lose electrons, which gives them a charge. The number of protons does not change.

Ions

Sports drinks are advertised as replacing the electrolytes lost in sweat. These are charged particles of elements such as sodium and potassium, which the body uses to send nerve signals.

Atoms can sometimes gain or lose electrons, usually from their outermost shell. When this occurs they acquire an overall charge and are referred to as ions.

  • Ion – atom that has gained or lost electrons to acquire a charge.
  • Only the electrons change when an ion forms.
    • The protons are held tightly in the nucleus.
    • The atomic number, and the element, stay the same.
  • When an atom loses electrons it acquires a positive charge.
  • When an atom gains electrons it acquires a negative charge.
In an atom, the number of protons (positive charge) equals the number of electrons (negative charge), making the atom overall neutral. However, an ion, which has gained or lost electrons, has a different number of protons and electrons. Therefore it has an overall positive or negative charge.

Losing Electrons

When atoms lose electrons they acquire a positive charge. This is because they will have more protons (+) than electrons (−).

  • Losing multiple electrons creates an ion with a larger positive charge.
    • Losing 2 electrons creates an ion with a charge of “2+”.
    • Losing 3 electrons creates an ion with a charge of “3+”.

The electrons an atom loses are its outermost electrons. They are farthest from the nucleus, so by Coulomb’s law they are held the most weakly and are the easiest to remove (see Electric Charge & Coulomb’s Law).

Gaining Electrons

When atoms gain electrons they acquire a negative charge. This is because they will have more electrons (−) than protons (+).

  • Gaining multiple electrons creates an ion with a larger negative charge.
    • Gaining 2 electrons creates an ion with a charge of “2−”.
    • Gaining 3 electrons creates an ion with a charge of “3−”.
When atoms gain electrons, they acquire a negative charge. Atoms can gain multiple electrons to acquire a larger negative charge (2−, 3−, etc.)

Cations and Anions

  • Cation – name for an ion that has lost electrons and acquired a positive charge
  • Anion – name for an ion that has gained electrons and acquired a negative charge
This Magnesium ion would be considered a cation due to its positive charge.
This Sulfur ion would be considered an anion due to its negative charge.

Remove electrons from an atom or add extra ones to see how the charge and symbol change. A warning appears for ions that do not normally form.

Writing Ion Symbols

The charge of an ion is written in the top right corner of its symbol.

Ion Symbols

We denote the charge of an ion by writing it in the top right hand corner of the isotope symbol.

  • Write the number of its net charge followed by the sign of the charge
  • If the charge is either “1+” or “1−” you can just write “+” or “−”
  • A “+” charge means that the ion lost electrons
  • A “−” charge means that the ion gained electrons
Since this Beryllium has a 2+ charge, it lost 2 electrons
This Fluorine has a 1− charge. Note that it is not necessary to write the “1”. Since the charge is negative it gained 1 electron.

Counting Electrons in Ions

In an atom, the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons. However, this is not the case in an ion. In order to calculate the number of electrons, look at the atomic number to determine the original number of electrons the atom possessed. Then, if the ion’s charge is “+”, subtract that number of electrons. If the ion’s charge is “−”, add that number of electrons.

Original number of electrons = 13Electrons lost = 3Final number of electrons = 10
Original number of electrons = 8Electrons gained = 2Final number of electrons = 10

Predicting the Charge

Atoms gain or lose electrons to reach a noble gas configuration, so the charge can be predicted from the periodic table.

Stable Configurations

Atoms will form ions in order to acquire a stable electron configuration like one of the noble gases in group 18. It is often necessary to determine which noble gas configuration an ion possesses.

  1. Determine how many electrons the ion has
  2. Find the Noble Gas whose atomic number is equal to that number of electrons

Noble gases have full outermost shells, which makes them very stable. An ion with the same number of electrons has the same full outer shell, so it is stable too. (The ion is still sulfur: it has 16 protons, not 18.)

Original number of electrons = 16Electrons gained = 2New number of electrons = 16 + 2 = 18Noble Gas configuration = [Ar]
Sulfur normally has 16 electrons. However, this ion has a charge of “2−” meaning that it gained 2 electrons giving it a total of 18. Argon has an atomic number of 18 and therefore the same number of electrons. We can say that this sulfur ion has the same electron configuration as Argon (Ar).

Groups and Ionic Charges

Since atoms are trying to achieve similar electron configurations to elements in group 18, we see patterns in the charges that elements within the same group take.

  • Each electron lost results in a configuration similar to the previous element on the periodic table
  • Each electron gained results in a configuration similar to the next element on the periodic table
