Gibbs Free Energy

Gibbs Free Energy (G)

Energy and entropy sometimes pull in opposite directions. Water freezing gives off heat, which is favorable, but it becomes more ordered, which is not. In the 1870s, the American scientist J. Willard Gibbs, a professor at Yale, found a way to combine the two driving forces into a single quantity, so that one number could decide whether a change is spontaneous.

Gibbs free energy (G) is the energy of a system that is available to do work. The change in free energy (ΔG) tells whether a reaction is spontaneous at a given temperature, and it is calculated from the change in enthalpy, the change in entropy and the Kelvin temperature: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. If ΔG < 0, the reaction is spontaneous; if ΔG > 0, it is nonspontaneous; and if ΔG = 0, the system is at equilibrium.

If we analyze each part of the equation, it becomes clear why temperature matters. The entropy term, TΔS, is multiplied by the temperature, so at low temperatures, ΔH has the larger effect, while at high temperatures, TΔS takes over. This is why water freezes below 0 °C, where its negative ΔH wins, but melts above 0 °C, where its entropy term wins. Since ΔS is usually given in J/K while ΔH is in kJ, ΔS must be divided by 1000 before the two are combined. To solve a ΔG problem:
