Metallic Bonding & Alloys

In a metal, positive ions share one big “sea” of mobile electrons. That sea is why metals conduct, bend without breaking, and can be mixed into alloys that are harder than the metals they came from.

Metallic Bonding

Copper can be drawn into thin electrical wires, and gold can be hammered into sheets thinner than a sheet of paper. These properties set metals apart from ionic and covalent substances, and they come from a third type of bonding.

  • Recall that metals have a low electronegativity and a weak attraction for electrons.
  • When metal atoms are brought together, their valence electrons are lost, forming numerous positive ions.
    • The valence electrons are free to move among the ions, forming a sea of mobile electrons that holds the ions together.
    • Since each positive ion is attracted to the same electrons, they function to keep the metal ions “glued” together. This is known as a metallic bond.
  • Metallic Bond – attraction between positive metal ions and the sea of mobile electrons around them.
Both metal ions have an attraction to the same electrons, which keeps them stuck together.

Properties of Metals

  • The sea of mobile electrons is what creates many metals’ unique properties:
    • Good conductors of heat and electricity: since electrons are not tied to any one atom in metallic bonds, they are free to move around, making metals good conductors.
    • Malleable and ductile: the rows of metal ions form sheets that can easily slide past each other while the mobile electrons maintain the electrostatic attraction between them.
    • High melting and boiling points: there is a strong attraction between the positive metal ions and the negative “sea of electrons.”
      • Melting points are generally high but cover a wide range: mercury is a liquid at room temperature (−39 °C), while tungsten melts at 3422 °C.

Metal Malleability

  • Metals are malleable, meaning they can be bent into different shapes.
  • When a force is applied to the layers of metal ions in a metallic bond, they shift over. The mobile electrons move with the ions and keep them “glued” together.
Metals can bend without breaking apart because when layers of metal ions slide past each other, the electrons follow them, maintaining the attraction between layers. Compare this to an ionic crystal, which shatters when like charges line up.

Alloys

Very few metal objects are made of a single pure metal. The steel in bridges, the bronze in statues and the brass in musical instruments are all mixtures designed to be stronger or more useful than the metals they started from.

  • Alloys – mixtures of metals and other substances.
    • Alloys are mixtures (often homogeneous), not compounds: their composition can vary.
  • Usually, alloys are formed by melting down metals, mixing them together with another substance and allowing them to cool back down into the solid phase.
  • Depending on the amount and type of substance added to the metal, its characteristics (hardness, malleability, conductivity, etc.) can change.
Bronze is an example of an alloy. It is a mixture of copper (Cu) and tin (Sn).

Real alloys:

Substitutional vs. Interstitial Alloys

  • Depending on the size of the atoms being mixed, alloys are classified as either substitutional or interstitial.
  • Substitutional Alloy – atoms of a similar size to the metal are mixed. Some atoms of the added substance replace metal ions in the matrix.
  • Interstitial Alloy – smaller atoms are mixed with the metal. These smaller atoms insert themselves into holes between the metal ions.

Alloys and Hardness

  • Alloys often have a greater hardness and strength in comparison to the original metals from which they were derived.
  • Due to the size difference between atoms in alloys, the layers of metal ions are not able to slide past each other easily. This leads to a decrease in malleability but an increase in the hardness of the metal.
  • Adding too much of another component to an alloy can cause it to lose much of its malleability and become brittle.
