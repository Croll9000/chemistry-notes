Every Element’s Own Light

Emission Spectra

When the light from excited atoms is passed through a prism, it does not spread into a full rainbow. Instead, it separates into a set of thin, bright lines of color. This pattern is referred to as an emission spectrum.

Emission Spectrum – pattern of light emitted by atoms when electrons transition from excited states to ground states.

Each line comes from one specific drop between two energy levels. A bigger drop gives off a photon with more energy, which has a shorter wavelength. A smaller drop gives off a photon with less energy and a longer wavelength.

Since the energy levels of an atom are fixed, only certain drops are possible, so only certain colors appear. No two elements have the same set of energy levels. Thus, each element’s emission spectrum is unique, like a fingerprint.

This fingerprint once revealed a new element 150 million kilometers away. In 1868, astronomers studying the light of the Sun during a solar eclipse found a bright yellow line that did not match any element known on Earth. They concluded that the Sun contained an unknown element and named it helium, after helios, the Greek word for the Sun. Helium was not found on Earth until 27 years later.

The same idea gives fireworks their colors. Strontium compounds glow red, barium compounds glow green and copper compounds glow blue.

Each element has its own unique energy levels and therefore possible electron transitions. This means that each element can release a unique collection of light. We call this collection of light released when excited electrons return to the ground state, an emission spectrum.

  • Emission Spectrum – pattern of light emitted by atoms when electrons transition from excited states to ground states.
  • Each line comes from one specific drop between two energy levels.
    • A bigger drop gives off a photon with more energy (shorter wavelength).

When electricity passes through a gas, each element glows with its own color.

These are also called bright-line spectra. The wavelength scale is in nanometers (nm); 1 nm = 1 × 10−9 m. Line wavelengths are from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database.

Identifying Elements and Mixtures

Mixtures and Emission Spectra

The light from a distant star is a mixture of the light given off by every element in it, so its spectrum contains the lines of many different elements on top of one another. Astronomers identify the elements in a star by matching its lines with the known spectra of individual elements. The same method works in a lab with a glowing mixture of gases.

An element always gives off its whole set of lines. Thus, in order to say an element is in a mixture, all spectral lines from that element must be present in the mixture. If even one of an element’s lines is missing, that element cannot be in the mixture.

However, the mixture can contain extra lines from other elements. A mixture’s spectrum will usually have more lines than any one element’s, so the extra lines do not rule an element out. Only a missing line does.

Since emission spectra are unique to each element, they can be used to identify elements. We can also use them to determine which elements are present in a mixture.

  • In order to say an element is in a mixture, ALL spectral lines from that element must be present in the mixture.
This mixture contains both element A and element C. All spectral lines from both elements can be found in the mixture. Although some lines from element B are in the mixture, not all of them are. Therefore, we cannot say B is present in the mixture.
