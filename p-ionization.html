Ionization Energy

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom. The more strongly the nucleus holds an electron, the greater the ionization energy.

Part1

Removing an Electron

Energy must be added to remove an electron.

Ionization Energy

  • Recall that ionization is the process of removing an electron from an atom.
  • Electrons are held in place by their attraction to the positive nucleus.
    • It takes energy to remove an electron from an atom.
  • Ionization Energy – amount of energy required to remove an electron from an atom.
    • Unit – kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol)
To remove an electron from an atom, energy must be added to overcome the electron’s attraction to the nucleus.

1st, 2nd, 3rd… Ionization Energy

  • Not every electron in an atom requires the same amount of energy to remove.
  • The valence electrons, farthest from the nucleus, are the most loosely held and the easiest to remove.
  • Electrons in inner shells are attracted to the nucleus more strongly and are harder to remove.
  • Electrons in the same shell also get harder to remove one after another.
    • With fewer electrons left, there is less electron-electron repulsion, so the remaining electrons are held more tightly.
  • 1st Ionization Energy – energy required to remove the most loosely held electron from an atom.
  • 2nd Ionization Energy – energy required to remove the next most loosely held electron.
  • Ionization energy increases with each electron removed from an atom.
  • A huge jump in ionization energy shows when you start removing an inner shell.
    • Ex: magnesium (2-8-2) loses its 2 valence electrons fairly easily, then the 3rd electron takes about 5 times more energy.
Part2

The Trends

Ionization energy across a period and down a group.

Ionization Energy in Periods

  • Electrons are held in place by their attraction to the net positive charge of the nucleus.
    • The stronger the nuclear charge (# of protons), the more tightly the electrons are held.
  • As you move from left to right across a period, the ionization energy increases.
    • The nuclear charge (# of protons) increases, so there is a greater attraction to the nucleus.
Cause: the nuclear charge increases across a period while the number of energy levels stays the same, so the valence electrons are held more tightly.

Ionization Energy in Groups

  • Recall that as you move down a group, atoms get larger as energy levels are added.
    • The valence electrons are farther from the nucleus.
    • The attraction between them and the nucleus is weaker.
  • As you move down a group, the ionization energy decreases.
    • The larger the atom, the less tightly its outer electrons are held and the easier they are to remove.
Cause: each step down a group adds an energy level, so the valence electrons are farther from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons.

Ionization Energy Trends

  • Ionization energy increases from left to right across a period.
  • Ionization energy decreases from top to bottom down a group.
  • Metals (left side) have low ionization energies, so they lose electrons easily and form positive ions.
  • Nonmetals (right side) have high ionization energies, so they rarely lose electrons.
  • Noble gases have the highest ionization energy in each period.
Across a period: increasing nuclear charge, same number of energy levels → ionization energy increases.
Down a group: more energy levels, greater distance from the nucleus → ionization energy decreases.

Why Are There Dips?

Optional. Uses the orbital ideas from the Orbitals enrichment pages.

Exceptions to the Trend

  • The trend graph is not perfectly smooth. Across a period, ionization energy dips in two places.
    • Group 2 → group 13: Be (900 kJ/mol) is higher than B (801 kJ/mol). Mg (738) is higher than Al (578).
    • Group 15 → group 16: N (1402 kJ/mol) is higher than O (1314 kJ/mol). P (1012) is higher than S (1000).
  • Dip 1: a new subshell starts.
    • Boron’s outermost electron is the first one in the 2p subshell.
    • The 2p subshell is higher in energy than 2s, so that electron is easier to remove than beryllium’s 2s electrons.
  • Dip 2: the first paired electron in a p subshell.
    • Nitrogen’s three 2p electrons each have their own orbital (a half-filled subshell).
    • In oxygen, the 4th 2p electron must share an orbital with another electron.
    • The two electrons in that orbital repel each other, so one of them is easier to remove.
  • Half-filled and completely filled subshells are extra stable. The atom right after each one is a little easier to ionize.
