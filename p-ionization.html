Ionization Energy

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom. The more strongly the nucleus holds an electron, the greater the ionization energy.

Part1

Removing an Electron

Ionization Energy

Since electrons are held in an atom by their attraction to the positive nucleus, it takes energy to pull one away. Recall that removing an electron from an atom is referred to as ionization. The amount of energy needed to do it is the atom’s ionization energy, measured in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol).

Ionization Energy – amount of energy required to remove an electron from an atom.

To picture it, imagine holding a meter stick while someone tries to pull it out of your hand. If you hold it loosely, very little effort is needed to take it. If you grip it tightly, it takes a great deal of effort. The meter stick is like an electron, and your grip is like the attraction of the nucleus. The amount of effort needed to pull the stick away is like the ionization energy.

Thus, a high ionization energy means the electron is held tightly, and a low ionization energy means it is held loosely, so the atom forms a positive ion easily. For example, removing the valence electron from a mole of potassium atoms takes only 419 kJ. Removing one from a mole of argon atoms takes 1521 kJ. This means that argon grips its outer electron more than three times as tightly. Potassium metal tarnishes within seconds of being cut, as its atoms hand their electrons to the oxygen in the air. Argon gives up an electron to nothing at all under ordinary conditions.

  • Recall that ionization is the process of removing an electron from an atom.
  • Electrons are held in place by their attraction to the positive nucleus.
    • It takes energy to remove an electron from an atom.
  • Ionization Energy – amount of energy required to remove an electron from an atom.
    • Unit – kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol)
To remove an electron from an atom, energy must be added to overcome the electron’s attraction to the nucleus.

1st, 2nd, 3rd… Ionization Energy

An atom with several electrons can lose them one at a time, and each one has its own ionization energy. The energy needed to remove the most loosely held electron is the 1st ionization energy. The energy needed to remove the next one, from the ion that is left, is the 2nd ionization energy, and so on.

1st Ionization Energy – the energy required to remove the most loosely held electron from an atom.

2nd Ionization Energy – the energy required to remove the next most loosely held electron.

Ionization energy increases with each electron removed from an atom. Once an electron is gone, there is less electron-electron repulsion, and the remaining electrons are held more tightly by the same nucleus.

However, the increase is not steady. Consider magnesium. Removing its first electron takes 738 kJ/mol, and removing its second takes 1451 kJ/mol, about twice as much. The third takes 7733 kJ/mol, more than five times the second. The first two electrons came from magnesium’s outer shell. The third would have to come from the full shell beneath it, which is much closer to the nucleus and far less shielded.

This explains why magnesium always forms Mg2+ and never Mg3+. It also gives chemists a tool. A huge jump in ionization energy shows when an inner shell is reached, so the position of that jump reveals how many valence electrons an atom has.

  • Not every electron in an atom requires the same amount of energy to remove.
  • The valence electrons, farthest from the nucleus, are the most loosely held and the easiest to remove.
  • Electrons in inner shells are attracted to the nucleus more strongly and are harder to remove.
  • Electrons in the same shell also get harder to remove one after another.
    • With fewer electrons left, there is less electron-electron repulsion, so the remaining electrons are held more tightly.
  • 1st Ionization Energy – energy required to remove the most loosely held electron from an atom.
  • 2nd Ionization Energy – energy required to remove the next most loosely held electron.
  • Ionization energy increases with each electron removed from an atom.
  • A huge jump in ionization energy shows when you start removing an inner shell.
    Example
    Magnesium (2-8-2) loses its 2 valence electrons fairly easily, then the 3rd electron takes about 5 times more energy.
Part2

The Trends

Ionization Energy in Periods

As we move across period 3, the first ionization energy rises from 496 kJ/mol for sodium to 1521 kJ/mol for argon, roughly tripling. Every element in the period holds its valence electrons in the same third energy level. At the same time, the number of protons in the nucleus increases by one with each step. The stronger the nuclear charge, the more tightly the electrons are held. Thus, as we move from left to right across a period, the ionization energy increases, since a greater attraction to the nucleus means more energy is needed to pull an electron away.

  • Electrons are held in place by their attraction to the net positive charge of the nucleus.
    • The stronger the nuclear charge (# of protons), the more tightly the electrons are held.
  • As you move from left to right across a period, the ionization energy increases.
    • The nuclear charge (# of protons) increases, so there is a greater attraction to the nucleus.
Cause: the nuclear charge increases across a period while the number of energy levels stays the same, so the valence electrons are held more tightly.

Ionization Energy in Groups

Moving down a group, the trend reverses. Each step down adds an energy level, so the valence electron sits farther from the nucleus and is shielded by more inner electrons. Both effects weaken the attraction between the valence electron and the nucleus. Therefore, as we move down a group, the ionization energy decreases. In group 1, it falls from 520 kJ/mol for lithium to 376 kJ/mol for cesium.

Cesium’s low ionization energy has a practical use. Light striking a cesium surface carries enough energy to knock electrons loose, so cesium coatings were used in early light sensors, such as the “electric eyes” that open automatic doors and the tubes in early television cameras.

  • Recall that as you move down a group, atoms get larger as energy levels are added.
    • The valence electrons are farther from the nucleus.
    • The attraction between them and the nucleus is weaker.
  • As you move down a group, the ionization energy decreases.
    • The larger the atom, the less tightly its outer electrons are held and the easier they are to remove.
Cause: each step down a group adds an energy level, so the valence electrons are farther from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons.

Ionization Energy Trends

The trends in ionization energy are the mirror image of the trends in atomic radius. This should make sense, since the farther an electron is from the nucleus, the easier it is to remove. Ionization energy increases from left to right across a period. Ionization energy decreases from top to bottom down a group. The lowest values are at the bottom left of the periodic table, and the highest values are at the top right.

These trends explain the behavior of whole regions of the table. Metals, on the left side, have low ionization energies, so they lose electrons easily and form positive ions. Nonmetals, on the right side, have high ionization energies, so they rarely lose electrons. The noble gases have the highest ionization energy in each period, one more reason they are so unreactive.

  • Ionization energy increases from left to right across a period.
  • Ionization energy decreases from top to bottom down a group.
  • Metals (left side) have low ionization energies, so they lose electrons easily and form positive ions.
  • Nonmetals (right side) have high ionization energies, so they rarely lose electrons.
  • Noble gases have the highest ionization energy in each period.
Across a period: increasing nuclear charge, same number of energy levels → ionization energy increases.
Down a group: more energy levels, greater distance from the nucleus → ionization energy decreases.
