Advanced Ionization Energy Patterns

Graphed across a period, first ionization energy rises, but not perfectly smoothly. Boron is a little easier to ionize than beryllium, and oxygen a little easier than nitrogen, even though each has one more proton than the element before it. Nuclear charge and distance cannot explain these small dips. The orbitals introduced on the Orbitals enrichment pages can.

Why Are There Dips?

Exceptions to the Trend

If ionization energy depended only on nuclear charge, a graph of the first ionization energies across a period would rise smoothly from left to right. When the graph for period 2 is drawn from measured values, it does rise, but not smoothly. It dips in two places. Beryllium (900 kJ/mol) is higher than boron (801 kJ/mol), even though boron has one more proton. Nitrogen (1402 kJ/mol) is higher than oxygen (1314 kJ/mol). The same two dips appear in period 3, between magnesium (738) and aluminum (578), and between phosphorus (1012) and sulfur (1000).

Explaining the dips requires a closer look at how electrons are arranged within each energy level. Recall that the second energy level is divided into two sublevels, 2s and 2p.

The first dip happens where a new sublevel begins. Beryllium’s outermost electrons are both in the 2s sublevel. Boron’s fifth electron is the first one in the 2p sublevel. A 2p electron is slightly higher in energy than a 2s electron, and it does not get as close to the nucleus as a 2s electron does. The two 2s electrons are also partly between it and the nucleus, so they shield it. Since boron’s 2p electron is held less tightly from the start, less energy is needed to remove it, even though boron has one more proton than beryllium.

The second dip happens at the first paired electron in a p sublevel. Following Hund’s rule, nitrogen’s three 2p electrons each occupy their own orbital. In oxygen, the fourth 2p electron must share an orbital with another electron. Since electrons all carry a negative charge, the two electrons in that orbital repel each other. This repulsion pushes one of them partly away, which makes it easier to remove. Oxygen therefore has a lower ionization energy than nitrogen, even with its extra proton.

Thus, an electron in a new, higher-energy sublevel, or an electron that has to share an orbital, is easier to remove than the trend predicts. Note that these exceptions do not break the explanation of the trend. Nuclear charge, distance and shielding still decide how tightly an electron is held. The dips show that the same ideas still work when we look at sublevels and orbitals instead of whole energy levels.

  • The trend graph is not perfectly smooth. Across a period, ionization energy dips in two places.
    • Group 2 → group 13: Be (900 kJ/mol) is higher than B (801 kJ/mol). Mg (738) is higher than Al (578).
    • Group 15 → group 16: N (1402 kJ/mol) is higher than O (1314 kJ/mol). P (1012) is higher than S (1000).
  • Dip 1: a new subshell starts.
    • Boron’s outermost electron is the first one in the 2p subshell.
    • The 2p subshell is higher in energy than 2s, and the 2s electrons partly shield it from the nucleus.
    • As a result, boron’s 2p electron is easier to remove than beryllium’s 2s electrons.
  • Dip 2: the first paired electron in a p subshell.
    • Nitrogen’s three 2p electrons each have their own orbital.
    • In oxygen, the 4th 2p electron must share an orbital with another electron.
    • The two electrons in that orbital repel each other, so one of them is easier to remove.
