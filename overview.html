What Atoms Are Made Of

Subatomic Particles

All atoms are built from the same three kinds of particles, known as subatomic particles. Two of them are found in the nucleus, the tiny, dense center of the atom. Protons carry a positive charge, while neutrons carry no charge at all.

Protons – massive, positively charged particles found in the nucleus

Neutrons – massive, neutral particles found in the nucleus

The third particle, the electron, carries a negative charge and is found outside the nucleus.

Electrons – light, negative particles found outside the nucleus

Protons and neutrons are described as massive because they are heavy compared with an electron. A proton or a neutron has about 1836 times the mass of an electron. As a result, nearly all of an atom’s mass is packed into its tiny nucleus, while its nearly massless electrons occupy the space around it.

What makes one element different from another is how many of these particles its atoms contain. For instance, a gold atom has 79 protons in its nucleus, while an oxygen atom has 8. That difference is what separates a shiny, heavy metal from the invisible gas that we breathe.

Although the nucleus contains almost all of the mass, it is the electrons that determine how an atom reacts. Since the electrons are found on the outside of the atom, they are the part that comes into contact with other atoms.

Atoms are composed of 3 subatomic particles:

  • Protons – massive, positively charged particles found in the nucleus
  • Neutrons – massive, neutral particles found in the nucleus
  • Electrons – light, negative particles found outside the nucleus

“Massive” here means heavy compared with an electron: a proton or neutron has about 1836 times the mass of an electron. Protons and neutrons have almost the same mass as each other.

An atom of Helium‑4 has 2 protons and 2 neutrons in its nucleus. Its 1st energy level possesses 2 electrons.

Mass and Charge

Subatomic Particles: Mass and Charge

A single carbon atom has a mass of about 0.000000000000000000000020 g. Numbers like this are so awkward that scientists created a unit of mass sized for atoms. They chose one particular atom, carbon-12, with 6 protons and 6 neutrons, and defined its mass as exactly 12 units. Every other atom is measured by comparison with it. This unit is referred to as the unified mass unit (u).

Unified Mass Unit (u) – unit of mass used to measure very small particles. Equal to 112 of the mass of a C-12 atom

Since a carbon-12 nucleus holds 12 particles of nearly equal mass, a proton and a neutron each have a mass of about 1 u. An electron has a mass of only about 0.0005 u, so small that it is usually treated as 0.

Charges are measured in units as well. A proton has a charge of +1, an electron has a charge of −1 and a neutron has no charge.

Putting these numbers together gives two useful shortcuts. The mass of an atom in u is about equal to its number of protons plus neutrons. The overall charge of an atom is its number of protons minus its number of electrons.

When measuring the masses of particles as small as a proton or neutron, we do not use units like grams or kilograms which are very large. Scientists developed a new unit of mass measurement known as a “unified mass unit”, abbreviated with the symbol u.

What Holds an Atom Together?

Attraction Between Charges

Charged particles push or pull on each other, and they follow one simple rule. Opposite charges attract. Like charges repel.

Consider what happens when you rub a balloon on your hair. Rubbing moves some electrons from your hair onto the balloon, leaving the balloon with extra negative charge and your hair with extra positive charge. The hair is drawn toward the balloon, since opposite charges attract. At the same time, the individual hairs all carry the same charge, so they push away from one another and stand apart.

This one rule is what holds an atom together. The negative electrons are held in the atom by their attraction to the positive protons in the nucleus. The strength of that pull depends on two things: the size of the charges and how far apart they are.

Atoms are also mostly empty space. If the nucleus of an atom were the size of a marble placed at the center of a football stadium, the electrons would be found out beyond the highest seats, and everything in between would be empty. Why, then, does a table feel solid? The answer is not that its atoms are packed tightly together. When your hand presses on the table, the electrons of your hand repel the electrons of the table, and that push is what you feel.

  • Charged particles push or pull on each other.
    • Opposite charges attract.
    • Like charges repel.
  • The negative electrons are held in the atom by their attraction to the positive protons in the nucleus.
  • The strength of the pull depends on two things:
    • the size of the charges
    • how far apart they are
  • Atoms are mostly empty space.
