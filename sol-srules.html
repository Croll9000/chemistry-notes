The Solubility Rules

Solubility Rules

Before an X-ray of the stomach, a patient drinks a thick, white mixture of barium sulfate and water. Dissolved barium ions are poisonous, yet the patient is perfectly safe, because barium sulfate is so insoluble that almost none of it breaks into ions. The solid simply coats the stomach and blocks the X-rays, making the stomach visible on the image, and then passes through the body. Swap the sulfate for chloride, and barium chloride, which is soluble, would be dangerous to drink. Whether an ionic compound dissolves can make the difference between a medicine and a poison.

Chemists predict the solubility of compounds like these with a longer list of solubility rules, which covers many more ions than the short list of ions that always dissolve. Each rule names a group of ions and the exceptions to it. Since some ions appear in more than one rule, a rule near the top of the list takes priority over the rules below it. Sodium phosphate, for instance, is soluble because rule 1 (group 1 ions) comes before the rule that phosphates are insoluble. The rules are:

  1. Compounds of group 1 ions (Li+, Na+, K+, etc.) and ammonium (NH4+) are soluble.
  2. Nitrates (NO3−), acetates (C2H3O2−) and chlorates (ClO3−) are soluble.
  3. Chlorides (Cl−), bromides (Br−) and iodides (I−) are soluble, except when combined with Ag+, Pb2+ and Hg22+.
  4. Sulfates (SO42−) are soluble, except when combined with Ba2+ and Pb2+ (insoluble) or Ca2+ and Ag+ (slightly soluble).
  5. Fluorides (F−) are soluble, except when combined with Mg2+, Ca2+, Ba2+ and Pb2+.
  6. With the exception of rule 1, carbonates (CO32−), phosphates (PO43−) and sulfites (SO32−) are insoluble.
  7. With the exception of rule 1 and the barium ion (Ba2+), hydroxides (OH−) are insoluble. Calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble.

The Solubility Table

Reading the Solubility Table

Silver nitrate is used to test water for chloride ions. Add a few drops of silver nitrate solution to tap water, and even a few milligrams of chloride per liter produce a cloudy white precipitate of silver chloride. A solubility table lets a chemist predict a result like this at a glance, without working through the rules one by one, because it shows every combination of common positive and negative ions in a single grid.

The positive ions are listed across the top of the table, while the negative ions are listed down the side. To read it, find the positive ion at the top of a column and the negative ion at the start of a row. The box where they meet gives the solubility of the compound: S for soluble, SS for slightly soluble and I for insoluble. A solution of silver nitrate mixed with a solution of sodium chloride can make two new combinations, silver chloride and sodium nitrate. The table marks silver chloride with an I and sodium nitrate with an S, so a compound marked I forms a precipitate when solutions of its two ions are mixed, while a compound marked S stays dissolved.

  • Find the positive ion at the top of a column and the negative ion at the start of a row.
  • The box where they meet gives the solubility: S = soluble, SS = slightly soluble, I = insoluble.
  • A compound marked I forms a precipitate when solutions of its ions are mixed.
  • Click any box in the table to highlight its row and column.
