Polyatomic Ions

Some ions are made of several atoms bonded together. They act as a single unit in ionic compounds. You do not need to memorize them: questions will give you their formulas.

Polyatomic Ions

Household bleach is a solution of sodium hypochlorite, NaClO. Each hypochlorite ion, ClO−, is a chlorine atom and an oxygen atom bonded together that carries a 1− charge as a whole, and it stays together as one unit when the compound dissolves. Not all ions are single atoms; sometimes a whole group of covalently bonded atoms carries a net charge. Polyatomic ions are covalently bonded groups of atoms that possess a charge, and they take part in ionic bonding just like monatomic ions, ions made of a single atom such as Na+. Baking soda, plaster and many farm fertilizers all contain polyatomic ions.

  • Not all ions take the form of single atoms.
    • Sometimes whole molecules can possess a net charge.
    • These are referred to as polyatomic ions.
  • Polyatomic Ions – covalently bonded molecules that possess a charge.
  • Polyatomic ions can take part in ionic bonding just like monatomic ions.
    • Monatomic ion – an ion made of a single atom (ex: Na+, Cl−).
Both ammonium (NH4+) and carbonate (CO32−) are examples of polyatomic ions. Notice how their structures include covalent bonds (lines) but they possess a net charge.

Naming Oxyanions

An oxyanion is a polyatomic ion that contains oxygen and has a negative charge. Many oxyanions come in families with the same elements and the same charge but different numbers of oxygen atoms, and their names follow a pattern. The -ate ending names the most common form, such as nitrate, NO3−, and the -ite ending names the ion with one less oxygen, such as nitrite, NO2−. The prefix per- with -ate means one more oxygen than the -ate ion, and hypo- with -ite means one less oxygen than the -ite ion, as in the hypochlorite ion in bleach. Adding “hydrogen” to the name adds an H to the formula and makes the charge 1 less negative, so carbonate, CO32−, becomes hydrogen carbonate, HCO3−.

  • Oxyanion – a polyatomic ion that contains oxygen and has a negative charge.
  • Many oxyanions come in families with the same elements and the same charge but different numbers of oxygen atoms. Their names follow a pattern:
    • -ate: the most common form (ex: nitrate, NO3−)
    • -ite: one less oxygen than the -ate ion (ex: nitrite, NO2−)
    • per- … -ate: one more oxygen than the -ate ion
    • hypo- … -ite: one less oxygen than the -ite ion
  • Adding “hydrogen” to the name adds an H to the formula and makes the charge 1 less negative (ex: carbonate CO32− → hydrogen carbonate HCO3−).

Formulas and Names with Polyatomic Ions

Writing Formulas with Polyatomic Ions

Sodium hydroxide, NaOH, the main ingredient in many drain cleaners, contains one sodium ion for every hydroxide ion. Formulas with polyatomic ions are written with the same steps as binary ionic compounds, and the charges must still add up to zero. The polyatomic ion is treated as one unit, so the subscripts inside it are never changed. If more than one of a polyatomic ion is needed, its formula is surrounded by parentheses and the subscript is written outside: Ca(OH)2 contains 1 Ca2+ ion and 2 OH− ions. If there is only one, no parentheses are needed, as in NaOH.

  • Write formulas using the same steps as binary ionic compounds. The charges must still add up to zero.
  • Treat the polyatomic ion as one unit. Never change the subscripts inside a polyatomic ion.
  • If there is more than one of a polyatomic ion, surround the polyatomic formula with parentheses and write the subscript outside.
    • Ca(OH)2 contains 1 Ca2+ ion and 2 OH− ions.
    • If there is only one, no parentheses are needed: NaOH.

Naming Compounds with Polyatomic Ions

Compounds with polyatomic ions are named the same way as other ionic compounds, except that a polyatomic ion keeps its own name, with no “-ide” ending. Na2CO3, for example, is sodium carbonate. The steps for naming a compound with a polyatomic ion are:

  1. Write the name of the positive ion. If it is a metal with more than one possible charge, include its charge as a Roman numeral.
  2. Write the name of the negative ion. A polyatomic ion keeps its own name (no “-ide” change).
