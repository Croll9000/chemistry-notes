Polyatomic Ions

Baking soda, plaster and many farm fertilizers all contain ions made of more than one atom. These groups of atoms stay together through chemical reactions and act as a single unit.

  • Not all ions take the form of single atoms. Sometimes whole molecules can possess a net charge. These are referred to as polyatomic ions.
  • Polyatomic Ions – covalently bonded molecules that possess a charge.
  • Polyatomic ions can take part in ionic bonding just like monatomic ions.
    • Monatomic ion – an ion made of a single atom (ex: Na+, Cl−).
Both ammonium (NH4+) and carbonate (CO32−) are examples of polyatomic ions. Notice how their structures include covalent bonds (lines) but they possess a net charge.

Naming Oxyanions

  • Oxyanion – a polyatomic ion that contains oxygen and has a negative charge.
  • Many oxyanions come in families with the same elements and the same charge but different numbers of oxygen atoms. Their names follow a pattern:
    • -ate: the most common form (ex: nitrate, NO3−)
    • -ite: one less oxygen than the -ate ion (ex: nitrite, NO2−)
    • per- … -ate: one more oxygen than the -ate ion
    • hypo- … -ite: one less oxygen than the -ite ion
  • Adding “hydrogen” to the name adds an H to the formula and makes the charge 1 less negative (ex: carbonate CO32− → hydrogen carbonate HCO3−).

A polyatomic ion stays together as one unit.

Writing Formulas with Polyatomic Ions

  • Write formulas using the same steps as binary ionic compounds. The charges must still add up to zero.
  • Treat the polyatomic ion as one unit. Never change the subscripts inside a polyatomic ion.
  • If there is more than one of a polyatomic ion, surround the polyatomic formula with parentheses and write the subscript outside.
    • Ca(OH)2 contains 1 Ca2+ ion and 2 OH− ions.
    • If there is only one, no parentheses are needed: NaOH.

Naming Compounds with Polyatomic Ions

  1. Write the name of the positive ion. If it is a metal with more than one possible charge, include its charge as a Roman numeral.
  2. Write the name of the negative ion. A polyatomic ion keeps its own name (no “-ide” change).
