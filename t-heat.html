Heat & Heat Transfer

Heat always flows from a higher temperature to a lower temperature, by conduction, convection or radiation, until the objects reach thermal equilibrium.

Part1

Heat

When two objects at different temperatures are brought near one another, energy is transferred from the warmer object to the cooler one. We call this transferred energy heat.

Heat

Hold a cold can of soda and your hand gets cold while the can warms up. Nothing called “cold” moved into your hand. Instead, energy moved out of your hand and into the can. In everyday speech, “heat” and “hot” mean almost the same thing, but in chemistry heat has a precise meaning: it is energy that is moving from one object to another because of a difference in temperature.

  • When two objects at different temperatures are brought near one another, energy is always transferred from the object at the higher temperature to the object at the lower temperature. The energy that is transferred is heat.
    • Heat – energy transferred from a high temperature object to a low temperature object.
  • Heat always flows from objects at a higher temperature to objects at a lower temperature. It never flows on its own from cold to hot.
  • Heat is measured in joules (J) or kilojoules (kJ), like all forms of energy.
  • Your hand feels “cold” when you touch ice because heat is flowing out of your hand into the ice, not because “cold” flows into your hand.

Systems and Surroundings

To keep track of where heat goes, chemists split the universe into two parts: the part they are studying and everything else. This is the same idea used in the Chemical Reactions unit.

  • System – specific part of the universe that is being focused on.
  • Surroundings – everything that is not part of the system.
    • When an ice cube melts in your hand, the ice is the system and your hand is part of the surroundings.
  • Heat flows into or out of a system depending on which is at the higher temperature:
    • Endothermic: heat flows from the surroundings into the system. The surroundings feel colder.
    • Exothermic: heat flows from the system into the surroundings. The surroundings feel warmer.
  • An open system allows both matter and energy to pass; a closed system allows only energy to pass; an isolated system allows neither. A foam-cup calorimeter is designed to be as close to an isolated system as possible.
Part2

Heat Transfer

Heat can be transferred between objects in three different ways. Each one explains a different everyday observation, from a hot pan handle to the warmth of sunlight.

Heat Transfer

Sit beside a campfire on a cool night and you experience all three ways that heat can travel. The metal skewer holding your marshmallow grows hot all the way to your fingers, sparks and smoke rise on a column of warm air, and your face feels warm even though nothing is touching it. Each of these is a different mechanism of heat transfer, and every heat transfer you encounter is one of them or a combination.

  • Heat can be transferred between objects in three different ways:
    • Conduction – heat transfer between objects in contact. Fast-moving particles in the hot object collide with particles in the cold object and transfer energy.
    • Convection – heat transfer by currents of a fluid (a liquid or gas). Warm fluid rises, cools, sinks and repeats the cycle.
    • Radiation – heat transfer by light waves (such as infrared light) from a hot object to a cold object. It does not need particles, so it can cross empty space.
  • Energy from the Sun reaches Earth only by radiation, because space is nearly empty.

Part3

Thermal Equilibrium

Heat continues to flow only as long as a temperature difference exists. Once two objects reach the same temperature, there is no longer a net flow of heat between them.

Thermal Equilibrium

A hot cup of cocoa left on the table does not stay hot, and it never gets colder than the room. It cools quickly at first, then more and more slowly, until it matches the temperature of the room. After that, its temperature stops changing. The cocoa and the room have reached a balance.

  • Since heat flows from high temperature to low temperature, there is no net flow of heat when two objects reach the same temperature.
    • Thermal Equilibrium – state in which there is no net flow of heat between objects because they are at the same temperature.
  • At thermal equilibrium, particles still collide and energy is still transferred back and forth between the objects, but there is no net flow of heat.
  • The hot object loses heat and its temperature falls; the cold object gains heat and its temperature rises. In an insulated system, the heat lost by the hot object equals the heat gained by the cold object.

Part4

Thermal Conductivity

Not all materials transfer heat at the same rate. This property explains why cookware is made of metal while oven mitts and thermal mugs are made of insulating materials.

Thermal Conductivity

On a cold morning, a metal doorknob feels colder than a wooden door, even though both are at the same temperature. The metal pulls heat out of your hand much faster than the wood does, so your hand cools faster. Cooks use the same idea: pots are made of metal so heat moves in quickly, while handles and oven mitts are made of materials that keep heat out.

  • Not all objects transfer heat at the same rate. We describe how well a material transfers heat with its thermal conductivity.
    • Thermal Conductivity (k) – a measure of a material’s ability to transfer heat.
    • Unit: W/m·K (watts per meter-kelvin). The higher the value, the faster heat moves through the material.
  • Thermal conductivity depends on the temperature, the structure of the material and the mobility of its electrons.
    • Metals have high thermal conductivities because of their mobile electrons, which carry energy through the metal. Heat flows into and out of metals very quickly.
    • Molecular substances usually have low thermal conductivities because their electrons are not mobile.
      • Heat flows into and out of them slowly.
      • Materials like these are good insulators.

Factors that Affect the Rate of Heat Transfer

A thick winter coat keeps you warmer than a thin jacket, and a wide pan heats food faster than a narrow one on the same burner. Engineers who design houses, coolers and computer chips control four factors to speed up or slow down the flow of heat.

  • Temperature difference (ΔT) – the larger the temperature difference between the two objects, the faster heat flows.
  • Surface area in contact (A) – the larger the area in contact, the faster heat flows.
  • Thickness of material separating the objects (d) – the thicker the material between the objects, the slower heat flows.
  • Thermal conductivity (k) – the higher the thermal conductivity of the material, the faster heat flows.
  • These combine into one equation (in joules per second, J/s):
rate of heat transfer=kAΔTd
