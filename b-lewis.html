Drawing Lewis Structures (Covalent)

Lewis dot diagrams show the valence electrons of a single atom. To understand how a molecule is held together, chemists extend these diagrams to show which electrons are shared and which are not.

Steps for drawing Lewis structures for covalent molecules:

  1. Draw the Lewis dot diagram for each atom in the compound.
  2. Determine which atom can form the most bonds and place that in the center of the molecule.
  3. Circle pairs of electrons between the central atom and outer atoms to represent covalent bonds.
  4. After each bond is formed, stop and count the number of valence electrons around each atom.
    • Remember that bonded (circled) electrons count towards both atoms in the bond.
  5. If necessary, form double or triple bonds.
  6. Stop when each atom has a full valence shell.
    • Hydrogen is stable with 2 valence electrons.
  7. Redraw any circled electrons as lines to represent covalent bonds.
  • Lone Pair – a pair of valence electrons that is not shared with another atom.

The Electron-Counting Method

A second way to draw Lewis structures, which also works for polyatomic ions.

Drawing Lewis Structures by Counting Electrons

Steps to drawing Lewis structures for covalent molecules:

  1. Count up the total number of valence electrons in each atom of the molecule. If the molecule has a charge, add/subtract the appropriate number of electrons.
    • A negative charge means extra electrons (add). A positive charge means missing electrons (subtract).
  2. Determine the atom that can form the greatest number of bonds and place that in the center of the molecule.
  3. Draw a single bond to every other atom in the molecule. Keep track of the total number of electrons used.
  4. Place any remaining electrons as lone pairs around the outer atoms.
  5. If any electrons remain, add them as lone pairs to the central atom.
  6. Double/triple bond if necessary.

Coordinate Covalent Bonds

  • In a normal covalent bond, each atom contributes 1 electron to the bond. However, sometimes a single atom can contribute both electrons to the bond. This type of bond is known as a coordinate covalent bond.
  • Coordinate Covalent Bond – a covalent bond where both shared electrons come from the same atom.
  • Once it forms, a coordinate covalent bond is identical to any other single bond.
Note how in the formation of the bond between NH3 and BF3, the nitrogen atom contributes 2 electrons, as opposed to each atom contributing 1. The bond that forms is known as a coordinate covalent bond.

Resonance Structures

  • To best represent the delocalized nature of some electrons in molecules, we draw sets of Lewis structures that show different possible locations for the electrons and higher order bonds. We call these sets of structures resonance structures.
  • Resonance Structures – sets of Lewis diagrams that show varying positions of electrons and bonds within the same molecule.
  • In reality, no one Lewis structure is correct. Rather, the actual molecule is a blend of all possible Lewis structures, with electrons delocalized across multiple bonds.
    • Delocalized – spread out over more than two atoms instead of belonging to one bond.
Ozone, O3, is a molecule consisting of 3 oxygen atoms bonded together with a single bond and a double bond. We can draw 2 possible resonance structures of ozone, with the double bond to either the left or right oxygen. In reality, the bonding electrons are spread out across each bond, creating something like two 1½ bonds rather than a single and a double bond.
Drawing a normal Lewis structure for carbonate (CO32−) would lead one to believe it has one double and two single bonds, with charges of 1− on the single-bonded oxygens. Carbonate really has 3 bonds of equal length, as the electrons from the double bond are spread out over the whole ion. Additionally, each oxygen has a partially negative charge.

Formal Charge

  • When drawing Lewis structures, it can be useful to keep track of how many electrons each atom “owns” compared to how many it started with.
  • Formal Charge – the charge an atom would have if the electrons in each of its bonds were shared equally.
  • Formal Charge = (valence electrons) − (lone pair electrons) − (number of bonds)
    • Each line counts as 1 electron that belongs to the atom, since the other electron belongs to its partner.
    • The formal charges in a structure always add up to the overall charge of the molecule or ion.
  • When more than one structure can be drawn, the best structure is usually the one with formal charges closest to zero, with any negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom.
