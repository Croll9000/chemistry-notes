Unsaturated and Saturated Solutions

Unsaturated and Saturated

Stir a spoonful of sugar into a glass of iced tea and it disappears. Keep adding spoonfuls, and eventually the grains stop dissolving and collect on the bottom no matter how long you stir. At a given temperature, a given amount of solvent can dissolve only a certain mass of solute, and the tea has dissolved all the sugar it can hold. Comparing the mass of solute dissolved with this maximum tells us how “full” a solution is.

A solution that can still dissolve more solute at its temperature is called an unsaturated solution. A solution that holds the maximum amount of dissolved solute at its temperature is called a saturated solution. A solution is saturated if it holds exactly the maximum amount with no solid present, and it is also saturated if undissolved solute sits on the bottom. Any extra solute added to a saturated solution settles out without dissolving.

'
  • Unsaturated Solution – a solution that can dissolve more solute at its temperature.
  • Saturated Solution – a solution that holds the maximum amount of dissolved solute at its temperature.
    • A solution is saturated if it holds exactly the maximum amount of solute with no solid present.
    • A solution is also saturated if undissolved solute (a precipitate) sits at the bottom. Any extra solute added to a saturated solution settles out without dissolving.
Part2

Supersaturated Solutions

Supersaturated Solutions

Some reusable hand warmers hold a clear liquid and a small metal disk. Click the disk and, within seconds, the whole pouch turns into a warm, white solid. The liquid inside was holding more dissolved sodium acetate than water can normally hold at that temperature, and the click was enough to make the extra solid crystallize all at once. A solution that holds more dissolved solute than it normally can at its temperature is called a supersaturated solution.

A supersaturated solution has to be made carefully. The solute is dissolved in hot solvent, where its solubility is high, and the saturated solution is then cooled slowly without being disturbed. The result is unstable: a seed crystal, a scratch or a bump makes the extra solute crystallize at once, leaving a saturated solution behind.

'
  • Supersaturated Solution – a solution that holds more dissolved solute than it normally can at its temperature.
  • It is made by dissolving solute in hot solvent, then cooling the saturated solution slowly without disturbing it.
  • A supersaturated solution is unstable. A seed crystal, a scratch or a bump makes the extra solute crystallize at once, leaving a saturated solution behind.

Dilute and Concentrated Solutions

Dilute and Concentrated

A pharmacy sells hydrogen peroxide as a 3% solution for cleaning cuts, while the same chemical at 35% is a hazardous material that burns skin. The difference is only how much solute is dissolved in the water. The amount of solute dissolved in a given amount of solvent or solution is called its concentration. A solution with a relatively small amount of solute in a relatively large amount of solvent is described as dilute, and a solution with a relatively large amount of solute in a relatively small amount of solvent is described as concentrated.

These two words are relative: a solution is dilute or concentrated only in comparison with another solution. They also say nothing about whether a solution is saturated. A saturated solution of a solute that is only slightly soluble can still be dilute. A saturated solution of potassium chlorate at 0 °C, for example, holds only about 5 g of solute in 100 g of water.

  • Concentration – the amount of solute dissolved in a given amount of solvent or solution.
  • Dilute – a solution with a relatively small amount of solute dissolved in a relatively large amount of solvent.
  • Concentrated – a solution with a relatively large amount of solute dissolved in a relatively small amount of solvent.
  • Dilute and concentrated are relative terms: a solution is dilute or concentrated compared with another solution.
    • A saturated solution of a solute that is only slightly soluble can still be dilute. For example, a saturated solution of potassium chlorate at 0 °C holds only about 5 g in 100 g of water.
