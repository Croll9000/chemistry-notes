Saturation and Concentration

Saturated solutions hold the maximum dissolved solute; unsaturated solutions can dissolve more; supersaturated solutions hold more than normal. Dilute and concentrated compare solutions.

Part1

Unsaturated and Saturated Solutions

Unsaturated and Saturated

Stir a spoonful of sugar into a glass of iced tea and it disappears. Keep adding spoonfuls, and eventually the grains stop dissolving and collect on the bottom no matter how long you stir. At a given temperature, a given amount of solvent can dissolve only a certain mass of solute, and the tea has dissolved all the sugar it can hold. Comparing the mass of solute dissolved with this maximum tells us how “full” a solution is.

A solution that can still dissolve more solute at its temperature is referred to as an unsaturated solution. A solution that holds the maximum amount of dissolved solute at its temperature is referred to as a saturated solution. There are two ways a solution can be saturated. A solution is saturated if it holds exactly the maximum amount of solute with no solid present, and it is also saturated if undissolved solute sits on the bottom.

The sugar at the bottom of a saturated glass of tea is not as still as it looks. Sugar molecules continue to leave the solid and dissolve, while dissolved sugar molecules continue to collide with the solid and stick to it. In a saturated solution, these two processes happen at equal rates, so the amount of dissolved sugar stays the same. This balance is the same kind of equilibrium that forms between a liquid and its vapor in a closed container, which is why vapor pressure stays constant at a given temperature. Thus, any extra solute added to a saturated solution settles out without increasing the amount dissolved.

'
  • Unsaturated Solution – a solution that can dissolve more solute at its temperature.
  • Saturated Solution – a solution that holds the maximum amount of dissolved solute at its temperature.
    • A solution is saturated if it holds exactly the maximum amount of solute with no solid present.
    • A solution is also saturated if undissolved solute (a precipitate) sits at the bottom. Any extra solute added to a saturated solution settles out without dissolving.
Part2

Supersaturated Solutions

Supersaturated Solutions

Some reusable hand warmers hold a clear liquid and a small metal disk. Click the disk and, within seconds, white crystals spread through the pouch until it becomes a warm, solid block. The liquid inside was holding far more dissolved sodium acetate than water can normally hold at room temperature, and the click was enough to make the extra solute crystallize all at once. Crystallizing releases energy, which is why the pouch warms up. Boiling the pouch redissolves the crystals, and the hand warmer can be used again.

A solution that holds more dissolved solute than it normally can at its temperature is referred to as a supersaturated solution. A supersaturated solution has to be made carefully. The solute is dissolved in hot solvent, where its solubility is high, and the saturated solution is then cooled slowly without being disturbed. As the solution cools, the extra solute has nowhere to start forming crystals, so it stays dissolved.

The result is unstable. A seed crystal, a scratch on the container or a bump gives the dissolved particles a surface to build on, and the extra solute crystallizes at once, leaving a saturated solution behind. Honey is a natural example: it is a supersaturated solution of sugars in a small amount of water, which is why a jar of honey slowly turns grainy as crystals form over time.

'
  • Supersaturated Solution – a solution that holds more dissolved solute than it normally can at its temperature.
  • It is made by dissolving solute in hot solvent, then cooling the saturated solution slowly without disturbing it.
  • A supersaturated solution is unstable. A seed crystal, a scratch or a bump makes the extra solute crystallize at once, leaving a saturated solution behind.

Dilute and Concentrated Solutions

Dilute and Concentrated

A pharmacy sells hydrogen peroxide as a 3% solution for cleaning cuts, while a 35% solution of the same chemical is a hazardous material that burns the skin and must be shipped with warning labels. Both bottles contain the same two substances, hydrogen peroxide and water. The only difference is how much solute is dissolved in the water.

The amount of solute dissolved in a given amount of solvent or solution is referred to as its concentration. A solution with a relatively small amount of solute dissolved in a relatively large amount of solvent is described as dilute, while a solution with a relatively large amount of solute dissolved in a relatively small amount of solvent is described as concentrated.

Dilute and concentrated are relative terms: a solution is dilute or concentrated only in comparison with another solution. The 3% peroxide is concentrated compared with a 1% solution, but dilute compared with the 35% solution. Additionally, these words say nothing about whether a solution is saturated, because saturation compares a solution with its own maximum, while concentration describes the actual amount dissolved. A saturated solution of potassium chlorate at 0 °C holds only a few grams of solute in 100 g of water, so it is saturated and dilute at the same time. Sugar, on the other hand, dissolves so well that a saturated solution at 20 °C holds about 200 g of sugar in 100 g of water, which is concentrated by any standard. Thus, a saturated solution of a solute that is only slightly soluble can still be dilute.

  • Concentration – the amount of solute dissolved in a given amount of solvent or solution.
  • Dilute – a solution with a relatively small amount of solute dissolved in a relatively large amount of solvent.
  • Concentrated – a solution with a relatively large amount of solute dissolved in a relatively small amount of solvent.
  • Dilute and concentrated are relative terms: a solution is dilute or concentrated compared with another solution.
    • A saturated solution of a solute that is only slightly soluble can still be dilute. For example, a saturated solution of potassium chlorate at 0 °C holds only about 5 g in 100 g of water.
