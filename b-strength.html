What Affects Bond Strength

Some bonds take three times as much energy to break as others. The number of shared pairs, the size of the atoms and the polarity of the bond all play a part.

Part1

Bond Order

Bond Strength and Bond Order

The number of shared pairs between two atoms is referred to as the bond order. It is 1 for a single bond, 2 for a double bond and 3 for a triple bond. Recall that the strength of a bond increases with the number of electron pairs shared between 2 atoms, so bond strength follows the order triple bond > double bond > single bond.

This explains a familiar packaging trick. Bags of potato chips are filled with nitrogen gas instead of air. Oxygen would react with the oils in the chips and make them go stale. The triple bond in N2 takes 945 kJ/mol to break, almost twice the 498 kJ/mol needed for the double bond in O2, so nitrogen molecules rarely break apart to react with anything, and the chips stay fresh.

  • As the number of bonds between 2 atoms increases, the amount of energy required to break those bonds increases.
    • The strength of a bond increases with the number of electron pairs shared between 2 atoms.
  • Bond Strength: Triple Bond > Double Bond > Single Bond
  • Bond Order – the number of shared pairs of electrons between two atoms (single = 1, double = 2, triple = 3).

Polarity and Bond Strength

Hydrogen fluoride, the acid used to etch designs into glass, contains one of the strongest single bonds known. Breaking a mole of H–F bonds takes 570 kJ. Compare that with the two nonpolar bonds the same elements form on their own: H–H takes 436 kJ, and F–F only 159 kJ. If the strength of a bond were just the average of these two, H–F should take only about 298 kJ to break.

The extra strength comes from polarity. In a polar bond, the partially positive (δ+) and partially negative (δ−) ends attract each other, and this adds to the attraction of both nuclei for the shared electrons. As a result, polar bonds are generally stronger than the average of the nonpolar bonds that the same elements form when bonded to themselves. The more polar the bond, the larger this extra strength.

  • Polar bonds are generally stronger than the average of the strengths of the nonpolar bonds that the same elements form when bonded to themselves.
    • In a polar bond, the partially positive (δ+) and partially negative (δ−) ends attract each other. This adds to the attraction of both nuclei for the shared electrons.
    • The more polar the bond, the larger this extra strength.

Atom Size and Bond Strength

Atom Size and Bond Strength

The bonds between hydrogen and the halogens grow steadily weaker down group 17. Breaking a mole of H–F bonds takes 570 kJ. H–Cl takes 431 kJ, H–Br 366 kJ and H–I only 299 kJ. In each bond, hydrogen shares a single pair of electrons with a halogen atom, so the bond order is the same. What changes is the size of the halogen atom.

If we analyze the bond using Coulomb’s law, the reason becomes clear. The shared electrons in a bond between small atoms are closer to the positive nuclei. A smaller distance creates a stronger electrical attraction between the nuclei and the shared pair. Thus, smaller atoms tend to form stronger bonds, as long as other factors, such as bond order, are kept the same. Smaller atoms form shorter, stronger bonds.

  • The size of the atoms participating in a bond has an effect on the strength, stability and amount of energy needed to break a bond.
  • Generally, smaller atoms tend to form stronger bonds, as long as other factors like bond order are controlled for.
    • This is due to the reduced distance between their positive nuclei and the negative shared electrons.
    • This smaller distance creates a shorter bond and a stronger electrostatic attraction between the nuclei and the shared pair of electrons between the atoms.
Cause: this is Coulomb’s law. The closer the shared electrons are to the positive nuclei, the stronger the attraction between them.

Patterns, Exceptions and Interactions

Bond order, atom size and polarity all affect bond strength at the same time, and each pattern works best when the other factors are kept the same. When the factors compete, the patterns can point in different directions.

Fluorine is a good example. Fluorine atoms are smaller than chlorine atoms, so the F–F bond would be expected to be stronger than the Cl–Cl bond. However, the F–F bond (159 kJ/mol) is weaker than the Cl–Cl bond (242 kJ/mol). The fluorine atoms are so small that their lone pairs are crowded close together, and they repel one another, weakening the bond.

Polarity can also surprise us. H–Cl is very polar compared with H–H, yet it is slightly weaker (431 compared with 436 kJ/mol). When the factors compete like this, the actual bond energy measured in the lab is the final answer. The patterns are tools for making predictions, not laws that every bond must obey.

  • Bond order, atom size and polarity all affect bond strength at the same time. Each pattern works best when the other factors are kept the same.
  • There are exceptions to these patterns, and the patterns can work against each other:
    • Size: fluorine atoms are smaller than chlorine atoms, but the F–F bond (159 kJ/mol) is weaker than the Cl–Cl bond (242 kJ/mol).
    • Polarity: H–Cl is very polar compared with H–H, yet it is slightly weaker than H–H (431 vs. 436).
  • When the factors compete, the actual bond energy (measured in the lab) is the final answer.
