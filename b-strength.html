What Affects Bond Strength

Some bonds take three times as much energy to break as others. The number of shared pairs, the size of the atoms and the polarity of the bond all play a part.

Part1

Bond Order

More shared pairs, stronger bond.

Bond Strength and Bond Order

Some bonds break easily while others survive high temperatures. High in the atmosphere, ozone absorbs ultraviolet light from the sun, and that energy breaks one of its bonds. How much energy a bond can absorb before breaking depends on several factors.

  • As the number of bonds between 2 atoms increases, the amount of energy required to break those bonds increases.
    • The strength of a bond increases with the number of electron pairs shared between 2 atoms.
  • Bond Strength: Triple Bond > Double Bond > Single Bond
  • Bond Order – the number of shared pairs of electrons between two atoms (single = 1, double = 2, triple = 3).

See Covalent Bonding for bond lengths and energies of single, double and triple bonds.

Polarity and Bond Strength

  • Polar bonds are generally stronger than the average of the strengths of the nonpolar bonds that the same elements form when bonded to themselves.
    • In a polar bond, the partially positive (δ+) and partially negative (δ−) ends attract each other. This adds to the attraction of both nuclei for the shared electrons.
    • The more polar the bond, the larger this extra strength.

Part2

Atom Size and Bond Strength

Smaller atoms, shorter and stronger bonds.

Atom Size and Bond Strength

  • The size of the atoms participating in a bond has an effect on the strength, stability and amount of energy needed to break a bond.
  • Generally, smaller atoms tend to form stronger bonds, as long as other factors like bond order are controlled for.
    • This is due to the reduced distance between their positive nuclei and the negative shared electrons.
    • This smaller distance creates a shorter bond and a stronger electrostatic attraction between the nuclei and the shared pair of electrons between the atoms.
Cause: this is Coulomb’s law. The closer the shared electrons are to the positive nuclei, the stronger the attraction between them.

Patterns, Exceptions and Interactions

  • Bond order, atom size and polarity all affect bond strength at the same time. Each pattern works best when the other factors are kept the same.
  • There are exceptions to these patterns, and the patterns can work against each other:
    • Size: fluorine atoms are smaller than chlorine atoms, but the F–F bond (159 kJ/mol) is weaker than the Cl–Cl bond (242 kJ/mol).
    • Polarity: H–Cl is very polar compared with H–H, yet it is slightly weaker than H–H (431 vs. 436).
  • When the factors compete, the actual bond energy (measured in the lab) is the final answer.
