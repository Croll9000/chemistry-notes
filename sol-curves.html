Solubility Curves

Solubility curves show how much solute dissolves in 100 g of water at each temperature, whether a solution is saturated, and how much precipitate forms when a solution cools.

Solubility Curves

Reading Solubility Curves

Potassium nitrate, which is used in fertilizers, is often purified by a method that depends on how its solubility changes with temperature. Impure potassium nitrate is dissolved in a small amount of hot water, where it is very soluble, and the solution is then cooled. As the water cools, it can no longer hold all of the potassium nitrate, and pure crystals form, while the small amounts of impurities stay dissolved. To know how much hot water to use and how much product to expect, the manufacturer needs to know the solubility of potassium nitrate at every temperature.

That information is shown on a solubility curve, a graph that shows the maximum mass of a solute that dissolves in 100 g of water at different temperatures. Most solid solutes have curves that rise, because they are more soluble at higher temperatures. Gases (NH3, HCl and SO2) have curves that fall, because they are less soluble at higher temperatures. The steeper a curve, the more strongly the solubility of that substance depends on temperature: the curve for potassium nitrate rises steeply, while the curve for sodium chloride is nearly flat, which is why cooling works well for purifying potassium nitrate but would recover very little sodium chloride.

Since every point on a solubility curve represents the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve, every point on the curve represents a saturated solution. The curve therefore divides the graph into two regions. A point on the curve is saturated. A point below the curve is unsaturated, because it holds less than the maximum, so more solute can dissolve. A point above the curve holds more solute than can dissolve, so it is saturated with the extra solute settled on the bottom, or supersaturated if the solution was cooled carefully. To read the solubility of a substance:

  1. Find the temperature on the x-axis.
  2. Move up to the curve of the substance.
  3. Move across to the y-axis and read the mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of water.

To decide whether a solution is saturated:

  1. Find the temperature on the x-axis and the mass of solute on the y-axis.
  2. Plot the point where they meet.
  3. Compare the point with the curve: on the curve is saturated, below is unsaturated, and above is saturated with extra solute on the bottom.

The curves are drawn for 100 g of water. Solubility is directly proportional to the mass of the solvent, so for a different mass of water, the mass of solute is scaled in the same proportion. Say, for instance, that 40 g of a solute dissolves in 100 g of water at some temperature. Then 50 g of water, half as much, dissolves 20 g, and 200 g of water dissolves 80 g.

  • A point on the graph shows a mass of solute in 100 g of water at a temperature:
    • On the curve – saturated.
    • Below the curve – unsaturated. More solute can dissolve.
    • Above the curve – saturated, with the extra solute settled on the bottom (or supersaturated, if the solution was cooled carefully).

Changing the Temperature

Precipitate from Cooling

Rock candy is made by dissolving as much sugar as possible in boiling water and then letting the solution cool with a string hanging in it. As the solution cools, the water can no longer hold all of the sugar, and the extra sugar comes out of solution as large crystals that grow on the string over several days. The same thing happens, more quickly, whenever a saturated solution of a solid is cooled: its solubility drops, and the solute that the water can no longer hold forms a precipitate.

Since the solution was saturated at the start, it held exactly the amount given by the curve at the starting temperature. After cooling, it can hold only the amount given by the curve at the ending temperature, and the difference between the two is the mass that comes out of solution. Consider a saturated solution of potassium nitrate in 100 g of water at 60 °C, which holds about 105 g of solute. At 20 °C, the same water holds only about 30 g, so about 75 g of potassium nitrate crystallizes as the solution cools. To find the mass of precipitate when a saturated solution cools:

  1. Find the solubility at the starting temperature.
  2. Find the solubility at the ending temperature.
  3. Subtract the ending solubility from the starting solubility.
  4. Write the final answer with units.

Heating a solution of a solid has the opposite effect. Its solubility rises, so a saturated solution becomes unsaturated and can dissolve more solute. If the water is not 100 g, the mass of precipitate is scaled in the same proportion as the mass of water.

  • Steps to finding the mass of precipitate when a saturated solution cools:
    1. Find the solubility at the starting temperature.
    2. Find the solubility at the ending temperature.
    3. Subtract the ending solubility from the starting solubility.
    4. Write the final answer with units.
  • Heating a solution of a solid the other way raises its solubility: the solution becomes unsaturated and can dissolve more solute.
