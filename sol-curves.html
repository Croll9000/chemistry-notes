Solubility Curves

Solubility curves show how much solute dissolves in 100 g of water at each temperature, whether a solution is saturated, and how much precipitate forms when a solution cools.

Solubility Curves

Potassium nitrate, used in fertilizers and in fireworks, dissolves to about 33 g in 100 g of water at room temperature, but to more than 100 g in water near 60 °C. Manufacturers purify it by dissolving it in hot water and cooling the solution, so pure crystals form while the impurities stay dissolved. The amounts involved are read from a solubility curve. In this section we will discuss how to read solubility curves.

Reading Solubility Curves

A solubility curve shows the mass of a solute that dissolves in 100 g of water at each temperature. Every point on the curve is a saturated solution, so the curve separates the conditions where solute can still dissolve from the conditions where it cannot.

  • Solubility Curve – a graph that shows the maximum mass of a solute that dissolves in 100 g of water at different temperatures.
  • Most solid solutes have curves that rise: they are more soluble at higher temperatures. Gases (NH3, HCl and SO2) have curves that fall: they are less soluble at higher temperatures.
  • A point on the graph shows a mass of solute in 100 g of water at a temperature:
    • On the curve – saturated.
    • Below the curve – unsaturated. More solute can dissolve.
    • Above the curve – saturated, with the extra solute settled on the bottom (or supersaturated, if the solution was cooled carefully).
  • Steps to reading a solubility curve:
    1. Find the temperature on the x-axis.
    2. Move up to the curve of the substance.
    3. Move across to the y-axis and read the mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of water.
  • Steps to deciding whether a solution is saturated:
    1. Find the temperature on the x-axis and the mass of solute on the y-axis.
    2. Plot the point where they meet.
    3. Compare the point with the curve: on the curve is saturated; below is unsaturated; above is saturated with extra solute on the bottom.
  • The curves are for 100 g of water. For a different mass of water, scale the mass of solute in the same proportion: 50 g of water dissolves half as much.

Changing the Temperature

Making rock candy works because cooling a saturated sugar solution leaves the water holding more sugar than it can keep dissolved. The extra sugar has to come out of solution. A solubility curve tells exactly how much comes out for any change in temperature. In this section we will discuss how to find the mass of precipitate that forms when a solution is cooled.

Precipitate from Cooling

When a saturated solution of a solid cools, its solubility drops, so the solute that the water can no longer hold comes out of solution as a precipitate.

  • Steps to finding the mass of precipitate when a saturated solution cools:
    1. Find the solubility at the starting temperature.
    2. Find the solubility at the ending temperature.
    3. Subtract the ending solubility from the starting solubility.
    4. Write the final answer with units.
  • Heating a solution of a solid the other way raises its solubility: the solution becomes unsaturated and can dissolve more solute.
