Linearizing Data

Curved graphs are hard to read with certainty, because many different curves look alike. Changing what is plotted on one axis can turn a curve into a straight line, and a straight line can be tested and its slope measured.

Linearizing Data

Why Linearize Data?

In 1662, Robert Boyle trapped air in the closed end of a J-shaped glass tube and squeezed it by pouring mercury into the open end. What he found was that the more mercury he added, the smaller the volume of the trapped air became. When his measurements of pressure and volume are plotted, the points fall on a curve that drops steeply and then levels off. Boyle suspected that the pressure and volume were inversely proportional, but a curve on its own cannot show this. An inverse curve, an inverse square curve and many other curves all drop and level off in much the same way, and it is nearly impossible to tell them apart by eye.

A straight line, on the other hand, is easy to recognize, and it either passes through the origin or it does not. This is the idea behind linearization. If we change the data before plotting so that the graph becomes a straight line through the origin, then our guess about the relationship is confirmed. If the graph is still curved, the guess was wrong, and a different change must be tried.

Linearization – changing the data (for example, plotting 1x instead of x) so that the graph becomes a straight line.

A straight line also gives us something a curve cannot: a slope that can be measured. For every relationship of the form y = k × (something), the slope of the linearized graph is the constant k. Additionally, a data point that does not fit the pattern stands out far more clearly from a straight line than from a curve, and values between or beyond the measured data can be read with more confidence. The steps to linearizing data are:

  • Linearization – changing the data (for example, plotting 1x instead of x) so that the graph becomes a straight line.
  • Many different curves look alike, so a curved graph alone cannot prove which relationship the data follow.
  • Linearizing is done for several reasons:
    • A straight line confirms the relationship: if the new graph is a straight line through the origin, the relationship we guessed is correct.
    • The slope of the straight line is the constant in the relationship, so it can be measured.
    • Points that do not fit, and values between or beyond the data, are easier to judge along a straight line than along a curve.
  • Steps to linearizing data:
    1. Graph y vs. x and look at the shape of the curve.
    2. Guess the relationship from the shape (inverse, quadratic or inverse square).
    3. Calculate the new x-values (1/x, x2 or 1/x2) for every data point.
    4. Graph y vs. the new x-values.
    5. Check for a straight line through the origin. If it is straight, the guess was correct, and the slope is the constant k.

Linearizing Inverse Relationships

When y is inversely proportional to x, the relationship is y = kx, which can also be written as y = k × 1x. Written this way, it has the same form as a direct proportion, y = kx, with 1x in place of x. It follows that a graph of y vs. 1x is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the constant k, which is the same as the product x × y. Thus, in order to test an inverse relationship, we calculate 1/x for every data point and plot y against those values instead.

When Boyle’s own measurements are replotted this way, as pressure vs. 1/volume, the points fall along a straight line through the origin. This confirmed what he had suspected: for a fixed amount of air at constant temperature, the pressure and volume are inversely proportional. This relationship is known today as Boyle’s law, and you will use it again in the gas laws unit.

  • If y is inversely proportional to x (y = kx), plot y vs. 1x.
  • The graph of y vs. 1x is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the constant k (k = x × y).
Example
For air in a sealed syringe, the graph of pressure vs. volume is a curve. The graph of pressure vs. 1/volume is a straight line that passes through the origin with a slope of 6000 kPa·mL, the constant value of P × V.

Linearizing Quadratic Relationships

When y depends on the square of x, the relationship is y = kx2. A graph of y vs. x is a curve that grows steeper and steeper, and it could easily be mistaken for some other upward-curving relationship. Using the same reasoning as before, y = k × (x2) has the form of a direct proportion with x2 in place of x. Therefore a graph of y vs. x2 is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the constant k.

Consider a reaction whose rate is measured at three concentrations of a reactant. At 0.10 mol/L the rate is 0.002 mol/L·s, at 0.20 mol/L it is 0.008 mol/L·s, and at 0.30 mol/L it is 0.018 mol/L·s. Doubling the concentration multiplied the rate by four, which hints at a quadratic relationship. Squaring the concentrations gives 0.010, 0.040 and 0.090, and a graph of the rate against these values is a straight line through the origin. What we see is that the rate of this reaction is proportional to the square of the concentration.

  • If y is proportional to the square of x (y = kx2), plot y vs. x2.
  • The graph of y vs. x2 is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the constant k (k = yx2).
Example
A 2.0 kg cart has 1.0 J of kinetic energy at 1.0 m/s, 4.0 J at 2.0 m/s and 9.0 J at 3.0 m/s. A graph of kinetic energy vs. speed is a curve, but a graph of kinetic energy vs. speed2 is a straight line through the origin with a slope of 1.0 J·s2/m2.

Linearizing Inverse Square Relationships

When y depends on 1 divided by the square of x, the relationship is y = kx2. This is the relationship in Coulomb’s law, which describes how the attraction between two charged particles weakens as they move apart. It is part of the reason why the outermost electrons of a large atom, which are far from the nucleus, are held less tightly than those of a small atom. The graph of an inverse square relationship looks very much like an inverse curve, only steeper, so the two are the easiest pair of relationships to confuse.

Linearizing is how we tell them apart. A graph of y vs. 1x2 is a straight line through the origin, with a slope equal to the constant k, while a graph of y vs. 1x would still be curved.

The same strategy works for every one of these relationships. We rewrite the relationship so that it looks like y = k × (something), plot y against that something, and check for a straight line through the origin. The table below lists what to plot for each relationship you have studied.

  • If y is proportional to 1 divided by the square of x (y = kx2), plot y vs. 1x2.
  • The graph of y vs. 1x2 is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the constant k (k = y × x2).
  • A graph of y vs. 1x would still be curved, which is how an inverse square relationship is told apart from an inverse one.
