Hess’s Law

The enthalpy of a reaction depends only on where the reaction starts and where it ends. This allows chemists to calculate enthalpies that are difficult or impossible to measure directly.

Hess’s Law

Two hikers can reach the same mountain summit by different trails: one climbs a steep, direct path and the other follows a long, winding road. However different their routes, both gain exactly the same height. Enthalpy behaves the same way. Some reactions, such as forming ethanol directly from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, cannot be carried out cleanly in a calorimeter, but their enthalpy can still be found by adding up reactions that can be measured.

  • Enthalpy is a state function, meaning that the only things that determine its value are its starting and ending states.
    • For a chemical reaction, the steps by which reactants turn into products have no bearing on the heat released or absorbed by the process.
    • The only things that matter are the starting and ending enthalpies of the particles.
  • Hess’s Law – a chemical reaction can be expressed as the sum of other reactions, in which case the enthalpy of the overall reaction is the sum of the enthalpies of the component reactions.
  • To use Hess’s law:
    • Arrange the given reactions so that, when added together, they produce the target reaction.
    • Reverse a reaction if needed (switch the sign of ΔH), and multiply it if needed (multiply ΔH by the same number).
    • Substances that appear on both sides cancel. Add the ΔH values of the adjusted reactions.
