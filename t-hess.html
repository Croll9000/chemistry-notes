Hess’s Law

Enthalpy depends only on where a reaction starts and where it ends. Adding reactions together adds their ΔH values, which lets chemists find enthalpies they cannot measure directly.

Hess’s Law

Hydrazine, N₂H₄, a rocket fuel used to steer spacecraft, cannot be made by simply combining nitrogen and hydrogen, so the heat of that reaction cannot be measured directly. Combining the measured heats of other reactions gives ΔH = +50.6 kJ for each mole of liquid hydrazine formed. In this section, we will discuss Hess’s law and use it to calculate enthalpies that cannot be measured directly.

Hess’s Law

Two hikers can reach the same mountain summit by different trails: one climbs a steep, direct path and the other follows a long, winding road. However different their routes, both gain exactly the same height. Enthalpy behaves the same way. Some reactions, such as forming ethanol directly from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, cannot be carried out cleanly in a calorimeter, but their enthalpy can still be found by adding up reactions that can be measured.

  • Enthalpy is a state function, meaning that the only things that determine its value are its starting and ending states.
    • For a chemical reaction, the steps by which reactants turn into products have no bearing on the heat released or absorbed by the process.
    • The only things that matter are the starting and ending enthalpies of the particles.
  • Hess’s Law – a chemical reaction can be expressed as the sum of other reactions, in which case the enthalpy of the overall reaction is the sum of the enthalpies of the component reactions.
  • Steps to solving Hess’s law problems:
    1. Identify the target equation.
    2. Find species unique to the target equation in the given equations.
    3. Manipulate the given equations by multiplying or dividing them by a factor, or by reversing them, in order to place the unique species in their appropriate spots based on the target equation. Apply all changes to the enthalpies as well.
    4. Sum the equations together and cancel out species present on both sides of the reaction.
    5. Sum the enthalpies together.
