Atoms: The Building Blocks

All matter is made of atoms.

Atoms

If a piece of aluminum foil were cut in half again and again, it would eventually become too small to cut with scissors. Even then, it would still be aluminum. The question of how far matter can be divided has been asked for thousands of years, and the answer lies in the atom.

  • All matter is composed of tiny particles known as “atoms”.
    • Atoms – the smallest fundamental building blocks of matter
    • Atoms are too small to be seen with the naked eye or even ordinary light microscopes
    • Not all atoms are identical; many have different properties
Everything around you that you can see, touch, or hold in your hands is made of small particles known as atoms. These atoms come in different types with different properties.

Elements and the Periodic Table

Atoms come in different types. Each type is an element, and the periodic table lists them all.

Atoms and Elements

  • All matter is made up of atoms
    • Matter – anything that has mass and takes up volume (space)
    • Atom – smallest fundamental building block of matter
  • Atoms come in different types called elements
    • Element – type of atom that contains a unique number of protons

Protons are tiny particles in the center of every atom (covered in Unit 2). The number of protons determines which element an atom is.

Atoms come in different varieties or “types”. We call these types “elements”. The difference between atoms of different elements is the number of protons that they contain.
It is common to misuse the terms atom and element. Atoms are single units of matter. Elements are types of atoms.

Periodic Table of Elements

Scientists have identified 118 elements. Roughly 90 of them occur naturally on Earth, while the rest have been produced in laboratories. To keep track of so many elements and their characteristics, chemists organize them into a single chart.

  • Periodic Table of Elements – chart which lays out all of the known chemical elements.
    • Elements are placed in order of their atomic number (number of protons)
    • The table gets its unique shape because elements with similar properties are grouped together in the same column (group).
  • Group – a vertical column of the periodic table (numbered 1–18).
    • Elements in the same group have similar properties.
  • Period – a horizontal row of the periodic table (numbered 1–7).
  • The periodic table is divided into three regions.
    • Metals – most elements; on the left side.
    • Nonmetals – on the right side.
    • Metalloids – elements that touch the staircase line (except aluminum); have properties of both.
  • Synthetic Elements – elements that do not occur naturally and are produced by humans by combining smaller atoms in particle accelerators.
    • Most elements with atomic numbers greater than 92 (uranium) are synthetic.
    • Ex: oganesson (118) was made by firing calcium atoms at californium atoms.
    • Synthetic elements are radioactive, and many exist for only a fraction of a second.

Decoding Elements

Some element symbols, such as H for hydrogen and O for oxygen, are easy to recognize. Others, like Fe for iron and Au for gold, seem to have no connection to their names. These symbols come from the elements’ Latin names, ferrum and aurum, which were in use long before the modern names.

  • Every chemical element has a unique name (hydrogen, oxygen, etc.)
  • Each element is also given a unique 1-2 letter symbol.
    • The first letter of each element’s symbol is capitalized
    • The second letter (if there is one) will always be lower case
Note that the letters of the symbol don’t always match the letters of the element name. Some symbols are derived from Latin names: sodium is Na (natrium), potassium is K (kalium), iron is Fe (ferrum), copper is Cu (cuprum), silver is Ag (argentum), gold is Au (aurum) and lead is Pb (plumbum).
Capital letters matter: Co is the element cobalt, but CO is carbon monoxide, a compound of carbon and oxygen.
