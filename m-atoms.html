Atoms: The Building Blocks

Atoms

Around 400 BCE, the Greek philosopher Democritus asked a simple question. What would happen if you cut a piece of matter in half, then cut one of the halves in half, and kept going? He argued that you could not keep cutting forever. Eventually you would reach a piece so small that it could not be cut again. He called these pieces atomos, meaning “uncuttable.”

Democritus had no way to test his idea, and for more than two thousand years it remained a guess. Today we know he was largely right. All matter is composed of tiny particles, which we call atoms. A tree, the air around it and the person looking at it are all built from atoms, just arranged in different ways.

Atom – the smallest fundamental building block of matter

Atoms are far too small to be seen with the naked eye or even with an ordinary light microscope. A single human hair is several hundred thousand carbon atoms wide. Even so, scientists can now detect individual atoms. In 1989, researchers at IBM used the needle-sharp tip of a scanning tunneling microscope to push 35 single xenon atoms into place, one at a time, on a cold nickel surface, spelling out the company’s initials.

However, not all atoms are identical. Some combine with almost anything they touch, while others hardly react at all. These differences between types of atoms are what make the enormous variety of matter around us possible.

  • All matter is composed of tiny particles known as “atoms”.
    • Atom – the smallest fundamental building block of matter
    • Atoms are too small to be seen with the naked eye or even ordinary light microscopes
    • Not all atoms are identical; many have different properties
Everything around you that you can see, touch, or hold in your hands is made of small particles known as atoms. These atoms come in different types with different properties.

Elements and the Periodic Table

Atoms and Elements

Copper wire, a copper pot and a copper roof look different and serve different purposes. Yet every one of them is made of the same kind of atom. Cut a piece of copper as small as you like, and every atom in it is still a copper atom with the same properties as every other copper atom. A gold ring is different from a copper wire, and the reason is not that its atoms are arranged differently. Its atoms are a different type altogether.

What makes one type of atom different from another? Every atom has a tiny center containing particles called protons, and the number of protons decides the type. Every atom with 29 protons is a copper atom, while every atom with 79 protons is a gold atom. No two types share the same number of protons, so that number works like an ID number. Chemists refer to the different types of atoms as elements.

Element – type of atom that contains a unique number of protons

About 118 elements are known, and only about 90 of them occur naturally on Earth. Yet these few types of atoms, combined in different ways, make up every substance in the world. This is very similar to the way the 26 letters of the alphabet can be combined to spell every word in the English language.

  • All matter is made up of atoms
    • Matter – anything that has mass and takes up volume (space)
    • Atom – the smallest fundamental building block of matter
  • Atoms come in different types called elements
    • Element – type of atom that contains a unique number of protons

Protons are tiny particles in the center of every atom. The number of protons determines which element an atom is.

It is common to misuse the terms atom and element. Atoms are single units of matter. Elements are types of atoms.

Periodic Table of Elements

With 118 elements, chemists need a way to organize them, and the periodic table of elements is that organization. In the modern table, the elements are placed in order of their atomic number, the number of protons in an atom. Hydrogen, with 1 proton, comes first. Helium, with 2, comes next, and so on through the whole table.

Periodic Table of Elements – chart which lays out all of the known chemical elements.

If the elements were simply listed in one long line, the table would be a single row. Instead, a new row is started at specific points so that elements with similar properties end up stacked in the same column. This is what gives the table its unusual shape. Each vertical column is referred to as a group, and elements in the same group have similar properties. Each horizontal row is known as a period.

Group – a vertical column of the periodic table (numbered 1–18).

Period – a horizontal row of the periodic table (numbered 1–7).

The periodic table is also divided into three regions. A zig-zag “staircase” line runs down the right side of the table. Most elements, including iron, copper and gold, sit to the left of the staircase, and these are the metals. Elements to the right of the staircase, including oxygen, nitrogen and carbon, are the nonmetals. The elements that touch the staircase, other than aluminum, are called metalloids, and they have some properties of metals and some of nonmetals.

Metals – most elements; on the left side.

Nonmetals – on the right side.

Metalloids – elements that touch the staircase on the periodic table (except aluminum).

Some elements on the table do not occur naturally at all. These synthetic elements are made by smashing smaller atoms together in particle accelerators or nuclear reactors, and most elements beyond uranium (92) are synthetic. Many exist for only a fraction of a second. A few, however, have important uses. Plutonium-238, a form of element 94, gives off a steady supply of heat as it breaks down. Since 1977, that heat has been turned into the electricity that powers the two Voyager spacecraft, which are now more than 20 billion kilometers from the Sun, far too distant for solar panels to work.

  • Periodic Table of Elements – chart which lays out all of the known chemical elements.
    • Elements are placed in order of their atomic number (number of protons)
    • The table gets its unique shape because elements with similar properties are grouped together in the same column (group).
  • Group – a vertical column of the periodic table (numbered 1–18).
    • Elements in the same group have similar properties.
  • Period – a horizontal row of the periodic table (numbered 1–7).
  • The periodic table is divided into three regions.
    • Metals – most elements; on the left side.
    • Nonmetals – on the right side.
    • Metalloids – elements that touch the staircase on the periodic table (except aluminum).
  • Synthetic Elements – elements that do not occur naturally and are produced by humans by combining smaller atoms in particle accelerators.
    • Most elements with atomic numbers greater than 92 (uranium) are synthetic.

Decoding Elements

Writing out element names in every formula would take far too long, so every element is given a short symbol that chemists around the world recognize, no matter what language they speak. Each element has a unique name, such as hydrogen or oxygen, and a unique 1–2 letter symbol.

The rule for writing symbols is strict. The first letter of each element’s symbol is capitalized. The second letter, if there is one, is always lowercase. This is not just a matter of style. Co is the symbol for cobalt, a metal. CO is the formula for carbon monoxide, a poisonous gas made of carbon and oxygen. A single capital letter changes the meaning completely.

Most symbols come from the first one or two letters of the English name, as in C for carbon and He for helium. However, some come from older Latin names instead, so the letters do not always match. Sodium is Na, from natrium. Iron is Fe, from ferrum, and gold is Au, from aurum.

Lead’s symbol, Pb, comes from the Latin plumbum. The Romans made their water pipes from lead, and the same word gave us “plumber” and “plumbing.” We now know that lead dissolves slowly into water that runs through it and is toxic to the brain and nerves, so lead pipes are being dug up and replaced in cities across the country.

  • Every chemical element has a unique name (hydrogen, oxygen, etc.)
  • Each element is also given a unique 1-2 letter symbol.
    • The first letter of each element’s symbol is capitalized
    • The second letter (if there is one) will always be lower case
Note that the letters of the symbol don’t always match the letters of the element name. Some symbols are derived from Latin names: sodium is Na (natrium), potassium is K (kalium), iron is Fe (ferrum), copper is Cu (cuprum), silver is Ag (argentum), gold is Au (aurum) and lead is Pb (plumbum).
Capital letters matter: Co is the element cobalt, but CO is carbon monoxide, a compound of carbon and oxygen.
