Mole Ratios

Mole Ratios

Much of the world’s food is grown with fertilizer made from ammonia, NH3, and factories produce well over a hundred million tons of it every year by combining nitrogen from the air with hydrogen:

N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3

To avoid wasting either gas, engineers must feed them into the reactor in the right proportions, and the balanced equation tells them exactly what those proportions are, the same way a recipe tells a baker how many eggs to use for each cup of flour. Since the coefficients in a balanced chemical equation represent the moles of the substances taking part in the reaction, the equation says that every 1 mole of nitrogen requires 3 moles of hydrogen and produces 2 moles of ammonia.

The ratio between the moles of any two substances in a balanced chemical equation is referred to as a mole ratio. The mole ratio of N2 to H2 is 1 : 3, while the mole ratio of N2 to NH3 is 1 : 2. A mole ratio can be written between any two substances in the equation, reactants or products, and a mole ratio holds for any amount of the reactants: 1 mole of N2 needs 3 moles of H2, 10 moles need 30 moles, and 0.5 mole needs 1.5 moles. A mole ratio comes only from a balanced equation, so the equation must always be checked before a ratio is written.

  • The coefficients in a balanced chemical equation represent the moles of substances taking part in the reaction.
    • The ratio between the moles in the equation can be used as a conversion factor to determine the amount of a substance that will be produced or consumed in the reaction.
    • Mole Ratio – ratio between the moles of any two substances in a balanced chemical equation.
Example
N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3
The mole ratio of N2 to H2 is 1 : 3.
The mole ratio of N2 to NH3 is 1 : 2.
The mole ratio of H2 to NH3 is 3 : 2.

Mole Ratio Conversions

Mole Ratio Conversions

An ammonia plant that needs to produce 1,000 moles of ammonia can calculate exactly how much nitrogen and hydrogen it must supply before the reaction ever starts. Since a mole ratio is a ratio of equal amounts, two quantities that always go together in the reaction, it can be treated as a conversion factor, just like an equivalence. This lets us predict the amount of any substance consumed or produced in a reaction from the amount of any other substance in the same reaction.

The setup follows the same logic as every other conversion factor. The substance you are given goes on the bottom of the mole ratio, and the substance you want goes on top, so the given substance cancels. For the ammonia plant, the setup is 1,000 mol NH3 × 3 mol H22 mol NH3 = 1,500 mol H2. The labels in the conversion factor must include the formula of each substance, not just “mol,” since a mole of ammonia and a mole of hydrogen are not interchangeable. To use a mole ratio:

  1. Write down the given quantity and the desired unit.
  2. Set up the mole ratio from the coefficients of the balanced equation, with the given substance on the bottom and the desired substance on top.
  3. Multiply and divide, then write the final answer with units.
