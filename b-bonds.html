Part1

Valence Electrons and Bonding

Only the outermost electrons take part in bonding, so Lewis dot diagrams show just those.

Lewis Dot Diagrams (Recall)

  • Recall that the valence (outermost) electrons in an atom are the most important when it comes to determining how an atom will react and its chemical properties.
    • When drawing atoms and ions, we often only need to show the valence electrons.
  • Lewis Dot Diagram – the element’s symbol surrounded by dots that represent its valence electrons (used for single atoms and ions).
    • Each dot represents one valence electron.
    • A pair of electrons around a single atom is called a lone pair.
  • Lewis Structure – a diagram of a molecule that shows shared pairs of electrons as lines and unshared pairs (lone pairs) as dots.
    • A line drawn between two atoms represents a covalent bond, or a sharing of electrons.
  • Rules for drawing ions:
    • Surround the symbol and its dots with square brackets [ ].
    • Write the charge in the top right corner, outside the brackets.
    • A cation has no dots left inside its brackets. An anion has 8 dots.
  • The steps for drawing Lewis dot diagrams of atoms and ions are on the Lewis Dot Diagrams page in Unit 2.
Part2

Chemical Bonds

Atoms transfer or share electrons to fill their valence shells.

Chemical Bonds

Nearly every substance around us, from the water we drink to the steel in a building, is held together by chemical bonds. In this unit we look more closely at why atoms form bonds and how the type of bond affects a substance’s properties.

  • In order to achieve a more stable electron configuration, atoms will form chemical bonds.
    • Chemical Bond – strong attraction between atoms or ions.
    • Chemical bonds involve the sharing or transfer of electrons.
  • An ionic bond involves a transfer of electrons from one atom to another, followed by an attraction between the two oppositely charged ions.
  • A covalent bond involves a sharing of electrons between two atoms.

The Octet Rule

Noble gases rarely form compounds because their outermost shells are already full. Most other atoms reach this same stable arrangement by forming bonds with one another.

  • Octet Rule – atoms form chemical bonds in order to achieve 8 valence electrons, or a complete octet.
    • 8 valence electrons is the same stable configuration as a noble gas.
  • Exceptions: hydrogen and helium are stable with only 2 valence electrons.
    • Their only energy level (the first) holds a maximum of 2 electrons, so 2 is a full valence shell.
    • Helium already has 2 valence electrons, so it does not form bonds. Hydrogen reaches 2 by sharing one pair of electrons, giving it the same configuration as helium.
Carbon is stable when it has 8 valence electrons. Note the 8 dots that surround the carbon atom in this molecule of CH4.
Hydrogen is an exception to the octet rule. It is stable with only 2 valence electrons. Note how each hydrogen is next to only 2 dots.
Part3

Bonds and Energy

Forming a bond releases energy. Breaking a bond absorbs energy.

Bonds and Energy

Fuels such as natural gas and gasoline are useful because of the energy changes that occur when bonds break and form. Every reaction involves both, and the balance between them determines whether energy is released or absorbed.

  • The breaking and formation of bonds involves a change in energy for the atoms or molecules involved.
    • When forming bonds, energy is released.
      • Forming bonds generally increases the stability of atoms or molecules.
      • This process is exothermic, meaning energy is released.
    • When breaking bonds, energy is absorbed.
      • Breaking bonds generally makes atoms or molecules less stable.
      • This process is endothermic, meaning energy is absorbed.
  • The energy released when a bond forms is equal to the energy absorbed to break that same bond.
    • Ex: 436 kJ of energy is absorbed to break 1 mole of H–H bonds, and 436 kJ is released when 1 mole of H–H bonds forms.
Cause: the nuclei of bonded atoms attract the shared or transferred electrons. Energy must be added to pull the atoms apart against this attraction, and energy is released when the atoms come together.

Break a bond by pulling the atoms apart, then let them bond again. Compare the energy absorbed and released for different bonds.

Types of Bonds

The types of elements involved decide whether a bond is ionic, covalent or metallic.

Types of Bonds

Table salt, sugar and copper wire are all held together by chemical bonds, yet they behave very differently. Salt is brittle, sugar melts at a fairly low temperature and copper bends and conducts electricity. These differences come from the different ways atoms can bond to one another.

  • Depending on their nature, atoms can form different types of bonds, including ionic, covalent and metallic bonds.
  • Recall that metals have low electronegativities and nonmetals have high electronegativities.
    • A metal and a nonmetal: the nonmetal pulls hard enough to take the metal’s electrons (ionic).
    • Two nonmetals: neither atom gives up its electrons, so they share (covalent).
    • Two metals: both give up their electrons to a shared “sea” (metallic).
  • Noble gases already have a full valence shell, so they almost never bond.
  • Metalloids bond with nonmetals by sharing electrons (covalent). Ex: SiO2, BF3
Mixing metals: two different metals do not form a compound. They form an alloy, a mixture held together by metallic bonding (more on the Metallic Bonding page).
Hydrogen: hydrogen is a nonmetal. It shares electrons with other nonmetals (H2O, HCl) but takes an electron from very active metals (NaH).
