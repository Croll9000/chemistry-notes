Part1

Valence Electrons and Bonding

Lewis Dot Diagrams (Recall)

When two atoms bond, only their outermost electrons meet. The nucleus and the inner electrons stay buried in the middle of each atom and play no direct part. For this reason, chemists usually draw only the valence electrons when they draw bonds.

Recall that a Lewis dot diagram shows an element’s symbol surrounded by one dot for each valence electron.

Lewis Dot Diagram – the element’s symbol surrounded by dots that represent its valence electrons.

When atoms bond, the diagram grows into a Lewis structure. In a Lewis structure, a line drawn between two atoms represents a covalent bond, a pair of shared electrons. A pair of electrons that belongs to a single atom and is not shared is called a lone pair, and it is still drawn as two dots.

Lewis Structure – a diagram of a molecule that shows shared pairs of electrons as lines and unshared pairs (lone pairs) as dots.

Ions are drawn with two extra marks. The symbol and its dots are surrounded with square brackets, and the charge is written at the top right, outside the brackets. A cation has no dots left inside its brackets, while an anion has 8.

  • Recall that the valence (outermost) electrons in an atom are the most important when it comes to determining how an atom will react and its chemical properties.
    • When drawing atoms and ions, we often only need to show the valence electrons.
  • Lewis Dot Diagram – the element’s symbol surrounded by dots that represent its valence electrons.
    • Each dot represents one valence electron.
    • A pair of electrons around a single atom is called a lone pair.
  • Lewis Structure – a diagram of a molecule that shows shared pairs of electrons as lines and unshared pairs (lone pairs) as dots.
    • A line drawn between two atoms represents a covalent bond, or a sharing of electrons.
  • Rules for drawing ions:
    • Surround the symbol and its dots with square brackets [ ].
    • Write the charge in the top right corner, outside the brackets.
    • A cation has no dots left inside its brackets. An anion has 8 dots.
Part2

Chemical Bonds

Chemical Bonds

Why do atoms bond at all?

Of the 118 known elements, only the noble gases are found in nature as single, unbonded atoms. Every other element, from the oxygen in the air to the iron in the ground, is found bonded to other atoms. Even pure oxygen gas is made of O2 molecules rather than separate oxygen atoms. Something about atoms makes them stick together, and something about the noble gases makes them the exception.

We can work out what that something is from an idea we already have. Recall that opposite charges attract, and that the attraction grows stronger as the charges get closer. Every atom is a positive nucleus surrounded by negative electrons. When two atoms come close together, the nucleus of each atom attracts the electrons of the other. If one atom attracts electrons much more strongly, it can pull electrons away from the other atom completely. If the two atoms attract electrons about equally, the electrons end up between them, attracted to both nuclei at once. Either way, the atoms end up held together by the attraction between positive and negative charges.

This strong attraction is a chemical bond. Since it is the movement of electrons that creates it, chemical bonds involve the sharing or transfer of electrons.

Chemical Bond – strong attraction that holds atoms or ions together.

The two possibilities give two kinds of bonds. When one atom takes electrons from the other, the two atoms become oppositely charged ions, and the attraction between them is an ionic bond. When neither atom can take the electrons, they share them, and the attraction of both nuclei for the shared electrons is a covalent bond. When potassium meets bromine, bromine pulls so much harder that it takes potassium’s valence electron completely. When two bromine atoms meet, neither can win, so they share.

Why, then, do the noble gases not bond? Their outer shells are already full. Any electron they gained would have to go into a new shell much farther from the nucleus, where the pull on it would be very weak. Their own electrons sit in a full shell close to the nucleus and are held so tightly that no other atom can pull them away. A noble gas atom has nothing to gain from another atom and nothing it will give up.

  • In order to achieve a more stable electron configuration, atoms will form chemical bonds.
    • Chemical Bond – strong attraction that holds atoms or ions together.
    • Chemical bonds involve the sharing or transfer of electrons.
  • An ionic bond involves a transfer of electrons from one atom to another, followed by an attraction between the two oppositely charged ions.
  • A covalent bond involves a sharing of electrons between two atoms.

The Octet Rule

If bonds form because electrons are attracted to more than one nucleus, how do we know how many bonds an atom will form, or how many electrons it will gain or lose? The answer lies with the noble gases. They are the one family that does not bond, so their arrangement of electrons must already be as stable as an atom can get. It stands to reason that other atoms bond until they reach that same arrangement.

We can check this reasoning with numbers we already have. Removing sodium’s single valence electron takes 496 kJ/mol. Removing the next electron takes 4,562 kJ/mol, more than nine times as much, since that electron sits in the full shell below, much closer to the nucleus and far less shielded. Sodium therefore loses one electron and stops, and it stops exactly when it has the same arrangement as neon. Chlorine shows the same pattern from the other side. It gains one electron easily, but a second electron would have to start a new shell far from the nucleus, so it stops at one, with the same arrangement as argon.

For most atoms, that noble gas arrangement means 8 electrons in the outer shell. This pattern is called the octet rule. 8 valence electrons is the same stable configuration as a noble gas.

Octet Rule – atoms form chemical bonds in order to achieve 8 valence electrons, or a complete octet.

The exceptions follow from the same reasoning. The first energy level can hold only 2 electrons, so the noble gas nearest to hydrogen, helium, has only 2. Thus, hydrogen and helium are stable with only 2 valence electrons. Helium already has 2, so it does not bond. Hydrogen reaches 2 by sharing one pair of electrons.

Now we can predict formulas instead of memorizing them. Carbon has 4 valence electrons and needs 4 more. Each hydrogen has 1 and needs 1 more. If carbon shares one pair with each of four hydrogen atoms, carbon counts 8 and every hydrogen counts 2. Methane, the main gas in natural gas, is therefore CH4, never CH3 or CH5. The octet rule predicts not only whether atoms will bond, but how many bonds each will form.

Note that the octet rule is a model. It summarizes where the energy costs of gaining and losing electrons usually lead, but it is not a law, and some molecules, such as BF3, do not follow it.

  • Octet Rule – atoms form chemical bonds in order to achieve 8 valence electrons, or a complete octet.
    • 8 valence electrons is the same stable configuration as a noble gas.
  • Exceptions: hydrogen and helium are stable with only 2 valence electrons.
    • Their only energy level (the first) holds a maximum of 2 electrons, so 2 is a full valence shell.
    • Helium already has 2 valence electrons, so it does not form bonds. Hydrogen reaches 2 by sharing one pair of electrons, giving it the same configuration as helium.
Carbon is stable when it has 8 valence electrons. Note the 8 dots that surround the carbon atom in this molecule of CH4.
Hydrogen is an exception to the octet rule. It is stable with only 2 valence electrons. Note how each hydrogen is next to only 2 dots.
Part3

Bonds and Energy

Bonds and Energy

We have seen that bonded atoms are held together by a strong attraction between positive and negative charges. As two atoms come together and form a bond, they are pulled toward each other, and they settle into a stable arrangement. Bonded atoms are more stable, and have less energy, than the same atoms apart.

To picture what this means for energy, recall the comparison with two strong magnets that have snapped together. Once they are stuck, they stay stuck on their own. Pulling them apart takes real effort, and the stronger the magnets, the more effort it takes. Chemical bonds behave the same way. To separate two bonded atoms, energy must be added to pull them apart against the attraction holding them together. Thus, when breaking bonds, energy is absorbed, so breaking bonds is endothermic.

Now think about the magnets coming together. Held near each other and released, they jump together with a snap, and the energy comes out as motion and sound. Atoms forming a bond do the same, except the energy is usually released as heat. Thus, when forming bonds, energy is released, so bond formation is exothermic. The comparison holds only for the energy, since the attraction in a bond comes from electric charges rather than magnetism.

Forming a bond and breaking the same bond are exact opposites, so the two energies must match. The energy released when a bond forms is equal to the energy absorbed to break that same bond. If they were not equal, we could form and break the same bond over and over and create energy from nothing.

Every chemical reaction breaks some bonds and forms others, so every reaction both absorbs and releases energy. What matters is which is larger. When your body uses the sugar in food, it breaks the bonds in sugar and oxygen molecules, which absorbs energy, and then forms new bonds in carbon dioxide and water, which releases energy. The new bonds are stronger, so forming them releases more energy than breaking the old ones absorbed. That difference is the energy your body uses.

  • The breaking and formation of bonds involves a change in energy for the atoms or molecules involved.
    • When forming bonds, energy is released.
      • Forming bonds generally increases the stability of atoms or molecules.
      • This process is exothermic, meaning energy is released.
    • When breaking bonds, energy is absorbed.
      • Breaking bonds generally makes atoms or molecules less stable.
      • This process is endothermic, meaning energy is absorbed.
  • The energy released when a bond forms is equal to the energy absorbed to break that same bond.
    Example
    436 kJ of energy is absorbed to break 1 mole of H–H bonds, and 436 kJ is released when 1 mole of H–H bonds forms.
Cause: the nuclei of bonded atoms attract the shared or transferred electrons. Energy must be added to pull the atoms apart against this attraction, and energy is released when the atoms come together.

Types of Bonds

Types of Bonds

We have seen that whether electrons are transferred or shared depends on how hard each atom pulls on them. That pull is an atom’s electronegativity. Recall that metals have low electronegativities, since their few valence electrons sit far from the nucleus and are well shielded. Nonmetals have high electronegativities, since their valence electrons sit closer to a nucleus with a large net charge.

There are only three ways two atoms can be matched, and each leads to a different outcome.

When a metal and a nonmetal meet, one atom pulls weakly and the other pulls strongly. The nonmetal takes the metal’s valence electrons completely, and the two ions that form attract each other. This is an ionic bond.

When two nonmetals meet, both pull strongly. Neither can take electrons from the other, so the electrons stay between the two nuclei, attracted to both. This is a covalent bond.

When two metals meet, both pull weakly. Neither atom can hold on to its valence electrons tightly, and neither can take electrons from the other. The valence electrons therefore drift away from their own atoms and move freely among all of the positive ions, holding them together as a shared “sea.” This is a metallic bond.

The same reasoning covers the remaining cases. Noble gases already have a full valence shell, so they almost never bond. Metalloids have moderate electronegativities, so when they bond with nonmetals, neither atom can take the electrons outright, and they share them, as in SiO2 and BF3.

If this reasoning is right, the three types of bonds should give substances very different properties, and they do. Table salt (ionic) shatters when crushed and melts only at 801 °C. Sugar (covalent) melts into caramel at about 186 °C. Aluminum foil (metallic) bends and crinkles without breaking, and it conducts electricity. Each of these properties can be explained from the type of bond holding the particles together.

  • Depending on their nature, atoms can form different types of bonds, including ionic, covalent and metallic bonds.
  • Recall that metals have low electronegativities and nonmetals have high electronegativities.
    • A metal and a nonmetal: the nonmetal pulls hard enough to take the metal’s electrons (ionic).
    • Two nonmetals: neither atom gives up its electrons, so they share (covalent).
    • Two metals: both give up their electrons to a shared “sea” (metallic).
  • Noble gases already have a full valence shell, so they almost never bond.
  • Metalloids bond with nonmetals by sharing electrons (covalent).
    Example
    SiO2, BF3
Mixing metals: two different metals do not form a compound. They form an alloy, a mixture held together by metallic bonding (more on the Metallic Bonding page).
Hydrogen: hydrogen is a nonmetal. It shares electrons with other nonmetals (H2O, HCl) but takes an electron from very active metals (NaH).
