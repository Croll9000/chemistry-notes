Average Atomic Mass

Why the masses on the periodic table aren’t whole numbers, how to calculate them from isotope data, and how scientists measure isotopes.

Part1

A Weighted Average

Most elements are a mix of isotopes. The mass on the periodic table averages them, counting the common isotopes more.

Atomic Mass

The atomic masses written on the periodic table are weighted averages of the masses of all the naturally occurring isotopes of an element.

Carbon has 3 main isotopes: C‑12, C‑13 and C‑14, which are found in differing amounts on Earth. The weighted average of the masses of these isotopes is 12.011 u.

A weighted average counts each isotope according to how common it is. Carbon‑12 makes up almost 99% of carbon atoms, so carbon’s average atomic mass (12.011 u) is very close to 12.

A Weighted Average You Already Know: Your Grade

Your marking period grade is a weighted average. Each category counts for a different percent of the grade, just like each isotope counts according to its percent abundance.

CategoryYour averageCounts forAverage × weight
Tests8050% (0.50)80 × 0.50 = 40
Labs9030% (0.30)90 × 0.30 = 27
Homework10020% (0.20)100 × 0.20 = 20
Marking period grade40 + 27 + 20 = 87
A plain average of 80, 90 and 100 would be 90. Your real grade is 87, closer to your test average, because tests count the most. In the same way, an element’s average atomic mass is always closest to the mass of its most abundant isotope.

Part2

Calculating Average Atomic Mass

Two ways to do the same calculation: with percents (divide by 100 at the end) or with decimals.

The Percent Method

The average atomic mass of an element is found by adding the product of the mass of each isotope and its percentage abundance and then dividing the sum by 100.

  • Percentage Abundance – relative frequency of a specific isotope of a given element.
Average Atomic Mass=(m1)(a1) + (m2)(a2)100
  • m1 − mass of isotope 1 (u)  ·  a1 − percentage abundance of isotope 1 (%)
  • m2 − mass of isotope 2 (u)  ·  a2 − percentage abundance of isotope 2 (%)
IsotopeMass (u)Percent Abundance (%)
Boron‑1010.01319.78
Boron‑1111.00980.22

Abundances are rounded. More precise values (19.9% and 80.1%) give the same answer.

Average Atomic Mass = (10.013 u)(19.78) + (11.009 u)(80.22)100Average Atomic Mass = 198.06 + 883.14100Average Atomic Mass = 10.81 u

The Decimal Method

The average atomic mass can also be found by adding the product of the mass of each isotope and its percent abundance expressed in the form of a decimal. If abundances are expressed as decimals there is no need to divide by 100.

  • To convert from percent abundances to decimals, divide the percentage by 100.
Average Atomic Mass=(m1)(a1) + (m2)(a2)
  • a1, a2 − abundance of each isotope as a decimal value
IsotopeMass (u)Percent Abundance (%)Abundance (decimal)
Potassium‑3938.96493.120.9312
Potassium‑4140.9626.880.0688

Abundances are rounded. More precise values (93.26% and 6.73%, plus a trace of K‑40) give the same answer.

Average Atomic Mass = (38.964 u)(0.9312) + (40.962 u)(0.0688)Average Atomic Mass = 36.283 + 2.818Average Atomic Mass = 39.10 u
Note that to convert from % to decimal, divide the abundance by 100 or move the decimal point two places left.

Part3

Measuring Isotopes: Mass Spectrometry

Scientists measure isotope masses and abundances with a mass spectrometer. This part goes beyond what the Regents requires.

The Mass Spectrometer

  • A mass spectrometer separates the atoms of a sample by mass, so scientists can measure the mass of each isotope and how common it is.
  1. Vaporize: the sample is turned into a gas so the atoms are separate.
  2. Ionize: a beam of fast electrons knocks an electron off each atom, making positive ions. (Ions can be pushed by electric and magnetic fields; neutral atoms can’t.)
  3. Accelerate: negatively charged plates attract the positive ions and speed them into a narrow beam.
  4. Deflect: a magnet bends the path of the moving ions into a curve.
    • Lighter ions bend more.
    • Heavier ions bend less.
    • Each isotope follows its own path.
  5. Detect: a detector counts the ions that arrive along each path.
    • More ions means a larger signal.
    • The signal shows the isotope’s percent abundance.
  • Mass Spectrum – a graph with one peak for each isotope.
    • The position of a peak is the isotope’s mass.
    • The height of the peak is its percent abundance.
  • The tallest peak is the most common isotope, and the average atomic mass is always closest to that peak.
