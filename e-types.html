Phase Equilibrium

Phase Equilibrium

In a closed water bottle left in a warm car, droplets form on the inside of the cap and run back down into the water. Water keeps evaporating and condensing the whole time, but after a while, the amount of liquid in the bottle stops changing. Equilibrium is not limited to chemical reactions. There are three types of equilibrium: chemical equilibrium, phase equilibrium and solution equilibrium.

Phase equilibrium is the condition in which two phases of matter exist at the same time with no net change in the amount of either one. In the closed bottle, H2O(l) ⇌ H2O(g), and the rate of evaporation equals the rate of condensation. In a glass of ice water at 0 °C, H2O(s) ⇌ H2O(l), and the rate of melting equals the rate of freezing.

For a substance to stay in phase equilibrium, it must be in a closed system with no heat added or removed. Open the bottle, and the vapor escapes, so evaporation wins. Warm the ice water, and melting wins. Phase equilibrium is the same balance that sets the vapor pressure of a liquid.

  • There are three types of equilibrium: chemical equilibrium, phase equilibrium and solution equilibrium.
  • Phase equilibrium – condition in which two phases of matter exist at the same time with no net change in the amount of either one.
    • Example: H2O(l) ⇌ H2O(g) in a closed container. The rate of evaporation equals the rate of condensation.
    • Example: H2O(s) ⇌ H2O(l) at 0 °C. The rate of melting equals the rate of freezing.
  • For a substance to be in phase equilibrium, it must be in a closed system with no heat added or removed.

Solution Equilibrium

Solution Equilibrium

A sealed bottle of soda can sit on a shelf for months with the same amount of carbon dioxide dissolved in it, while carbon dioxide molecules leave the liquid and re-enter it every second. Twist off the cap, and the gas above the liquid escapes, so the balance is lost and the soda slowly goes flat.

Solution equilibrium is the equilibrium in a saturated solution, where the rate of dissolving equals the rate of crystallizing, as in NaCl(s) ⇌ Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq). A saturated solution with undissolved solid is at solution equilibrium, so the concentration of the dissolved solute stays constant, even though particles keep leaving and rejoining the solid.

It follows that adding more solid to a saturated solution does not change the concentration. The extra solid does not dissolve, since dissolving and crystallizing are already balanced. Gases dissolved in a closed bottle are at solution equilibrium too, CO2(g) ⇌ CO2(aq), which is why the soda stays fizzy only while the cap is on.

  • Solution equilibrium – the equilibrium in a saturated solution, where the rate of dissolving equals the rate of crystallizing. Example: NaCl(s) ⇌ Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq).
  • A saturated solution with undissolved solid is at solution equilibrium. The concentration of the dissolved solute stays constant.
  • Adding more solid to a saturated solution does not change the concentration; the extra solid does not dissolve.
  • Gases dissolved in a closed bottle of soda are also at solution equilibrium: CO2(g) ⇌ CO2(aq). Opening the bottle lets the gas escape, so the equilibrium is lost and the soda goes flat.
