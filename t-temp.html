Temperature

What is a thermometer actually measuring?

Every particle in a sample of matter is moving. In a gas, the particles fly in straight lines until they collide; in a liquid, they slide past one another; and in a solid, they vibrate in place. When a sample is heated, its particles move faster, and when it cools, they slow down. Temperature is directly related to the kinetic energy of these particles: particles at a high temperature move fast and have high kinetic energy, and particles at a low temperature move slowly and have low kinetic energy.

The particles in a sample do not all move at the same speed. At any moment, some particles have just been struck and are moving quickly, while others are moving slowly. A single particle’s speed changes with every collision, so it would be meaningless to describe the temperature of one particle. Instead, temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample. It is measured in degrees Celsius (°C) or kelvin (K) with a thermometer.

Because temperature is an average, it does not depend on how much of a substance there is. A teaspoon of boiling water and a large pot of boiling water are both at 100 °C, since their particles have the same average kinetic energy, even though the pot contains far more particles. The pot holds more total energy, which is why it would cause a much worse burn, but its temperature is the same. A liquid-in-glass thermometer works because its liquid expands as its particles move faster and spread apart, and contracts as they slow down. A digital thermometer uses a small electronic part whose electrical resistance changes with temperature. Either way, the thermometer is reporting how fast, on average, the particles around it are moving.

  • Temperature is directly related to the kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
    • Particles at a high temperature move fast and have high kinetic energy.
    • Particles at a low temperature move slowly and have low kinetic energy.
  • Temperature – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample.
    • Unit – degrees Celsius (°C) and kelvin (K)
    • Measuring device – thermometer
    • The faster particles move, the more kinetic energy they have.
    • Particles in a sample do not all move at the same speed. Some are fast and some are slow, so temperature measures their average kinetic energy.

Heat vs. Temperature

Which contains more energy: a cup of coffee at 90 °C or an iceberg at −5 °C? The coffee has the higher temperature, but the iceberg holds vastly more energy, because it contains trillions of times as many particles. The question shows why heat and temperature, which are often used as if they meant the same thing, must be kept separate in chemistry.

Heat is energy, specifically energy transferred from a hotter object to a colder one, and it is measured in joules or kilojoules. Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample, and it is measured in °C or K. Temperature tells us how fast the particles are moving on average; it says nothing about how many particles there are. Heat depends on both. Consider a bathtub of water at 40 °C and a small cup of tea at 90 °C. The tea is hotter, but if each were allowed to cool to room temperature, the bathtub would release far more heat, because every one of its many particles gives up a little energy as it slows down.

The two quantities are connected: heat flows because of a difference in temperature, and adding heat usually raises the temperature of a substance. There is one important exception. During a phase change, such as ice melting, heat is added but the temperature stays the same, because the energy is being used to separate particles rather than to speed them up. This exception is the clearest evidence that heat and temperature are different things, and it is the subject of the heating curves page.

  • Heat and temperature are not the same thing.
    • Heat is energy: energy transferred from a high temperature object to a low temperature object. It is measured in joules (J) and kilojoules (kJ).
    • Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles. It is measured in °C or K.
  • Heat flows because of a difference in temperature. Adding heat usually raises the temperature (but not during a phase change).
  • A large object and a small object can be at the same temperature, but the large object can transfer more heat because it has more particles.

Temperature Scales

Temperature Scales

When a weather report in New York says it is 77 degrees, a report in Paris describing the same weather says 25 degrees, and a physicist would say 298. All three are correct. They are simply using different temperature scales, each with its own starting point and size of degree. In chemistry, we use the Celsius scale for most measurements and the Kelvin scale for calculations.

The Celsius scale was proposed by the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius in 1742. He chose two temperatures that anyone could reproduce in a laboratory: the freezing point and the boiling point of water at normal air pressure. These were set as 0 °C and 100 °C, and the space between them was divided into 100 equal degrees. The scale is convenient, but its zero is arbitrary. Nothing special happens to most substances at 0 °C, and temperatures below it are negative.

The Kelvin scale was designed to start at a zero that means something. Scientists studying gases noticed that as a gas is cooled, its volume shrinks in a perfectly straight line. When they extended this line to lower and lower temperatures, it reached zero volume at −273.15 °C. A gas cannot have less than zero volume, so this temperature must be the lowest one possible. In 1848, William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, proposed a scale that begins there, and −273.15 °C was defined as 0 K. This temperature is called absolute zero: the lowest possible temperature, at which particles have the least possible kinetic energy. Since nothing can be colder, the Kelvin scale has no negative temperatures, and Kelvin temperatures are written without a degree sign.

The two scales are closely related: a change of 1 kelvin is the same size as a change of 1 Celsius degree. The scales simply start at different places, so every Kelvin temperature is exactly 273.15 higher than the Celsius temperature. The Kelvin scale is required in gas law calculations because it is proportional to the kinetic energy of the particles. Doubling a Kelvin temperature doubles the average kinetic energy, but doubling a Celsius temperature does not, as you will see on the next note.

  • There are a number of different scales that we use to measure temperature, including Fahrenheit (°F), Celsius (°C) and Kelvin (K). In chemistry we use the Celsius and Kelvin scales.
  • Celsius Scale – temperature scale based on the freezing point and boiling point of water.
    • The freezing point of water was defined as 0 °C.
    • The boiling point of water was defined as 100 °C.
  • Kelvin Scale – temperature scale that starts at absolute zero (0 K), the lowest possible temperature.
    • Scientists observed that as the temperature of a gas decreased, so did its volume.
      • They extended the graph to find the temperature at which a gas would have no volume: −273.15 °C.
      • This temperature was defined as 0 K.
    • Absolute Zero – the lowest possible temperature (0 K, or −273.15 °C). At absolute zero, particles have the least possible kinetic energy.
    • There are no negative Kelvin temperatures.
    • Kelvin temperatures are written with no degree sign (298 K, not 298 °K).
    • A change of 1 kelvin is the same size as a change of 1 Celsius degree. The scales just start at different places.

Temperature Conversions

Is 20 °C twice as hot as 10 °C? It seems as though it should be, but converting to kelvin shows otherwise: 20 °C is 293.15 K and 10 °C is 283.15 K, so the particles at 20 °C have only about 3.5% more kinetic energy. Ratios of temperatures only make sense on the Kelvin scale, because only the Kelvin scale starts at the true zero of particle motion. This is why every gas law calculation in Unit 8 requires temperatures in kelvin.

Because the two scales have degrees of the same size and differ only in their starting points, converting between them takes a single addition or subtraction:

To go from Celsius to kelvin, add 273.15; to go from kelvin to Celsius, subtract 273.15. A quick check catches most mistakes: a Kelvin temperature is always the larger number, and it can never be negative. If a conversion gives a negative Kelvin temperature, the subtraction was done in the wrong direction. Since a change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K, a temperature change (ΔT) has the same value on both scales and never needs to be converted. The steps for converting temperatures are:

  • Your Reference Table converts between them: K = °C + 273.15
    • To go from kelvin to Celsius, subtract: °C = K − 273.15.
  • Steps to converting temperatures:
    1. Write the formula: K = °C + 273.15
    2. Substitute the temperature you are given.
    3. Add or subtract to solve for the unknown temperature.
    4. Write the final answer with units.

Some Important Temperatures

Some Important Temperatures

The volume of a gas, the speed of a reaction and even the density of water all change with temperature. If one scientist measured the density of a gas on a cold morning and another measured it on a hot afternoon, their results could not be compared. To solve this problem, scientists agree on reference conditions. Standard temperature is a baseline temperature that scientists use for consistency in describing chemical properties and reactions. For gases, standard temperature is 0 °C (273.15 K), and it is paired with a standard pressure of 1 atm (101.3 kPa). Together these are known as STP.

Other important temperatures appear throughout chemistry. Values in thermodynamics, such as the heats of reaction you will use later in this unit, are usually reported at 25 °C (298.15 K), which is close to a comfortable room temperature. Room temperature itself is not an exact standard; it is usually taken to be about 20 to 25 °C (293 to 298 K). Body temperature is about 37 °C (310 K), and water freezes at 0 °C (273.15 K) and boils at 100 °C (373.15 K) at 1 atm. Knowing a few of these values helps you judge whether an answer is reasonable. A calculated room temperature of 2,980 K, for instance, signals a mistake right away.

  • Standard Temperature – baseline temperature that scientists use for consistency in describing chemical properties and reactions. For gases, standard temperature is 0 °C (273.15 K).
