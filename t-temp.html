Temperature

Heat and temperature are often used interchangeably in everyday speech, but they are different quantities. Temperature is a measurement that reflects the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample.

Temperature

Touch a mug of hot tea and you can feel that it is hot, but what does “hot” actually mean for the water inside? Even in a still cup, the water molecules are racing around, crashing into one another billions of times every second. Energy cannot be measured directly, but temperature gives us a window into it: it tells us how fast, on average, those particles are moving.

  • Temperature is directly related to the kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
    • Particles at a high temperature move fast and have high kinetic energy.
    • Particles at a low temperature move slowly and have low kinetic energy.
  • Temperature – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample.
    • Unit – degrees Celsius (°C) and kelvin (K)
    • Measuring device – thermometer
    • The faster particles move, the more kinetic energy they have.
    • Particles in a sample do not all move at the same speed. Some are fast and some are slow, so temperature measures their average kinetic energy.
  • Temperature does not depend on how much of the substance you have.
    Example
    A small cup and a large pot of boiling water are both at 100 °C.
    Their particles have the same average kinetic energy.
  • A thermometer works because the liquid inside it expands as its particles move faster, and contracts as they slow down.

Heat vs. Temperature

A sparkler throws off sparks at over 1000 °C, yet they barely sting when they land on your hand. A bathtub of warm water is far cooler, but it could melt a bucket of ice. The sparks have a high temperature but very little mass, so they carry little energy. The two ideas are connected, but heat and temperature are not the same thing.

  • Heat and temperature are not the same thing.
    • Heat is energy: energy transferred from a high temperature object to a low temperature object. It is measured in joules (J) and kilojoules (kJ).
    • Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles. It is measured in °C or K.
  • Heat flows because of a difference in temperature. Adding heat usually raises the temperature (but not during a phase change).
  • A large object and a small object can be at the same temperature, but the large object can transfer more heat because it has more particles.

Temperature Scales

Temperature can be measured on several different scales. In chemistry we prefer the Celsius and Kelvin scales, which are related by a single equation.

Temperature Scales

A weather report in New York says 77 degrees; the same report in Paris says 25 degrees. Both describe the same warm day on different scales. Each scale picks its own zero point and its own size of degree. The Fahrenheit scale was built around a freezing salt-water mixture, the Celsius scale around water, and the Kelvin scale around the coldest temperature that is possible at all.

  • There are a number of different scales that we use to measure temperature, including Fahrenheit (°F), Celsius (°C) and Kelvin (K). In chemistry we use the Celsius and Kelvin scales.
  • Celsius Scale – temperature scale based on the freezing point and boiling point of water.
    • The freezing point of water was defined as 0 °C.
    • The boiling point of water was defined as 100 °C.
  • Kelvin Scale – temperature scale that starts at absolute zero (0 K), the lowest possible temperature.
    • Scientists observed that as the temperature of a gas decreased, so did its volume.
      • They extended the graph to find the temperature at which a gas would have no volume: −273.15 °C.
      • This temperature was defined as 0 K.
    • Absolute Zero – the lowest possible temperature (0 K, or −273.15 °C). At absolute zero, particles have the least possible kinetic energy.
    • There are no negative Kelvin temperatures.
    • Kelvin temperatures are written with no degree sign (298 K, not 298 °K).
    • A change of 1 kelvin is the same size as a change of 1 Celsius degree. The scales just start at different places.

Temperature Conversions

Is 20 °C “twice as hot” as 10 °C? No: in kelvin those temperatures are 293 K and 283 K, and their particles have nearly the same average kinetic energy. Only the Kelvin scale is proportional to the average kinetic energy of particles, so gas laws and many chemistry equations only work in kelvin. Converting from Celsius is one of the most common calculations in chemistry.

  • Your Reference Table converts between them: K = °C + 273.15
    • To go from kelvin to Celsius, subtract: °C = K − 273.15.

Some Important Temperatures

Since substances behave differently at different temperatures, chemists rely on a set of agreed-upon reference temperatures to describe and compare their observations.

Some Important Temperatures

The volume of a gas, the speed of a reaction and even the density of water all change with temperature. To compare results fairly, chemists agree on a few standard temperatures. That way, a measurement made in New York can be compared with one made in Tokyo.

  • Standard Temperature – baseline temperature that scientists use for consistency in describing chemical properties and reactions. For gases, standard temperature is 0 °C (273.15 K).
