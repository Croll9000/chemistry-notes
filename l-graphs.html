Graphs & Proportional Relationships

A graph shows the relationship between an independent and a dependent variable. Its shape and slope reveal whether two quantities are directly proportional, inversely proportional or linearly related.

Part1

Making Graphs

Since 1958, scientists at the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii have measured the carbon dioxide in the air. The yearly average rose from about 316 parts per million (ppm) in 1959 to about 421 ppm in 2023. A table of more than sixty yearly values is hard to read, but a graph shows the steady rise at a glance. In this section, we will discuss how to make a graph from the data of an experiment.

Making a Graph

A graph turns a table of numbers into a picture of the relationship between two variables. Scientists follow the same rules every time, so anyone can read a graph correctly.

  • The independent variable goes on the x-axis (see Experimental Design) (horizontal) and the dependent variable goes on the y-axis (vertical).
  • Label each axis with the quantity and its unit, for example “Volume of gas (mL).”
  • Choose a scale for each axis:
    • The intervals must be even (every space stands for the same amount).
    • The scale must include every value, and the data should fill most of the grid.
  • Plot each data point.
  • Draw a Line of Best Fit – a straight line or smooth curve that follows the trend of the data, with about as many points above it as below. Do not connect the dots.
  • Give the graph a title in the form “dependent variable vs. independent variable.”

Part2

Reading Graphs and Slope

Five samples of zinc with volumes of 1.00 to 5.00 cm³ have masses that rise by about 7.1 g for every 1.00 cm³ added. On a graph of mass against volume, that steady rise is the slope of the line, and it equals the density of zinc, 7.14 g/cm³. In this section, we will discuss how to read values from a graph and how to calculate and interpret its slope.

Reading Values from a Graph

A graph can answer questions about values that were never measured. On the Mauna Loa graph, no point was plotted for 1995, but the line passes through that year, so the carbon dioxide level in 1995 can still be read.

  • To read a value: go up from the x-axis to the line, then across to the y-axis.
  • Interpolation – reading a value between the measured data points.
  • Extrapolation – extending the line to predict a value beyond the measured data. It assumes the trend continues, so it is less reliable than interpolation.
Example
Using the graph, the carbon dioxide level in 1995 was about 362 ppm. This is interpolation, because 1995 is between measured years (1990 and 2000).

Slope

The slope of a graph tells how fast the dependent variable changes when the independent variable changes. A steeper line means a faster change. The slope often has a meaning of its own: on a graph of mass against volume, the slope is the density.

  • Slope – the change in y divided by the change in x (rise over run).
  • Pick two points on the line that are far apart. Use data points only if the line passes through them.
  • The unit of a slope is the y-axis unit divided by the x-axis unit, for example g/cm³ or mL/s.
  • A line that rises has a positive slope, a line that falls has a negative slope, and a horizontal line has a slope of zero.
  • The equation of any straight line is y = mx + b:
    • m is the slope.
    • b is the y-intercept – the value of y where the line crosses the y-axis (when x = 0).
    • When b = 0, the line passes through the origin.

Part3

Proportional Relationships

A 2.0 L bottle of water has twice the mass of a 1.0 L bottle. When the air in a sealed syringe is squeezed into half of its volume, its pressure doubles, from 100 kPa to 200 kPa. In the first case, doubling one quantity doubles the other; in the second, doubling one quantity halves the other. In this section, we will discuss direct and inverse relationships and how to recognize them in data and graphs.

Direct and Inverse Relationships

Many relationships in chemistry follow one of a few simple patterns. Recognizing the pattern in a table or a graph makes it possible to predict what happens when one variable changes. The gas laws in Unit 8 are built on these patterns.

  • Direct Relationship – as one variable increases, the other increases.
    • Directly proportional: yx is constant (y = kx). The graph is a straight line through the origin, and doubling x doubles y.
  • Inverse Relationship – as one variable increases, the other decreases.
    • Inversely proportional: x × y is constant (y = kx). The graph is a curve that falls steeply and then levels off, and doubling x cuts y in half.
  • A straight line that does not pass through the origin is linear but not proportional: yx is not constant.
    • Example: Kelvin temperature = Celsius temperature + 273.
  • A horizontal line has a slope of zero: y remains constant as x changes.
  • To test data: calculate yx and x × y for several points. Whichever stays the same identifies the relationship.
Example
Air in a sealed syringe at constant temperature: 60 mL at 100 kPa, 40 mL at 150 kPa and 30 mL at 200 kPa. Pressure × volume = 6000 kPa·mL every time, so the volume is inversely proportional to the pressure. (This is Boyle’s law, in Unit 8.)

Linearizing Data

A curved graph is hard to test by eye, because many different curves look alike. A straight line is easy to check, and its slope can be measured. Scientists therefore change one of the variables before plotting, so that the graph becomes a straight line.

  • Linearization – changing the data (for example, plotting 1x instead of x) so that the graph becomes a straight line.
  • If y is inversely proportional to x (y = kx), plot y vs. 1x.
  • If the new graph is a straight line through the origin, the inverse relationship is confirmed, and the slope is the constant k (k = x × y).
Example
For air in a sealed syringe, the graph of pressure vs. volume is a curve. The graph of pressure vs. 1/volume is a straight line through the origin with a slope of 6000 kPa·mL, the constant value of P × V.
