Graphs & Proportional Relationships

A graph shows the relationship between an independent and a dependent variable. Its shape and slope reveal how two quantities are related: directly proportional, linear, inversely proportional, constant, quadratic or inverse square.

Making Graphs

Why Make a Graph?

Since 1958, scientists at the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii have measured the amount of carbon dioxide in the air. Written out as a table, the yearly averages are a column of more than sixty numbers: 315.98, 316.91, 317.64 and so on. Reading down that column, you might notice that the numbers are getting larger, but it is very hard to say how quickly, whether the increase is steady or speeding up, or whether any one year was unusual. However, when the same numbers are plotted on a graph, what we see right away is that the carbon dioxide level has risen every year, and that the line is getting slightly steeper over time.

This is the main reason scientists make graphs. A graph turns a table of numbers into a picture, so that the pattern or relationship between two variables can be seen at a glance. From the shape of a graph we can tell whether the dependent variable increases, decreases or stays the same as the independent variable changes, and from its steepness we can tell how quickly it changes. A graph also makes any data point that does not fit the pattern stand out, which often points to an error in a measurement.

A graph can even answer questions about values that were never measured. If a year between two plotted points is needed, we can go up from that year on the x-axis to the line, and then across to the y-axis, and read off the value. This is known as interpolation.

Interpolation – reading a value between the measured data points.

We can also extend the line past the last measurement in order to predict a value, such as the carbon dioxide level in 2040. We call this extrapolation. Extrapolation assumes that the pattern continues beyond the data, which may not be true, so it is less reliable than interpolation.

Extrapolation – extending the line to predict a value beyond the measured data. It assumes the trend continues, so it is less reliable than interpolation.

  • A graph turns a table of numbers into a picture, so that patterns and relationships between variables can be seen at a glance.
  • A graph shows whether the dependent variable increases, decreases or stays the same as the independent variable changes, and how quickly it changes.
  • A graph makes data points that do not fit the pattern easy to spot.
  • A graph can be used to find values that were never measured:
    • Interpolation – reading a value between the measured data points.
    • Extrapolation – extending the line to predict a value beyond the measured data. It assumes the trend continues, so it is less reliable than interpolation.
  • To read a value: go up from the x-axis to the line, then across to the y-axis.
Example
Using the graph, the carbon dioxide level in 1995 was about 362 ppm. This is interpolation, because 1995 is between measured years (1990 and 2000).

How to Make a Graph

A graph is only useful if everyone who looks at it reads it the same way, so scientists follow the same rules every time they make one. The first decision is which variable goes on which axis. By convention, the independent variable goes on the x-axis and the dependent variable goes on the y-axis. This way, reading a graph from left to right always shows what happens to the measured quantity as the quantity we changed on purpose increases. Each axis is labeled with the quantity it shows and its unit, since a number such as “40” means nothing until we know whether it is 40 mL or 40 °C.

The next decision, choosing a scale, is where most graphs go wrong. If the scale is too large, the data are squeezed into a corner of the grid and the pattern is hard to see. If it is too small, some points do not fit at all. Every space on an axis must also stand for the same amount, or the shape of the graph will be distorted. A simple way to choose a scale is to divide the largest value by the number of gridlines on that axis, and then round up to the next convenient number, such as 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 or 50. In the case of temperatures that reach 82 °C on an axis with 10 gridlines, 82 °C ÷ 10 = 8.2 °C per gridline, which rounds up to 10 °C per gridline.

Once the points are plotted, we draw a line of best fit, a straight line or smooth curve that follows the trend of the data with about as many points above it as below. It may seem natural to connect the dots, but we do not connect the dots. This is due to the fact that every measurement contains a little error, so the points scatter slightly around the true relationship, and the line of best fit averages out that scatter. Finally, the graph is given a title in the form “dependent variable vs. independent variable.” The steps to making a graph are:

  • Steps to making a graph:
    1. Choose the axes. The independent variable goes on the x-axis (horizontal) and the dependent variable goes on the y-axis (vertical).
    2. Label each axis with the quantity and its unit, for example “Volume of gas (mL).”
    3. Choose a scale for each axis. Divide the largest value by the number of gridlines on that axis, then round up to the nearest convenient number (such as 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 or 50). Count by that number on every gridline.
    4. Plot each data point.
    5. Draw a line of best fit – a straight line or smooth curve that follows the trend of the data, with about as many points above it as below. Do not connect the dots.
    6. Give the graph a title in the form “dependent variable vs. independent variable.”
  • The intervals on each axis must be even: every space stands for the same amount.
  • The scale must include every value, and the data should fill most of the grid.
Example
The largest mass in a data table is 35.5 g, and the y-axis has 10 gridlines. 35.5 g ÷ 10 = 3.55 g per gridline, which rounds up to the next easy number, 5 g per gridline. The y-axis is numbered 0, 5, 10, 15, … 50 g, so every value fits and the data fill most of the grid.

Slope

Slope

A student measures the mass of five samples of zinc with volumes from 1.00 cm³ to 5.00 cm³ and finds that each additional 1.00 cm³ adds about 7.1 g. On a graph of mass vs. volume, the points fall on a straight line, and how steeply that line climbs tells us how much mass is added for each cubic centimeter. We call this steepness the slope.

Slope – the change in y divided by the change in x: how much the dependent variable changes for each unit of change in the independent variable.

In order to find the slope, we pick two points on the line that are far apart and divide the difference in their y-values by the difference in their x-values. We use points on the line rather than data points since the line averages out the small errors in the individual measurements; a data point should only be used if the line passes through it. Points that are far apart are also better than points that are close together, since any small error in reading the graph becomes a smaller fraction of the larger differences.

The unit of a slope is the y-axis unit divided by the x-axis unit, and the unit often reveals what the slope means. For the zinc samples, the slope has the unit g/cm³, which is the unit of density. Thus, the slope of a mass vs. volume graph is the density of the substance: (35.5 g − 7.1 g) ÷ (5.00 cm³ − 1.00 cm³) = 7.1 g/cm³ for zinc. A line that rises from left to right has a positive slope, a line that falls has a negative slope, and a horizontal line has a slope of zero. Any straight line can be written as y = mx + b, where m is the slope and b is the y-intercept, the value of y where the line crosses the y-axis. When b = 0, the line passes through the origin.

  • Slope – the change in y divided by the change in x: how much the dependent variable changes for each unit of change in the independent variable.
  • Pick two points on the line that are far apart. Use data points only if the line passes through them.
  • The unit of a slope is the y-axis unit divided by the x-axis unit, for example g/cm³ or mL/s.
  • A line that rises has a positive slope, a line that falls has a negative slope, and a horizontal line has a slope of zero.
  • The equation of any straight line is y = mx + b:
    • m is the slope.
    • b is the y-intercept – the value of y where the line crosses the y-axis (when x = 0).
    • When b = 0, the line passes through the origin.

Types of Mathematical Relationships

Types of Mathematical Relationships

A 2.0 L bottle of water has twice the mass of a 1.0 L bottle. On the other hand, if you add water to a glass of juice until it has twice the volume, the juice becomes half as concentrated. In the first case the two quantities rise together, while in the second, one rises as the other falls. Most relationships between two variables in chemistry follow one of a handful of patterns, and each pattern has its own graph shape and, more usefully, its own quantity that stays constant. Identifying which quantity stays constant is how a scientist decides which relationship a set of data follows.

Directly Proportional

When increasing one variable increases the other by the same factor, so that doubling x doubles y and tripling x triples y, the two variables are directly proportional. Since y and x grow together, their ratio never changes, so yx is constant. The mass and volume of a substance are directly proportional, and the constant ratio, massvolume, is its density.

Directly Proportional – as x increases, y increases by the same factor. yx is constant. The graph is a straight line through the origin.

Linear

A linear relationship also produces a straight line, but the line does not have to pass through the origin. Consider Kelvin temperature plotted against Celsius temperature. Every increase of 1 °C is an increase of 1 K, so the slope is constant, but at 0 °C the Kelvin temperature is 273 K, not zero. Since yx is not constant in this case, the two temperatures are linearly related but not directly proportional. This is why 20 °C is not “twice as hot” as 10 °C.

Linear – y changes by the same amount for every equal change in x. The slope is constant. The graph is a straight line that does not have to pass through the origin.

Inversely Proportional

When increasing one variable decreases the other by the same factor, so that doubling x cuts y in half, the two variables are inversely proportional. If one is multiplied by a factor and the other is divided by the same factor, their product never changes, so x × y is constant. The graph falls steeply at first and then levels off, approaching but never reaching the x-axis. The juice is one example: the concentration of a solution and its volume are inversely proportional when a fixed amount of solute is diluted with water. The pressure and volume of a gas at constant temperature are another.

Inversely Proportional – as x increases, y decreases by the same factor. x × y is constant. The graph is a curve that falls steeply and then levels off.

Constant

Sometimes changing the independent variable has no effect at all. A small piece of aluminum and a large piece both have a density of 2.70 g/cm³, so a graph of density vs. sample size is a horizontal line with a slope of zero, and y itself is constant. This is still a useful result, since it tells us that y remains constant as x changes.

Constant – y stays the same as x changes. y is constant. The graph is a horizontal line.

Quadratic

In a quadratic relationship, y depends on the square of x, so doubling x multiplies y by four and tripling x multiplies y by nine. In this case, it is the ratio of y to x squared that stays the same, so yx2 is constant. The graph starts at the origin and grows steeper and steeper. For some chemical reactions, the rate depends on the square of the concentration of a reactant, so doubling the concentration makes the reaction go four times as fast.

Quadratic – y increases with the square of x. yx2 is constant. The graph is a curve that gets steeper and steeper.

Inverse Square

In an inverse square relationship, y depends on 1 divided by the square of x, so doubling x cuts y to one fourth, and tripling x cuts it to one ninth. The product of y and x squared stays the same, so y × x2 is constant. The graph looks like an inverse curve but falls even more steeply. According to Coulomb’s law, the attraction between two charged particles follows this pattern: moving a proton and an electron twice as far apart makes the attraction between them only one fourth as strong.

Inverse Square – y decreases with the square of x. y × x2 is constant. The graph falls even more steeply than an inverse curve.

In order to identify the relationship in a set of data, we calculate yx, x × y, yx2 and y × x2 for several data points. Whichever quantity stays the same, within the error of the measurements, identifies the relationship.

  • Directly Proportional – as x increases, y increases by the same factor. yx is constant. The graph is a straight line through the origin.
  • Linear – y changes by the same amount for every equal change in x. The slope is constant. The graph is a straight line that does not have to pass through the origin.
  • Inversely Proportional – as x increases, y decreases by the same factor. x × y is constant. The graph is a curve that falls steeply and then levels off.
  • Constant – y stays the same as x changes. y is constant. The graph is a horizontal line.
  • Quadratic – y increases with the square of x. yx2 is constant. The graph is a curve that gets steeper and steeper.
  • Inverse Square – y decreases with the square of x. y × x2 is constant. The graph falls even more steeply than an inverse curve.
  • To test data: calculate yx, x × y, yx2 and y × x2 for several points. Whichever stays the same identifies the relationship.
Example
Air in a sealed syringe at constant temperature: 60 mL at 100 kPa, 40 mL at 150 kPa and 30 mL at 200 kPa. Pressure × volume = 6000 kPa·mL every time, so the volume is inversely proportional to the pressure. (This is Boyle’s law, in Unit 8.)
