The Aufbau Principle

Building an atom one electron at a time

Aufbau Principle – electrons fill the orbitals and subshells of the lowest available energy before moving to orbitals and subshells of higher energy.
Please note that there are a number of exceptions to this principle (see the Exceptions page).

Aufbau is German for "building up." Imagine building an atom by adding its electrons one at a time. Each new electron goes into the lowest-energy orbital that still has room. Only when a subshell is full does the next electron move up to the next subshell.

With the Bohr model, you filled one energy level completely before moving to the next (fluorine is 2-7, argon is 2-8-8). Your notes warned that this only works for elements 1–18. Here is why: an electron's energy depends on both its shell number and its subshell letter.

  • Within a shell, the subshells increase in energy in the order s < p < d < f.
    • Ex: 2s fills before 2p.
    • Ex: 3s fills before 3p, which fills before 3d.
  • Subshells from different shells overlap.
    • The 4s subshell is lower in energy than the 3d subshell, so 4s fills before 3d.
    • That's why potassium (19) and calcium (20) put their last electrons in shell 4 even though shell 3 isn't full yet.
    • Ex: potassium is 2-8-8-1.
  • After 4s, the 3d subshell fills (scandium to zinc), and then 4p.

Think of water poured into a container: it settles into the lowest space first and rises from there. Electrons do the same with energy.

The filling order

Memorizing the energy order is hard, so chemists use the diagonal rule:

  1. Write the subshells in rows, one row per shell: 1s; 2s 2p; 3s 3p 3d; …
  2. Draw diagonal arrows from upper right to lower left.
  3. Follow the arrows in order, from the top arrow to the bottom.
    • Each arrow lists subshells in the order they fill.

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s …

Reading a configuration from the periodic table

The periodic table is arranged into blocks representing subshells: the s block, d block, and p block (and an f block further down). To determine the electron configuration of an element:

  1. Identify its location on the periodic table.
  2. Count through each block on the table leading up to that element.
  3. List the subshells and number of electrons that must be added to reach the element's position.
