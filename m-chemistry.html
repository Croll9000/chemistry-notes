Chemistry and Matter

Chemistry

In science we take a systematic approach to describing, understanding and looking for patterns in the natural world around us. Science is broken up into different branches. Biology is the study of living things. Geology is the study of the Earth and what makes it up. In chemistry, we study matter and how it reacts. As a field this is important because matter makes up everything that we see around us. The chair that you’re sitting on, the pencil that you’re holding and even the Earth itself are all composed of matter.

Chemistry – the study of matter: its properties, composition and how it reacts.

Over time, matter undergoes changes, and chemistry can help us explain how and why that happens and then allow us to make predictions about the future. For instance, if you’ve eaten recently there might be food in your stomach. Your stomach secretes a chemical known as hydrochloric acid, HCl, which helps break down the proteins in your food through a chemical reaction. You might also have had to light a match before to ignite your stove or a campfire. The striking strip on the side of the matchbox contains red phosphorus, an element. When the match is struck, the heat from friction turns a tiny amount of it into a more reactive form of phosphorus, which combines with oxygen in the air to produce tetraphosphorus decoxide, P4O10. This reaction gives off a lot of energy, enough to set the match head burning. This is the light that you see and the heat that you feel.

Chemistry is all around us in the medicine that we take, the electronics that we use and the clothes that we wear. Understanding how these materials are structured at their most fundamental level is the goal of chemists.

  • Chemistry – the study of matter: its properties, composition and how it reacts.

A Brief History of Chemistry

The origins of chemistry can be found in alchemy. Alchemy was an early combination of philosophy, science and mysticism. Alchemists sought to purify metals and cure diseases. Some alchemists hunted for elixirs of immortality and something known as the philosopher’s stone. The philosopher’s stone was believed to be an object that could turn lesser metals into gold and maybe even extend a person’s life.

Although much of the methodology of alchemy was decidedly unscientific, it did lay the foundations for chemistry. Alchemists developed laboratory equipment and processes such as distillation, and they made acids, gunpowder, dyes and medicines. In many ways, the chemists of today still pursue similar goals of developing new materials and creating new medicines.

Chemistry began to develop into a science in the 17th and 18th centuries with the wider adoption of empirical and rationalist beliefs. In 1620, Sir Francis Bacon published Novum Organum, an early version of what would become the scientific method. It allowed for a rigorous, systematic approach to studying and understanding the world.

Robert Boyle, who lived from 1627 to 1691, advocated for a less philosophical and more scientific approach to chemistry. He rejected the idea that the world was made up of the four classical elements, earth, air, fire and water, and instead believed that it was composed of small particles. He also came up with early models for describing gases.

In the 1700s, a number of different chemists began to isolate and study individual gases. In 1754, the Scottish chemist Joseph Black isolated carbon dioxide. In 1766, Henry Cavendish separated hydrogen gas, which he named “inflammable air” due to the way that it reacted explosively in air. In 1774, Joseph Priestley managed to isolate oxygen gas. Throughout his career, Priestley discovered many gases, including ammonia and nitrous oxide, which dentists still use today as a sedative.

Perhaps the most famous early chemist was Antoine Lavoisier. In one of his famous experiments, Lavoisier heated tin inside sealed glass containers. He weighed the materials before and after the reaction and saw that the mass of the tin increased, while the mass of the sealed container as a whole stayed the same. From this he reasoned that the tin was reacting with unseen particles in the air. He concluded that matter was conserved in the reaction and that nothing had been created or destroyed, only rearranged. Through his work, he became known as the father of modern chemistry.

Over time our understanding of the structure of matter progressed. In 1808, the English chemist John Dalton put forth his atomic theory that all matter was composed of small indivisible units known as atoms. Amedeo Avogadro, who lived from 1776 to 1856, proposed that equal volumes of different gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of particles. His idea later allowed chemists to count the number of individual particles in a sample of matter.

In 1869, the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the 63 elements known at the time into the first periodic table. Where no known element fit, he left gaps and predicted the properties of elements that had not yet been discovered.

Working in Paris with her husband Pierre, Marie Curie studied radioactivity, a term she coined. In 1898, they discovered two new elements, polonium and radium. She became the first person to win two Nobel Prizes, in physics in 1903 and in chemistry in 1911.

In 1909, the German chemist Fritz Haber found a way to combine nitrogen from the air with hydrogen to make ammonia, NH3. Ammonia is used to make fertilizer, and the food grown with that fertilizer now feeds about half of the people on Earth. However, Haber also directed Germany’s use of chlorine gas as a weapon during World War I.

The American chemist Linus Pauling spent much of his career studying what holds atoms together. His 1939 book, The Nature of the Chemical Bond, shaped the way chemists explain bonding. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1954 and the Nobel Peace Prize in 1962 for campaigning against nuclear weapons testing.

Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin and Rosalind Franklin both used X-rays to work out how atoms are arranged in substances. Hodgkin determined the structures of penicillin and vitamin B12 and won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1964. Franklin’s X-ray images of DNA were key evidence for its double helix shape, which was described in 1953.

In 1965, Stephanie Kwolek, a chemist at DuPont, created the fiber now known as Kevlar. By weight, it is five times stronger than steel, and it is used in bulletproof vests, helmets and tires.

Chemistry also powers the electronics that we use. John Goodenough, M. Stanley Whittingham and Akira Yoshino developed the lithium-ion battery, which is found in nearly every phone and laptop today. They shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2019.

In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed that CRISPR-Cas9, a system that bacteria use to defend themselves against viruses, could be used to cut DNA at an exact location. This allows scientists to edit genes, and in 2023 it was used in the first approved treatment for sickle cell disease. They won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020.

Most recently, the 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Susumu Kitagawa, Richard Robson and Omar Yaghi for creating metal–organic frameworks. These are crystals built with large empty spaces inside them, and they can be designed to pull water out of desert air or to capture carbon dioxide.

What Is Matter?

Since chemistry is the study of matter, we first need a clear way of deciding what counts as matter. To be classified as matter, something must have mass, and it must take up volume (space). The desk in front of you, the water in your bottle and even your own body all have mass and take up space. Less obviously, so does the air in the room around you.

Matter – anything that has mass and takes up volume (space).

For instance, consider a basketball before and after it is pumped full of air. If both are placed on a sensitive balance, the inflated ball is slightly heavier. Since the only thing that was added was air, the air must have mass. The air also takes up space, which is what pushes the rubber out into a firm, round shape.

Mass and volume are two of the most basic measurements in chemistry. Mass is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in grams (g) or kilograms (kg) using a balance. At the level of particles, the mass of an object depends on how many particles it contains and what type of particles they are.

Mass – the amount of matter (“stuff”) in an object.

Volume is the amount of space an object takes up. It is measured in milliliters (mL), liters (L) or cubic centimeters (cm³). A graduated cylinder is used to measure the volume of a liquid, and a ruler can be used for a solid with a regular shape. Note that 1 cm³ is exactly the same as 1 mL.

Volume – the amount of space an object takes up.

It is important to keep mass and volume separate. A bowling ball and a beach ball take up about the same volume, but the bowling ball has a far greater mass because much more matter is packed into the same amount of space. How tightly matter is packed into a space is a separate property called density, which we will look at later in this unit.

  • Matter – anything that has mass and takes up volume (space).
  • Mass – the amount of matter (“stuff”) in an object.
    • Unit – grams (g) or kilograms (kg)
    • Measuring device – balance
  • Volume – the amount of space an object takes up.
    • Unit – milliliters (mL), liters (L) or cubic centimeters (cm³)
    • Measuring device – graduated cylinder (liquids); ruler (regular solids)
Your pencil would be an example of matter.
Most things that you can see and touch every day would be considered matter.

Air is also matter, even though it is invisible. A balloon full of air has mass and takes up space.

What Isn’t Matter?

What Isn’t Matter?

Not everything that exists is matter. Light, heat and electricity are all real, since we can see and feel the effects that they have. Sunlight warms your skin, and a hot stove can burn your hand. However, when we apply the two tests for matter, light, heat and electricity fail both of them.

For instance, a flashlight that is switched on has the same mass as one that is switched off, no matter how long it shines. A pot of hot soup does not have a greater mass than the same soup once it has cooled, and a room does not become more crowded when the lights are turned on. This means that light, heat and electricity have no mass and take up no space.

Instead of being matter, they are forms of energy. The same is true of sound, which is energy carried through matter. Matter and energy together make up everything in the universe, and much of chemistry is the study of how changes in matter are tied to changes in energy. When wood burns, for example, the wood and the oxygen in the air are changed into new substances, and energy is given off as heat and light.

Matter or Not?

Energy

Matter can store energy and pass it along. When you turn on a flashlight, chemicals inside the battery react, and the chemical energy stored in them becomes electrical energy that flows through the wires. In the bulb, that electrical energy becomes light, and a little of it becomes heat, which is why a bulb that has been on for a while feels warm. At every step, energy is being passed from one form to the next.

Energy is measured in joules (J) or kilojoules (kJ), where 1 kJ = 1000 J.

Energy – the ability to do work or cause change.

Energy itself cannot be seen. We can only detect it through the changes that it causes, such as an object speeding up, a sample getting warmer or a bulb giving off light. For this reason, the definition of energy describes what energy does rather than what it is.

Energy can take many forms, including chemical, electrical, light, heat and motion, and it can change from one form to another again and again. No matter how many times it changes form, the total amount of energy stays the same. Energy cannot be created or destroyed, but it can be converted from one form into another. When a flashlight battery “dies,” its energy has not been destroyed. It has been converted into light and heat, which spread out into the room until the chemicals in the battery have no more energy to give.

  • Energy – the ability to do work or cause change.
    • Unit – joule (J), kilojoule (kJ)
      • 1 kJ = 1000 J
    • It represents the ability to create a change in a system.
  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed, but it can be converted from one form into another.
