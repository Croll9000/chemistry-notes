The Equilibrium Constant

Two flasks of the same reaction at the same temperature can start with very different amounts of reactants, yet when they reach equilibrium, one ratio of their concentrations comes out the same. That ratio is the equilibrium constant. In this section, we will discuss how to write and use the equilibrium constant.

The Equilibrium Constant (Keq)

For a general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD:

Keq=[C]c[D]d[A]a[B]b
[ ] – equilibrium concentration (M)a, b, c, d – coefficients
  • Equilibrium constant (Keq) – the ratio of the concentrations of the products to the reactants at equilibrium, each raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation.
  • Products go in the numerator and reactants in the denominator.
  • Solids and pure liquids are left out of the expression, because their concentrations do not change.
  • Keq has one value for a reaction at a given temperature; it changes only when the temperature changes.
  • The size of Keq tells which side the equilibrium favors:
    • Keq > 1: more products than reactants at equilibrium (product-favored).
    • Keq < 1: more reactants than products at equilibrium (reactant-favored).
  • Steps to calculating Keq:
    1. Write the balanced equation.
    2. Write the Keq expression, leaving out solids and pure liquids.
    3. Substitute the equilibrium concentrations.
    4. Solve.

The Reaction Quotient (Q)

A mixture that has just been made is usually not at equilibrium yet. By calculating the same ratio with the concentrations at that moment, a chemist can predict which way the reaction will go before it happens. In this section, we will discuss the reaction quotient.

The Reaction Quotient (Q)

Q is written exactly like Keq, but uses the concentrations at any moment, not only at equilibrium.

  • Reaction quotient (Q) – the ratio of products to reactants, each raised to the power of its coefficient, using the concentrations at any moment.
  • Q < K: there are too few products, so the forward reaction is faster and the reaction shifts right.
  • Q > K: there are too many products, so the reverse reaction is faster and the reaction shifts left.
  • Q = K: the system is at equilibrium.
