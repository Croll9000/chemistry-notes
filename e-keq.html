The Equilibrium Constant

The Equilibrium Constant (Keq)

Two flasks of the same reaction at the same temperature can start with very different amounts of reactants and end up with very different amounts of each substance at equilibrium. Yet when the concentrations in each flask are combined in one particular ratio, the result is the same number in both. That number is a property of the reaction itself, as characteristic of it as a boiling point is of a liquid.

The equilibrium constant (Keq) is the ratio of the concentrations of the products to the reactants at equilibrium, each raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation. Products go in the numerator and reactants in the denominator. Solids and pure liquids are left out of the expression, because their concentrations do not change. Keq has one value for a reaction at a given temperature, and it changes only when the temperature changes.

The size of Keq tells which side the equilibrium favors. Since the products are on top, a Keq greater than 1 means there are more products than reactants at equilibrium, so the reaction is product-favored, while a Keq less than 1 means there are more reactants than products, so it is reactant-favored. To calculate Keq:

Keq=[C]c[D]d[A]a[B]b
[ ] – equilibrium concentration (M)a, b, c, d – coefficients

The Reaction Quotient (Q)

The Reaction Quotient (Q)

A mixture that has just been made is usually not at equilibrium yet, and a chemist may want to know which way it will shift as it settles. The reaction quotient (Q) is written exactly like Keq, but it uses the concentrations at any moment, not only at equilibrium, so comparing Q with K shows how far, and in which direction, the mixture still has to go.

If Q < K, there are too few products, so the forward reaction is faster and the reaction shifts right, making more products until Q rises to K. If Q > K, there are too many products, so the reverse reaction is faster and the reaction shifts left. If Q = K, the system is at equilibrium, and there is no net change.

  • Reaction quotient (Q) – the ratio of products to reactants, each raised to the power of its coefficient, using the concentrations at any moment.
  • Q < K: there are too few products, so the forward reaction is faster and the reaction shifts right.
  • Q > K: there are too many products, so the reverse reaction is faster and the reaction shifts left.
  • Q = K: the system is at equilibrium.
