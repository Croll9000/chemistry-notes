Dimensional Analysis

Equivalences and Conversion Factors

In 1983, an Air Canada jet ran out of fuel in the middle of a flight because its fuel had been calculated in pounds instead of kilograms, giving the plane less than half the fuel its crew believed it had. A single unit left unconverted can turn a correct number into a dangerous one. Stoichiometry is the part of chemistry that answers the question “how much?”: how much fuel a rocket needs, or how much medicine is in a tablet. Every one of these questions is answered by converting one quantity into another, and the tool for every conversion in this unit is the same one used in Unit 0, dimensional analysis.

In dimensional analysis we use a series of equivalences and conversion factors to get from the given unit to the desired unit. An equivalence is two equal quantities with different units, such as 1 kg = 1000 g. A conversion factor is a fraction made from an equivalence, in which the numerator and denominator are equal quantities with different units, such as 1000 g1 kg. From each equivalence we can make two conversion factors, with either unit in the numerator. Since both quantities are equal, every conversion factor is equal to 1, so multiplying by one changes the unit but not the amount.

  • In science, it is often necessary to convert from one unit of measurement to another.
    • One of the quickest ways to do this is with a process known as dimensional analysis.
    • In this process we use a series of equivalences and conversion factors to get from the given unit to the target (desired) unit.
    • Equivalence – two equal quantities with different units.
    • Conversion Factor – fraction/ratio of one scientific unit to another where the numerator and denominator are equal quantities with different units.
  • From each equivalence we can make 2 conversion factors, with either unit in the numerator. Since both quantities are equal, each conversion factor is equal to “1”.

Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis, also called the factor-label method, is a method of unit conversion that involves multiplying by conversion factors. Each conversion factor is set up so that the unit we want to get rid of is in the denominator, where it cancels with the same unit in the numerator. When entering numbers in scientific notation into a calculator, surround them with parentheses. The steps for dimensional analysis are:

  1. Write down the given number and the unit you wish to convert.
  2. Identify the desired unit.
  3. Identify relevant equivalences.
  4. Set up the conversion factor, with the given unit in the denominator and the desired unit in the numerator.
  5. Cancel out units that are in both the numerator and the denominator.
  6. Multiply the given quantity by all numbers in the numerator, and divide by all numbers in the denominator.
  7. Check that the final answer has appropriate units.
  • Dimensional Analysis (also called the factor-label method) – method of unit conversion which involves multiplying by fractions known as conversion factors.
  • Steps to Dimensional Analysis:
    1. Write down the given number and unit you wish to convert.

    3. Identify relevant equivalences.

  • Dimensional analysis can be used to convert between different magnitude units of the same quantity within the metric system. See Unit 0: Dimensional Analysis for worked examples.

The Mole

Moles

A single drop of water contains about 1,700,000,000,000,000,000,000 molecules. Atoms and molecules are so small that numbers like this are impossible to work with one particle at a time, so chemists count particles in groups. Just as a “dozen” represents 12 of something, 1 mole represents 6.02 × 1023 of something. 1 mole = 6.02 × 1023 particles, whether the particles are atoms, molecules or formula units, and the Reference Table lists this equivalence. The number of particles in one mole is called Avogadro’s number, 6.02 × 1023. It is so large that counting the atoms in 12 grams of carbon-12 at a rate of one billion per second would take about 19 million years.

  • Similarly to how a “dozen” represents 12 of something, 1 mole represents 6.02 × 1023 of something.
    • 1 mole = 6.02 × 1023 particles (atoms, molecules or formula units).
    • The Reference Table lists this equivalence: 1 mole = 6.02 × 1023.
  • Avogadro’s Number – the number of particles in one mole: 6.02 × 1023

Mole–Particle Conversions

Mole–Particle Conversions

A 200-mg ibuprofen tablet contains about 5.8 × 1020 ibuprofen molecules, or about 0.00097 mole. A reaction in the lab is measured in moles, but it happens one particle at a time, so we often need to convert between moles and numbers of particles. Because every mole holds 6.02 × 1023 particles, to convert from moles to particles, we multiply by 6.02 × 1023, and to convert from particles to moles, we divide by 6.02 × 1023. In dimensional analysis, this is done with the conversion factor 6.02 × 1023 particles1 mol or its reverse.

  • To convert from moles to particles, multiply by 6.02 × 1023.
  • To convert from particles to moles, divide by 6.02 × 1023.

Moles and Equations

Moles and Equations

The reaction 2NO + O2 → 2NO2 turns the nitrogen monoxide in car exhaust into the brown haze of smog. No chemist ever measures out exactly two molecules of a gas, so the same balanced equation has to describe both the particles and the amounts measured in the lab. When reading a chemical equation, the coefficients represent the number of atoms and molecules taking part in the reaction at the atomic level. Because every mole contains the same number of particles, at the macroscopic level the coefficients also represent the number of moles of each substance. The equation 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O can therefore be read two ways: two molecules of hydrogen react with one molecule of oxygen to produce two molecules of water, or two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to produce two moles of water.

  • Recall that when reading a chemical equation, the coefficients represent the number of atoms and molecules taking part in the reaction at an atomic level.
    • If we look at the reaction at a macroscopic level, the coefficients can also represent the number of moles of each substance taking part in the reaction.
  • 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O can be read 2 ways:
    • Atomic level: two molecules of hydrogen react with one molecule of oxygen to produce two molecules of water.
    • Macroscopic level: two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to produce two moles of water.
