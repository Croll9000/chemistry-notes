Equivalences and Conversion Factors

Stoichiometry is the part of chemistry that answers “how much?” How much fuel does a rocket need? How much medicine is in a tablet? Every one of these questions is answered by converting one quantity into another, and the tool for every conversion in this unit is the same one used in Unit 0: dimensional analysis.

  • In science, it is often necessary to convert from one unit of measurement to another. One of the quickest ways to do this is with a process known as dimensional analysis. In this process we use a series of equivalences and conversion factors to get from the given unit to the target (desired) unit.
    • Equivalence – two equal quantities with different units.
    • Conversion Factor – fraction/ratio of one scientific unit to another where the numerator and denominator are equal quantities with different units.
  • From each equivalence we can make 2 conversion factors, with either unit in the numerator. Since both quantities are equal, each conversion factor is equal to “1”.

Dimensional Analysis

  • Dimensional Analysis (also called the factor-label method) – method of unit conversion which involves multiplying by fractions known as conversion factors.
  • Steps to Dimensional Analysis:
    1. Write down the given number and unit you wish to convert.
    2. Identify the desired unit.
    3. Identify relevant equivalences.
    4. Set up the conversion factor you wish to multiply by. Place the given unit in the denominator and the desired unit in the numerator.
    5. Cancel out units that are in both the numerator and denominator.
    6. Multiply the given quantity by all numbers in the numerator. Divide by all numbers in the denominator.
    7. Check to see that the final answer has appropriate units.
  • When entering numbers in scientific notation into your calculator, make sure to surround them with parentheses, ( ).
  • Dimensional analysis can be used to convert between different magnitude units of the same quantity within the metric system. See Unit 0: Dimensional Analysis for worked examples.

Moles

A single drop of water contains about 1,700,000,000,000,000,000,000 molecules. Numbers this large are impossible to work with one particle at a time, so chemists count particles in groups, the same way a baker counts eggs by the dozen. The chemist’s group is the mole, and it is the central unit of every calculation in this unit.

  • Similarly to how a “dozen” represents 12 of something, 1 mole represents 6.02 × 1023 of something.
    • 1 mole = 6.02 × 1023 particles (atoms, molecules or formula units).
    • The Reference Table lists this equivalence: 1 mole = 6.02 × 1023.
  • Avogadro’s Number – the number of particles in one mole: 6.02 × 1023

Mole–Particle Conversions

A recipe in a chemistry lab is written in moles, but the reaction happens one particle at a time. Converting between moles and numbers of particles connects what we measure in the lab to what happens at the atomic level.

  • To convert from moles to particles, multiply by 6.02 × 1023.
  • To convert from particles to moles, divide by 6.02 × 1023.

Moles and Equations

No chemist ever measures out exactly two molecules of hydrogen. Real reactions involve enormous numbers of particles, so the same balanced equation has to describe both the particles and the amounts measured in the lab. The mole makes that possible.

  • Recall that when reading a chemical equation, the coefficients represent the number of atoms and molecules taking part in the reaction at an atomic level. If we look at the reaction at a macroscopic level, the coefficients can also represent the number of moles of each substance taking part in the reaction.
  • 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O can be read 2 ways:
    • Atomic level: two molecules of hydrogen react with one molecule of oxygen to produce two molecules of water.
    • Macroscopic level: two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to produce two moles of water.
