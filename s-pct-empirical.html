Converting Percent Composition to Empirical Formula

Almost every analysis of an unknown substance ends in a list of percents. Turning those percents into a formula is how chemists begin to identify it. However, a list of percents cannot be turned directly into a formula. A formula counts atoms, and a percent compares masses. An oxygen atom has about 16 times the mass of a hydrogen atom, so a large percent of oxygen may represent only a few atoms.

If we treat the problem as a series of conversions, the path becomes clear. Consider vitamin C. In the 1920s, the Hungarian scientist Albert Szent-Györgyi isolated a white crystalline substance from cabbages and adrenal glands that turned out to be vitamin C, and analysis showed that it is 40.9% carbon, 4.6% hydrogen and 54.5% oxygen by mass.

  • Assuming a 100 g sample turns each percent into a mass: 40.9 g of carbon, 4.6 g of hydrogen and 54.5 g of oxygen.
  • Converting each mass to moles gives 3.41, 4.6 and 3.41 moles.
  • Dividing by the smallest value gives the ratio 1 : 1.33 : 1.
  • Multiplying by 3 gives whole numbers, so the empirical formula is C3H4O3.

Its molar mass, 176 g/mol, shows that the molecular formula is twice this, C6H8O6. To convert percent composition to an empirical formula:

  1. Assume 100 g of the substance, so each percent becomes a mass in grams.
  2. Convert the mass of each element to moles (divide by its atomic mass).
  3. Divide all mole values by the smallest number from step 2.
  4. If the resulting values are whole numbers, use them as the subscripts of the empirical formula. If a value ends in about .5, multiply every value by 2.

Since lab measurements always include some uncertainty, values such as 1.98 or 3.02 are rounded to the nearest whole number. However, a value such as 1.33 or 1.5 is too far from a whole number to round. It means that every value must be multiplied by the same small number, 3 or 2, to make whole numbers.
