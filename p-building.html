How chemists found a pattern in the elements, used it to predict elements no one had seen yet, and why today’s table is ordered by atomic number.

Part1

Mendeleev’s Table

Properties repeat when the elements are placed in order.

Dmitri Mendeleev

One of a chemist’s greatest tools in studying matter is the Periodic Table of Elements. Understanding how to read this table reveals numerous patterns in element characteristics and reactivity. One of the first widely used periodic tables was developed by a chemist named Dmitri Mendeleev, who saw that when elements were placed in order of increasing atomic mass, their characteristics repeated themselves.

  • Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) – Russian chemist who created the first widely used periodic table.
    • He placed the elements in order of increasing atomic mass (weight).
    • He saw that certain properties would repeat themselves.
  • Periodic – repeating at regular intervals.
    • Ex: the days of the week and the phases of the Moon are periodic.
When the elements are lined up in order, elements with similar properties show up at regular intervals (red, green, blue). Starting a new row each time the pattern repeats stacks similar elements into the same column.

Predicting Missing Elements

Mendeleev’s table was more than a way to organize the known elements. Its patterns were reliable enough that he used them to predict elements that had not yet been discovered. When gallium was discovered in 1875 and germanium in 1886, their properties closely matched his predictions.

  • Mendeleev left gaps in his table for elements that had not been discovered yet.
  • He predicted the properties of each missing element from the elements around its gap.
    • Ex: he predicted “eka-silicon,” the element below silicon, would have an atomic mass of about 72 and a density of about 5.5 g/cm³.
    • Germanium was discovered in 1886. Its atomic mass is 72.63 and its density is 5.32 g/cm³.
  • Correct predictions like this convinced scientists that the periodic table was a real pattern in nature.

Part2

The Modern Periodic Table

Ordered by atomic number, not mass.

Periodic Law

  • The modern periodic table is arranged in order of increasing atomic number.
  • Chemical and physical properties repeat because of similarities in the valence (outermost) shell electron configuration.
  • Periodic Law – the physical and chemical properties of elements recur in regular patterns when they are arranged in order of increasing atomic number.
  • As you move across each row on the periodic table, you see the same properties recur.
  • Elements in the same column have similar chemical properties.

Why Atomic Number?

  • In 1913, Henry Moseley found a way to measure each element’s atomic number (number of protons).
  • Ordering by atomic number fixes a few pairs that Mendeleev had to switch by hand.
    • Ex: tellurium (127.60) is heavier than iodine (126.90), but tellurium comes first. Its atomic number is 52; iodine’s is 53.
    • Ex: argon (39.95) is heavier than potassium (39.10), but argon comes first. Its atomic number is 18; potassium’s is 19.
  • With atomic number order, every element lands in the column with the elements it behaves like.
