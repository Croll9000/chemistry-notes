Building the Periodic Table

How chemists found a pattern in the elements, used it to predict elements no one had seen yet, and why today’s table is ordered by atomic number.

Part1

Mendeleev’s Table

Dmitri Mendeleev

How can a chart of elements predict substances no one has ever seen?

By the 1860s, chemists had discovered 63 elements. Each one had its own mass, its own appearance and its own way of reacting, and there was no clear way to organize them. Learning chemistry meant memorizing a long list of unrelated facts. Several chemists had noticed that some elements seemed to come in families, but no one had found an arrangement that worked for all of them.

Dmitri Mendeleev, a Russian chemistry professor, ran into this problem while writing a textbook for his students. He wrote the name, atomic mass and properties of each known element on a separate card. He then arranged and rearranged the cards on his desk, much like someone laying out a game of solitaire, looking for a pattern.

When the cards were placed in order of increasing atomic mass, Mendeleev noticed that certain properties came back again and again. Lithium, sodium and potassium are all soft metals that react with water, and they appear at regular intervals along the list with the same number of elements between each one. Something that repeats at regular intervals, like the days of the week or the phases of the Moon, is described as periodic.

Mendeleev started a new row each time the properties began to repeat. As a result, elements with similar properties lined up in the same column. In 1869 he published this arrangement, the first widely used periodic table. You can think of it as a list of the elements folded into rows so that the repeating pattern can be seen at a glance.

  • Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) – Russian chemist who created the first widely used periodic table.
    • He placed the elements in order of increasing atomic mass (weight).
    • He saw that certain properties would repeat themselves.
  • Periodic – repeating at regular intervals.
    Example
    The days of the week and the phases of the Moon are periodic.
When the elements are lined up in order, elements with similar properties show up at regular intervals (red, green, blue). Starting a new row each time the pattern repeats stacks similar elements into the same column.

Predicting Missing Elements

Mendeleev’s table did not fit perfectly. In a few places, the next element by mass did not have the properties of the next column. Instead of forcing it in, Mendeleev skipped a space and left a gap. He reasoned that these gaps belonged to elements that had not been discovered yet.

He went a step further. Since every column held a family of similar elements, the properties of a missing element should fall between those of its neighbors above, below and beside it. Using this idea, he predicted the atomic mass, density and even the formulas of the compounds of several missing elements. For the gap below silicon, which he called “eka-silicon,” he predicted an atomic mass of about 72 and a density of about 5.5 g/cm³.

In 1886, the German chemist Clemens Winkler discovered germanium. Its atomic mass is 72.6 and its density is 5.3 g/cm³, almost exactly what Mendeleev had predicted fifteen years earlier. Gallium and scandium, discovered in the same years, also matched his predictions for two other gaps.

A table that only organized known facts would have been useful. However, a table that correctly predicted unknown facts was far more convincing. Correct predictions like these convinced scientists that the periodic table was a real pattern in nature, not simply a convenient way to arrange cards. Germanium itself went on to matter a great deal. The first transistors, built at Bell Labs in 1947 and the starting point of all modern electronics, were made from germanium.

  • Mendeleev left gaps in his table for elements that had not been discovered yet.
  • He predicted the properties of each missing element from the elements around its gap.
    Example
    He predicted “eka-silicon,” the element below silicon, would have an atomic mass of about 72 and a density of about 5.5 g/cm³.
    Germanium was discovered in 1886. Its atomic mass is 72.63 and its density is 5.32 g/cm³.
  • Correct predictions like this convinced scientists that the periodic table was a real pattern in nature.

The Modern Periodic Table

Periodic Law

Mendeleev could see the pattern, but he could not explain it. Electrons had not yet been discovered, so no one knew why fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine should all react with metals in the same way, or why neon, argon, krypton and xenon should all refuse to react at all. The pattern itself, however, was clear, and it became one of the central ideas of chemistry. Today it is stated as the periodic law.

Periodic Law – the physical and chemical properties of elements recur in regular patterns when they are arranged in order of increasing atomic number.

If you look at any column of the modern periodic table, the elements in it behave alike. Thus, elements in the same column have similar chemical properties. Moving along a row, the properties change step by step from a reactive metal on the left to an unreactive gas on the right, and then the same sequence starts over in the next row.

We now know where this repetition comes from. Each new row begins a new energy level, and as you move across the row, the number of valence electrons climbs from 1 to 8. When the next row begins, the count starts over at 1. Since an atom’s chemical behavior depends mostly on its valence electrons, the same valence arrangements produce the same behavior in every row. Chemical and physical properties repeat because of similarities in the valence (outermost) shell electron configuration.

The same idea matters in medicine. Lithium carbonate is prescribed to treat bipolar disorder, and doctors must watch for the fact that the body handles lithium ions somewhat like the sodium ions in the same column, so changes in a patient’s salt intake can change the amount of lithium in the blood.

  • The modern periodic table is arranged in order of increasing atomic number.
  • Chemical and physical properties repeat because of similarities in the valence (outermost) shell electron configuration.
  • Periodic Law – the physical and chemical properties of elements recur in regular patterns when they are arranged in order of increasing atomic number.
  • As you move across each row on the periodic table, you see the same properties recur.
  • Elements in the same column have similar chemical properties.

Why Atomic Number?

Mendeleev had to break his own rule in a few places. Ordering the elements strictly by mass would place iodine (126.9) before tellurium (127.6). However, iodine clearly belongs with chlorine and bromine, and tellurium clearly belongs with sulfur and selenium. Mendeleev switched the two by hand and assumed the masses must have been measured incorrectly. They had not been.

The answer came in 1913 from the young English physicist Henry Moseley. Moseley fired beams of electrons at samples of different elements and measured the X-rays that each element gave off. He found that the X-rays changed by a regular step from one element to the next, and he concluded that the step matched the charge of the nucleus. In other words, he had found a way to measure each element’s atomic number, its number of protons.

When the elements are ordered by atomic number, tellurium (52) comes before iodine (53), and every element lands in the column with the elements it behaves like. Ordering by atomic number fixes the pairs that Mendeleev had to switch by hand.

This should make sense. The atomic number is what defines an element, and it also sets the number of electrons in a neutral atom. Mass, on the other hand, also depends on the number of neutrons, and neutrons have almost no effect on chemical properties. Tellurium simply happens to have a few more neutrons than iodine. Moseley was killed fighting in World War I two years later, at the age of 27, but his ordering is the one used on every periodic table today.

  • In 1913, Henry Moseley found a way to measure each element’s atomic number (number of protons).
  • Ordering by atomic number fixes a few pairs that Mendeleev had to switch by hand.
    Example
    Tellurium (127.60) is heavier than iodine (126.90), but tellurium comes first. Its atomic number is 52; iodine’s is 53.
    argon (39.95) is heavier than potassium (39.10), but argon comes first. Its atomic number is 18; potassium’s is 19.
  • With atomic number order, every element lands in the column with the elements it behaves like.
