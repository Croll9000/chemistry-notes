Predicting Synthesis Reactions

Predicting Synthesis Reactions

In a synthesis reaction, two or more simple substances combine to form one more complex product. The key to predicting that product is identifying the types of reactants.

A metal and a nonmetal form an ionic compound. We find its formula by criss-crossing the charges of the ions and reducing if necessary. Hydrogen and a nonmetal form a molecular compound, and we find its formula by combining the atoms in the ratio that fills each element’s valence shell.

Consider potassium and bromine, which react so violently that the reaction is only ever done behind a safety shield. Potassium is a metal and bromine is a nonmetal, so they form an ionic compound. Potassium forms K+ and bromine forms Br−, so the compound is KBr. Bromine is diatomic, so the balanced equation is:

2K + Br2 → 2KBr

Since the diatomic elements, H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2 and I2, are always written as pairs, the equation must be balanced last. To predict a synthesis reaction:

  • In a synthesis reaction, two or more simple substances combine to form one more complex product.
  • Steps to Predicting Synthesis Reactions:
    1. Identify the types of reactants: a metal and a nonmetal, or two nonmetals (such as hydrogen and a nonmetal).
    2. Write the formula for the product.
      • Metal + nonmetal → ionic compound: criss-cross the charges of the ions and reduce if necessary.
      • Hydrogen + nonmetal → molecular compound: combine the atoms in the ratio that fills each element’s valence shell (count the bonds each atom forms).
    3. Balance the equation. Remember the diatomic elements (H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2).

Predicting Decomposition Reactions

Predicting Decomposition Reactions

In a decomposition reaction, one compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. The simplest case is a binary compound, made of only two elements.

Binary compound – a compound made of only two elements.

A binary compound decomposes into its two elements. Each element is written as a product in its standard form, so diatomic elements are written as H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2 and I2.

The decomposition of a binary compound is the reverse of its synthesis. Synthesis builds 2H2O from 2H2 + O2. Decomposition, driven by electricity, splits 2H2O back into 2H2 + O2.

A car’s airbag depends on a decomposition that happens in about 30 milliseconds, faster than the blink of an eye. In many airbags, a crash sensor sets off a small electrical spark that decomposes a solid called sodium azide into sodium metal and a large volume of nitrogen gas:

2NaN3 → 2Na + 3N2

The nitrogen fills the bag. To predict a decomposition reaction:

  • In a decomposition reaction, one compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. Energy (heat or electricity) is often needed.
  • Binary compound – a compound made of only two elements.
    Example
    NaCl, H2O, Al2O3
  • A binary compound decomposes into its two elements.
  • Steps to Predicting Decomposition Reactions:
    1. Identify the two elements in the binary compound.
    2. Write each element as a product in its standard form. Diatomic elements are written as H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2 and I2.
    3. Balance the equation.
  • Decomposition of a binary compound is the reverse of the synthesis of that compound.

Oxides in Water

Oxides in Water and Acid Rain

A compound of an element with oxygen is known as an oxide, and oxides react with water in two very different ways.

Oxide – a compound of an element with oxygen.

When metal oxides combine with water, they produce basic solutions of metal hydroxides.

CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2

When nonmetal oxides combine with water, they produce acids, such as carbonic acid:

CO2 + H2O → H2CO3

The second process leads to acid rain. Burning fossil fuels releases nonmetal oxides such as sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere. There they combine with water and oxygen to produce acids such as sulfuric acid (H2SO4), nitric acid (HNO3) and carbonic acid (H2CO3). Acid rain lowers the pH of lakes and soil, and it damages forests and buildings.

New York felt this directly. By the 1980s, hundreds of lakes in the Adirondack Mountains had become so acidic that fish could no longer survive in them. The cause was hundreds of kilometers away. Coal-burning power plants in the Midwest released sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, and the wind carried them east, where they fell as acid rain. After the Clean Air Act was strengthened in 1990, emissions dropped sharply, and many Adirondack lakes have slowly begun to recover.

  • Oxide – a compound of an element with oxygen.
  • When metal oxides combine with water, they produce basic solutions of metal hydroxides.
    Example
    CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2
  • When nonmetal oxides combine with water, they produce acids.
    Example
    CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 (carbonic acid)
  • This process can lead to the formation of acid rain.
    • Burning fossil fuels releases nonmetal oxides such as sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere.
    • These nonmetal oxides combine with water (and oxygen) in the atmosphere to produce acids such as sulfuric acid (H2SO4), nitric acid (HNO3) and carbonic acid (H2CO3).
    • Acid rain lowers the pH of lakes and soil and damages forests and buildings.
