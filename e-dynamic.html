Reversible Reactions

Reversible Reactions

Sealed glass tubes of nitrogen dioxide, NO2, are a favorite chemistry demonstration. Placed in ice water, the reddish-brown gas fades to almost colorless as it turns into dinitrogen tetroxide, N2O4. Placed in hot water, the brown color returns. The same tube can be switched back and forth again and again, because the reaction can run in both directions.

A reversible reaction is a reaction in which the products can react to form the original reactants again. A double arrow (⇌) shows a reversible reaction: 2NO2(g) ⇌ N2O4(g). The forward reaction reads left to right, while the reverse reaction reads right to left. In fact, most reactions can run in both directions, as long as the products stay where they can collide with one another.

This is why a reversible reaction can reach equilibrium only in a closed system, where no reactants or products can enter or leave. If a product escapes, as carbon dioxide does from an open glass of soda, the reverse reaction cannot happen, and the reaction simply runs in one direction until it is done.

  • Reversible reaction – a reaction in which the products can react to form the original reactants again.
  • A double arrow (⇌) shows a reversible reaction: 2NO2(g) ⇌ N2O4(g).
    • The forward reaction reads left to right; the reverse reaction reads right to left.
  • A reversible reaction can reach equilibrium only in a closed system, where no reactants or products can enter or leave.

Dynamic Equilibrium

Dynamic Equilibrium

Imagine a shopping mall on a busy Saturday afternoon. People are constantly walking in through the doors and walking out, yet the number of people inside stays about the same all afternoon, because people enter at the same rate that others leave. Nothing seems to be changing, even though there is a great deal of motion.

A reversible reaction behaves the same way. When it begins, the forward reaction is fast, since there are plenty of reactants, while the reverse reaction is slow, since there are few products. As products build up, the reverse reaction speeds up, and as reactants are used up, the forward reaction slows down, until the two rates are equal. Equilibrium is the state in which the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, so there is no net change in the amounts of reactants and products.

Equilibrium is dynamic: both reactions keep happening, at equal rates. Additionally, at equilibrium the concentrations of the reactants and products are constant, but they are not necessarily equal. The mall may hold 300 shoppers and only 50 employees, yet both numbers stay steady. On a graph of concentration against time, equilibrium begins where the lines become flat.

  • Equilibrium – the state in which the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, so there is no net change in the amounts of reactants and products.
  • Equilibrium is dynamic: both reactions keep happening, at equal rates.
  • At equilibrium the concentrations of the reactants and products are constant, but they are not necessarily equal.
  • On a graph of concentration against time, equilibrium begins where the lines become flat.
