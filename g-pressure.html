Gas Pressure

Pressure

At sea level, the air pushes on every square centimeter of your skin with a force of about 10 newtons, roughly the weight of a 1 kg bag of sugar. We do not feel this push because the fluids inside the body push outward with the same pressure. The force comes from an enormous number of air molecules colliding with every surface they touch. Each molecule that strikes a surface gives it a tiny push, and the total push on each unit of area is what we call pressure. Pressure is calculated by dividing the force by the area it acts on, as shown in the equation below.

Any change that makes the particles hit the walls more often or harder raises the pressure. Adding more particles to the same container means more collisions every second. Raising the temperature makes the particles move faster, so they hit the walls both more often and with more force. Measuring Pressure

Barometers and Pressure Gauges

In 1643, Evangelista Torricelli filled a long glass tube with mercury, turned it upside down in a dish of mercury, and watched the column fall until it was about 760 mm tall. The air pushing down on the mercury in the dish held the rest of the column up. Torricelli had built the first barometer, an instrument used to measure the pressure of the air. The higher the pressure of the surrounding air, the higher the mercury column rises, so the height of the column is a direct measure of the air pressure. At sea level the column stands 760 mm tall, which defines standard pressure: 1 atm = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg.

The pressure of the air depends on how much air is above us. At the top of a mountain there is less air pushing down, so the higher the altitude, the lower the air pressure. To measure the pressure of a gas inside a closed container, such as a tire or a gas cylinder, a pressure gauge is used instead.

  • Barometer – an instrument used to measure air (atmospheric) pressure.
    • The first barometers used inverted tubes whose bases were submerged in mercury. The pressure of the surrounding gas pushed the mercury up the tube.
    • The higher the external pressure, the higher the column of mercury rises. The height of the mercury column measures the external gas pressure.
    • At sea level, the column is 760 mm tall. This is standard pressure: 1 atm = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg.
    • The higher the altitude, the lower the air pressure, because there is less air above pushing down.
  • Pressure Gauge – an instrument used to measure the pressure of a gas inside a container, such as a tire or a gas cylinder.

Units of Pressure

Pressure Units and Conversions

Each unit of pressure comes from the way pressure was measured. The millimeter of mercury (mmHg) is the pressure that holds up a column of mercury 1 mm tall, as in Torricelli’s barometer. The atmosphere (atm) is the average pressure of the air at sea level. The kilopascal (kPa) is 1000 pascals, and the pascal, one newton of force on one square meter, is the SI unit of pressure. The kilopascal and the atmosphere are the two units used most often in chemistry.

Because the three units all describe standard pressure, 1 atm = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg. Any two of these amounts are equal, so each pair can be written as a conversion factor. This means that a pressure in millimeters of mercury can be converted directly to kilopascals using 760 mmHg = 101.3 kPa, without first converting to atmospheres.
