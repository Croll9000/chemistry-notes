Gas Pressure

At sea level, the air pushes on every square centimeter of your skin with a force of about 10 newtons, about the weight of a 1 kg bag of sugar. That force comes from an enormous number of air molecules colliding with every surface they touch. In this section, we will discuss what pressure is and why gases exert it.

Pressure

You do not feel the air pushing on you because the fluids inside your body push back with the same pressure. Seal a plastic water bottle on a mountaintop and drive back down, and the outside air crushes it: the air at the bottom of the mountain pushes harder than the air sealed inside.

  • Gas particles are constantly moving and colliding with each other and with the walls of their container. When they collide with the walls, they apply a force to them. This force, per unit area, is the pressure.
  • Pressure (P) – the amount of force per unit area acting on a surface.
P=FA

  • The pressure of a gas increases when the particles hit the walls more often or harder:
    • More particles in the same container means more collisions each second.
    • A higher temperature means faster particles, which hit the walls more often and harder.
    • A smaller volume means the particles reach the walls more often.

Measuring Pressure

In 1643, Evangelista Torricelli filled a glass tube with mercury, turned it upside down in a dish of mercury, and found that the column fell until it was about 760 mm tall. The air pushing down on the dish held the rest of the column up, so the height of the mercury measured the pressure of the air. In this section, we will discuss how the pressure of the air and of a gas in a container are measured.

Barometers and Pressure Gauges

Weather forecasters watch the air pressure closely: falling pressure often means an approaching storm, and rising pressure means clearing skies. The pressure of the air above us also changes with altitude, because there is less air pushing down at the top of a mountain than at sea level.

  • Barometer – an instrument used to measure air (atmospheric) pressure.
    • The first barometers used inverted tubes whose bases were submerged in mercury. The pressure of the surrounding gas pushed the mercury up the tube.
    • The higher the external pressure, the higher the column of mercury rises. The height of the mercury column measures the external gas pressure.
    • At sea level, the column is 760 mm tall. This is standard pressure: 1 atm = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg.
    • The higher the altitude, the lower the air pressure, because there is less air above pushing down.
  • Pressure Gauge – an instrument used to measure the pressure of a gas inside a container, such as a tire or a gas cylinder.

Units of Pressure

A weather report in the United States might give the air pressure as 29.92 inches of mercury, while a forecaster in Europe gives the same reading as 1013 hectopascals. Both describe one force over one area, so any pressure unit can be converted into another with dimensional analysis. In this section, we will discuss the three units of pressure used in chemistry and convert between them.

Pressure Units and Conversions

Pressure units came from the instruments used to measure them. The millimeter of mercury is the height of a mercury column, the atmosphere is the average pressure of the air at sea level, and the pascal is the SI unit, one newton of force on one square meter.

  • The two most common units of pressure are kilopascals (kPa) and atmospheres (atm). A less commonly used unit is millimeters of mercury (mmHg).
  • Each unit of pressure is defined by the measurement it came from:
    • Atmosphere (atm) – the average pressure of the air at sea level.
    • Kilopascal (kPa) – 1000 pascals; the pascal is the SI unit of pressure.
    • Millimeter of mercury (mmHg) – the pressure that holds up a column of mercury 1 mm tall. It is also called the torr.
  • 1 atm = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg
  • Pressure units are converted using dimensional analysis. The given unit is placed in the denominator of the conversion factor so that it cancels.
