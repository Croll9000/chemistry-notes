Systems and Surroundings

Systems and Surroundings

A disposable hand warmer, opened on a cold day, heats up within minutes. Inside the packet, iron powder reacts with oxygen from the air, forming iron oxide, the same reaction as rusting but much faster. The reaction releases energy, and that energy flows out of the packet into your hands. To describe where energy comes from and where it goes, chemists divide the world into two parts: the part they are studying, and everything else.

The system is the specific part of the universe that is being focused on, usually the molecules taking part in a chemical reaction, such as the iron and oxygen in the hand warmer. The surroundings are everything that is not part of the system, such as the packet, your hands and the air. Together, they make up the universe: the system and its surroundings together.

Dividing the world this way makes energy changes easy to describe. Energy can only move across the boundary between the system and the surroundings, so any energy the system loses, the surroundings gain, and any energy the system gains comes from the surroundings.

  • When analyzing chemical reactions, it is often useful to do so through the lens of systems and surroundings.
  • System – specific part of the universe that is being focused on.
    • Usually, the molecules taking part in a chemical reaction.
  • Surroundings – everything that is not part of the system.
  • Universe – the system and its surroundings together.

Example: Magnesium and Hydrochloric Acid

A strip of magnesium ribbon, Mg(s), is added to a beaker of aqueous hydrochloric acid, HCl(aq). Bubbles of hydrogen gas, H2(g), are produced, and a thermometer in the beaker shows the temperature of the solution rising. The strip of magnesium slowly dissolves as aqueous magnesium chloride, MgCl2(aq), is produced.

In this reaction, the system consists of the particles taking part in the reaction: Mg, HCl, MgCl2 and H2. The surroundings are everything else, including the beaker, the water the acid is dissolved in and the thermometer. A thermometer can only measure the surroundings, since it is part of them. Since the temperature of the surroundings rose, heat must have been transferred from the system, the reaction, to the surroundings.

  • To better understand the difference between a system and its surroundings, let’s look at a chemical reaction:
    • A strip of magnesium ribbon, Mg(s), is added to a beaker of aqueous hydrochloric acid, HCl(aq).
      • Bubbles of hydrogen gas, H2(g), are produced and the temperature of the solution increases.
      • The strip of magnesium dissolves as aqueous magnesium chloride, MgCl2(aq), is produced.
  • In this reaction, the system consists of the particles taking part in the reaction: Mg, HCl, MgCl2 and H2. The surroundings are everything else, including the beaker, the water and the thermometer.
  • Since the temperature rose and the thermometer and water are part of the surroundings, heat was transferred from the system (the reaction) to the surroundings.

Open, Closed and Isolated Systems

Open vs. Closed Systems

A pot of soup boiling on the stove loses both heat and steam into the kitchen. Put a tight lid on the pot, and the steam is trapped, but heat still escapes through the metal. Pour the soup into a high-quality thermos, and for several hours almost nothing, neither soup nor heat, gets in or out. The boundary around a system determines whether matter or energy may pass from one side to the other.

Systems can be defined as open, closed or isolated. In an open system, both matter and energy are allowed to pass freely between the system and its surroundings, like the open pot. In a closed system, energy is allowed to move between the system and its surroundings, but matter is not, like the pot with a lid. In an isolated system, neither matter nor energy is allowed to move between the system and its surroundings, like the thermos.

The type of system matters when testing the law of conservation of mass. In an open system, gases can escape or enter, and mass seems to change, so mass is only conserved on a balance when the reaction is carried out in a closed system.

  • The boundary between the system being studied and its surroundings will determine whether matter or energy may pass from one to the other. We can define systems as open, closed or isolated.
  • Open System – both matter and energy are allowed to pass freely between the system and its surroundings.
  • Closed System – energy is allowed to move between the system and its surroundings, but matter is not.
  • Isolated System – neither matter nor energy is allowed to move between the system and its surroundings.

Heat

Heat

A mug of hot cocoa left on a table slowly cools until it is the same temperature as the room, and a glass of iced tea slowly warms until it reaches the same temperature. Neither drink ever goes the other way: the cocoa never gets hotter on its own, and the tea never gets colder. Energy always flows in the same direction, from the warmer object to the cooler one.

Heat is energy transferred from a high temperature object to a low temperature object, measured in joules (J) or kilojoules (kJ). If we analyze the particles, it becomes clear why heat always flows this way. When two objects at different temperatures are brought into contact, the fast-moving particles in the high-temperature object collide with the slower-moving particles in the low-temperature object, and in each collision, kinetic energy is passed to the slower particle. The transferred energy is what we call heat.

Thus, heat always flows from the hotter object to the colder object, until both reach the same temperature. At that point, the particles in both objects have the same average kinetic energy, and the collisions no longer transfer energy in one direction more than the other.

  • Heat – energy transferred from a high temperature object to a low temperature object.
    • Unit – joule (J), kilojoule (kJ)
  • If two objects at different temperatures are brought into contact, the fast-moving particles in the high-temperature object will collide with the slower-moving particles in the low-temperature object.
    • In the process, their kinetic energy will be transferred to the cooler object.
    • This transferred energy is what we call heat.
  • Heat always flows from the hotter object to the colder object, until both reach the same temperature.

Endothermic and Exothermic

Thermal Energy Transfer

An instant cold pack and a hand warmer both start a chemical reaction when they are activated, but they feel completely different in your hand. The cold pack feels cold because its contents absorb energy from your skin, while the hand warmer feels warm because its contents release energy into your skin. Your hand is part of the surroundings, and it feels the energy moving in or out of the system.

During a chemical reaction, there is always a change in energy as reactants are converted into products, so energy is either absorbed or released by the molecules undergoing the reaction, the system. An endothermic reaction is a reaction which absorbs energy from its surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings decreases. An exothermic reaction is a reaction which releases energy to its surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings increases.

It is important to keep track of which part is the system. A thermometer measures the surroundings, so a falling temperature means the system absorbed energy and the reaction is endothermic, while a rising temperature means the system released energy and the reaction is exothermic.

  • During a chemical reaction, there will always be a change in energy as reactants are converted into products. Energy will be either absorbed or released by the molecules undergoing the reaction (the system).
  • Endothermic Reaction – reaction which absorbs energy from its surroundings.
    • The system absorbs energy from the surroundings and the temperature of the surroundings decreases.
  • Exothermic Reaction – reaction which releases energy to its surroundings.
    • The system releases energy to the surroundings and the temperature of the surroundings increases.

Endothermic and Exothermic Equations

Lime, CaO, used to make cement and plaster, is produced by heating limestone, CaCO3, in a kiln to more than 800 °C. The reaction will not happen without a constant supply of heat, which makes heat something that has to be put in, like a reactant. Chemists show this by writing the heat into the equation itself:

In an endothermic reaction, heat is absorbed from the surroundings, so heat is written on the reactants side of a chemical equation: 178 kJ + CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). In an exothermic reaction, heat is released to the surroundings, so heat is written on the products side, as when hydrogen burns and gives off heat along with water.

  • We can denote whether a chemical reaction absorbs or releases heat by including it in a chemical equation.
    • In an endothermic reaction, heat is absorbed from the surroundings, and it is written on the reactants side of a chemical equation.
    • In an exothermic reaction, heat is released to the surroundings, and it is written on the products side of a chemical equation.
Note how in the endothermic reaction, heat is written as a reactant since it is absorbed over the course of the reaction. In the exothermic reaction, heat is a product because it is released or produced. In either reaction we can write “heat” or the specific number of joules or kilojoules that are absorbed or released.
