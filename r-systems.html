Systems, Surroundings & Energy

To track energy in a reaction, split the universe in two: the system you are studying and everything around it. Heat flows between them, and its direction tells you whether a reaction is endothermic or exothermic.

Part1

Systems and Surroundings

Where is the reaction, and where is everything else?

Systems and Surroundings

  • When analyzing chemical reactions, it is often useful to do so through the lens of systems and surroundings.
  • System – specific part of the universe that is being focused on.
    • Usually, the molecules taking part in a chemical reaction.
  • Surroundings – everything that is not part of the system.
  • Universe – the system and its surroundings together.

Example: Magnesium and Hydrochloric Acid

  • To better understand the difference between a system and its surroundings, let’s look at a chemical reaction:
    • A strip of magnesium ribbon, Mg(s), is added to a beaker of aqueous hydrochloric acid, HCl(aq). Bubbles of hydrogen gas, H2(g), are produced and the temperature of the solution increases. The strip of magnesium dissolves as aqueous magnesium chloride, MgCl2(aq), is produced.
  • In this reaction, the system consists of the particles taking part in the reaction: Mg, HCl, MgCl2 and H2. The surroundings are everything else, including the beaker, the water and the thermometer.
  • Since the temperature rose and the thermometer and water are part of the surroundings, heat was transferred from the system (the reaction) to the surroundings.

Open, Closed and Isolated Systems

What can cross the boundary?

Open vs. Closed Systems

  • The boundary between the system being studied and its surroundings will determine whether matter or energy may pass from one to the other. We can define systems as open, closed or isolated.
  • Open System – both matter and energy are allowed to pass freely between the system and its surroundings.
  • Closed System – energy is allowed to move between the system and its surroundings, but matter is not.
  • Isolated System – neither matter nor energy is allowed to move between the system and its surroundings.

Part3

Heat

Energy on the move.

Heat

  • Heat – form of energy that is transferred between two objects at different temperatures.
    • Unit – joule (J), kilojoule (kJ)
  • If two objects at different temperatures are brought into contact, the fast-moving particles in the high-temperature object will collide with the slower-moving particles in the low-temperature object. In the process, their kinetic energy will be transferred to the cooler object. This transferred energy is what we call heat.
  • Heat always flows from the hotter object to the colder object, until both reach the same temperature.
Part4

Endothermic and Exothermic

Which way does the heat go?

Thermal Energy Transfer

  • During a chemical reaction, there will always be a change in energy as reactants are converted into products. Energy will be either absorbed or released by the molecules undergoing the reaction (the system).
  • Endothermic Reaction – reaction which absorbs energy from its surroundings.
    • The system absorbs energy from the surroundings and the temperature of the surroundings decreases.
  • Exothermic Reaction – reaction which releases energy to its surroundings.
    • The system releases energy to the surroundings and the temperature of the surroundings increases.

Endothermic and Exothermic Equations

  • We can denote whether a chemical reaction absorbs or releases heat by including it in a chemical equation.
    • In an endothermic reaction, heat is absorbed from the surroundings, and it is written on the reactants side of a chemical equation.
    • In an exothermic reaction, heat is released to the surroundings, and it is written on the products side of a chemical equation.
Note how in the endothermic reaction, heat is written as a reactant since it is absorbed over the course of the reaction. In the exothermic reaction, heat is a product because it is released or produced. In either reaction we can write “heat” or the specific number of joules or kilojoules that are absorbed or released.
