Calculations with Significant Figures

A calculator does not account for the precision of measurements. A calculated answer must be rounded so it is no more precise than the data it came from.

Rounding Calculated Answers

An answer can’t be more precise than the least precise measurement.

Why Round?

  • A calculated answer cannot be more precise than the least precise measurement used to calculate it.
  • Your calculator will often show many more digits than you actually know.
    • Ex: a sample of aluminum has a mass of 12.50 g and a volume of 4.6 mL.
    • The calculator says 12.50 ÷ 4.6 = 2.7173913… g/mL.
    • The volume was only measured to 2 significant figures, so the density can only have 2 significant figures: 2.7 g/mL.
  • We always write down the calculator’s answer first, then the final rounded answer.
measurements12.50 g ÷ 4.6 mL=calculator’s answer2.7173913→final rounded answer2.7 g/mL

Rounding Rules

  1. Find the last digit you are keeping.
  2. Look at the next digit to its right.
    • If it is 5 or more, round up.
    • If it is 4 or less, keep the digit the same.
  3. Drop the rest of the digits.
    • If the dropped digits are to the left of the decimal point, replace them with zeros so the number stays the same size.
  • If the zeros make the number of sig figs unclear, use scientific notation.
    • Ex: 1,496 to 3 sig figs is 1,500, which looks like 2 sig figs, so write 1.50 × 103.
  • In a problem with several steps, keep all the digits in your calculator and round only the final answer.

Adding and Subtracting

  • When adding or subtracting, the answer can have no more decimal places than the measurement with the fewest decimal places.
  • Count decimal places, not significant figures.
Why? If one measurement is only known to the tenths place, the sum can’t be known to the hundredths place: the hundredths digit of that measurement was never measured.

Multiplying and Dividing

  • When multiplying or dividing, the answer can have no more significant figures than the measurement with the fewest significant figures.
  • Count significant figures, not decimal places.

Exact Numbers

  • Counted Number – a number that comes from counting whole objects, not from reading a measuring tool.
    • Ex: 3 trials, 24 students, 2 beakers
    • You can’t have 2.7 beakers, so there is no estimated digit and no uncertainty.
  • Defined Equivalence – a relationship that is true by definition.
    • Ex: 1 m = 100 cm, 1 kJ = 1000 J
  • Counted numbers and defined equivalences are exact.
    • They have unlimited significant figures.
    • They never limit the answer.
    • Only measured numbers limit the answer.
  • Ex: the average of three masses: (5.24 g + 5.31 g + 5.28 g) ÷ 3 = 5.276666… → 5.28 g
    • The “3” is a counted number, so the answer keeps the hundredths place of the masses.
