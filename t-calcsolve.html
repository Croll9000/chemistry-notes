Steps to Solving Heat Problems

A Regents heat question can involve any of the three heat equations, and the most common mistake is not the arithmetic, but choosing the wrong equation. The problem itself usually tells you which equation to use, if you know what to look for. A starting and an ending temperature mean the temperature is changing, so the heat goes into kinetic energy and q = mcΔT applies. Words such as melts, freezes or fusion, at the melting point, mean a solid ↔ liquid change, so q = mHf applies. Words such as boils, vaporizes, evaporates or condenses, at the boiling point, mean a liquid ↔ gas change, so q = mHv applies. If you are unsure, sketch the heating curve and decide whether the problem describes a sloped segment or a flat one.

Once the equation is chosen, the same steps work for all three. The steps to solving heat problems are:

  1. Identify which equation to use. If the temperature changes, use q = mcΔT. If there is a solid ↔ liquid change, use q = mHf. If there is a liquid ↔ gas change, use q = mHv.
  2. List the variables and the unknown, each with its unit.
  3. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation.
  4. Solve for the unknown algebraically.
  5. Write the final answer with units.

Combining the Heat Equations

Heat for Several Segments

Turning an ice cube from the freezer into a glass of cool water takes three separate steps: warming the ice to 0 °C, melting it and then warming the liquid water. Each step is one segment of the heating curve, and each needs its own calculation. Each sloped segment uses q = mcΔT with the specific heat capacity of that phase, since ice, liquid water and steam each have their own value. Each flat segment uses q = mHf or q = mHv. Because energy is conserved, the total heat is simply the sum of the heat for every segment: qtotal = q1 + q2 + q3 + …

Working through each segment shows where the energy really goes. To turn 50.0 g of ice at −10.0 °C into water at 25.0 °C, warming the ice takes about 1,000 J, warming the liquid water takes about 5,200 J, but melting the ice takes 16,700 J, nearly three quarters of the total. The phase change dominates, just as the long flat segments of the heating curve suggest. Before calculating, it helps to sketch the heating curve, mark the starting and ending temperatures on it and list the segments in between. Be careful to stop at the correct point: if the final temperature is below the boiling point, the vaporization segment is not included at all.
