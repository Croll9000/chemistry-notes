Heating & Cooling Curves

Heat added to a substance goes into either kinetic energy (the temperature rises) or potential energy (a phase change), never both at once.

Part1

Heating and Cooling Substances

A glass of ice water stays at 0 °C until the last piece of ice has melted, even on a warm day. The heat it absorbs is separating water molecules, increasing their potential energy, rather than speeding them up, and only one type of energy changes at a time. In this section, we will discuss how added or removed heat affects the kinetic and potential energy of particles.

Heating Substances

Leave a frozen water bottle on the dashboard of a car parked in the sun and record its temperature every minute. The readings climb from −10 °C toward 0 °C, then stop at 0 °C for a long time while the ice melts, then begin to climb again. The sun never stopped delivering energy, so where did that energy go during the pause? Energy added to particles can do one of two things: speed them up, or pull them apart.

  • When a substance is heated, it gains energy.
    • This energy increases either the kinetic energy or the potential energy of its particles.
    • Only one type of energy increases at a time.
    • If the kinetic energy of the particles increases, they move faster and the temperature of the substance increases.
    • If the potential energy of the particles increases, the particles spread farther apart and a phase change occurs. The temperature stays the same.

Cooling Substances

A pot of soup taken off the stove cools quickly at first. Placed in a freezer, its temperature continues to fall until it reaches its freezing point, where it remains nearly constant for a long time while it turns solid, even though the freezer keeps removing heat. As heat is removed, particles either slow down or move closer together.

  • When a substance is cooled, it loses energy.
    • The energy comes out of either the kinetic energy or the potential energy of its particles.
    • Only one type of energy decreases at a time.
    • If the kinetic energy of the particles decreases, they move slower and the temperature of the substance decreases.
    • If the potential energy of the particles decreases, the particles move closer together and a phase change occurs. The temperature stays the same.
Part2

Heating Curves

Heating 10.0 g of ice from −40 °C to steam at 140 °C takes about 32,000 J, and more than 70% of that energy is used during boiling alone. Each flat segment of a heating curve is a phase change, where added heat increases potential energy, and each sloped segment is a temperature change. In this section, we will read the segments of a heating curve.

Heating Curve

In the previous section, you saw that heat added to a substance either speeds up its particles or separates them. A heating curve records this process as a graph of temperature against heat added. It resembles a staircase of ramps and flat steps, and each segment reveals exactly what the particles are doing and which type of energy they are gaining.

  • Heating Curve – a graph of the temperature of a substance as heat is added to it.
    • The y-axis is the temperature of the substance.
    • The x-axis is the heat added, or the time over which heat is added at a constant rate.
  • On the sloped segments, the temperature increases as heat is added. The kinetic energy of the particles increases while their potential energy stays the same.
  • On the flat segments, the temperature stays the same even though heat is still being added.
    • The energy becomes potential energy, separating the particles in a phase change.
    • Kinetic energy stays the same.
  • The first flat segment is at the melting point (melting); the second is at the boiling point (vaporization).
  • The longer a segment, the more heat that change requires. The vaporization segment is longer than the melting segment because separating liquid particles completely into a gas takes more energy than loosening a solid into a liquid.

Cooling Curves

Steam at 100 °C causes far worse burns than water at 100 °C, because each gram of steam releases 2,260 J as it condenses on the skin. A cooling curve shows the same phase changes as a heating curve in reverse, and its flat segments are where heat is released at a constant temperature. In this section, we will read cooling curves.

Cooling Curve

Steam from a kettle cooling down in a cold room follows a cooling curve: the reverse of the heating curve. The flat steps happen at exactly the same temperatures as on the heating curve, because condensing and boiling happen at the same temperature, and so do freezing and melting.

  • Cooling Curve – a graph of the temperature of a substance as heat is removed from it.
  • On the sloped segments, the temperature decreases and the kinetic energy of the particles decreases. Potential energy stays the same.
  • On the flat segments, heat is still being removed, but the potential energy of the particles decreases as they move closer together in a phase change.
    • Kinetic energy and temperature stay the same.
  • The first flat segment is condensation (at the boiling point); the second is freezing (at the freezing point, which is the same temperature as the melting point).
  • The longer a segment, the more heat is removed during that interval.
