Heating & Cooling Curves

Heat added to a substance goes into either kinetic energy (the temperature rises) or potential energy (a phase change), never both at once.

Part1

Heating and Cooling Substances

Heating Substances

Put a pot of ice on a stove, and the burner delivers heat at a steady rate the whole time. Yet a thermometer in the pot behaves strangely: it rises, stops at 0 °C for several minutes, rises again, then stops at 100 °C for even longer. The heat is going in the entire time, so where is it going when the temperature does not change? The answer comes from the two types of energy that particles can have. When a substance is heated, it gains energy, and that energy increases either the kinetic energy or the potential energy of its particles. Only one type of energy increases at a time.

If the energy goes into kinetic energy, the particles move faster, and since temperature measures average kinetic energy, the temperature of the substance increases. This happens whenever the substance is entirely in one phase: heating solid ice, liquid water or steam simply makes the particles move faster. If the energy goes into potential energy, the particles spread farther apart, which means a phase change is taking place. During the phase change, the particles are not moving any faster on average, so the temperature stays the same, even though heat is still being added.

Why does the energy go into one type and not both at once? At the melting point, any extra energy given to the particles is quickly used to free them from their fixed positions, and the particles stay at the same average speed until all of the solid has melted. Only then is there nothing left to separate, and the added energy can again speed the particles up. In summary, a rising temperature means kinetic energy is increasing, and a constant temperature during heating means potential energy is increasing.

  • When a substance is heated, it gains energy.
    • This energy increases either the kinetic energy or the potential energy of its particles.
    • Only one type of energy increases at a time.
    • If the kinetic energy of the particles increases, they move faster and the temperature of the substance increases.
    • If the potential energy of the particles increases, the particles spread farther apart and a phase change occurs. The temperature stays the same.

Cooling Substances

A pot of soup placed in a freezer cools steadily until it reaches its freezing point, then stays at that temperature for a long time before the frozen soup cools any further. Cooling is simply heating in reverse. When a substance is cooled, it loses energy, and that energy comes out of either the kinetic energy or the potential energy of its particles. Only one type of energy decreases at a time.

If the kinetic energy of the particles decreases, they move more slowly and the temperature of the substance decreases. If the potential energy of the particles decreases, they move closer together and a phase change occurs, while the temperature stays the same. During freezing, for instance, the water molecules in the soup are settling into the fixed positions of ice, and the energy they lose as they come together is released as heat to the freezer.

That released heat explains why the temperature holds steady. As long as the substance is changing phase, the heat given off by particles coming together replaces the heat being removed by the freezer, so the remaining liquid does not cool any further. This is the same reason a large tub of water placed in a cellar can keep stored vegetables from freezing on a cold night: as the water freezes, it releases heat and holds the room near 0 °C.

  • When a substance is cooled, it loses energy.
    • The energy comes out of either the kinetic energy or the potential energy of its particles.
    • Only one type of energy decreases at a time.
    • If the kinetic energy of the particles decreases, they move slower and the temperature of the substance decreases.
    • If the potential energy of the particles decreases, the particles move closer together and a phase change occurs. The temperature stays the same.
Part2

Heating Curves

Heating Curve

A heating curve is a graph of the temperature of a substance as heat is added to it. The y-axis is the temperature of the substance, and the x-axis is either the heat added or the time during which heat is added at a constant rate. Because the burner supplies heat steadily, equal distances along the x-axis represent equal amounts of heat, so the length of each segment shows how much heat that part of the process requires.

A heating curve has two kinds of segments, and each one tells you which type of energy is changing. On the sloped segments, the temperature rises as heat is added, so the kinetic energy of the particles is increasing while their potential energy stays the same. On the flat segments, the temperature does not change even though heat is still being added, so the energy is becoming potential energy as the particles are separated in a phase change, and their kinetic energy stays the same. The first flat segment is at the melting point, where the solid melts, and the second is at the boiling point, where the liquid vaporizes. Between the two flat segments, both phases are present together.

The shape of the curve carries more information than this. The longer a segment, the more heat that change requires. For water, the vaporization segment is almost seven times as long as the melting segment, since vaporizing 1 g of water takes 2,260 J while melting it takes only 334 J. Melting merely loosens the particles so they can slide past one another, while vaporizing must separate them completely. The slopes of the sloped segments also differ. The steeper the slope, the less heat is needed to raise the temperature. Ice and steam need only about half as much heat as liquid water to warm by 1 °C, so their segments are about twice as steep. You will put numbers to every one of these segments on the Heat Calculations pages.

  • Heating Curve – a graph of the temperature of a substance as heat is added to it.
    • The y-axis is the temperature of the substance.
    • The x-axis is the heat added, or the time over which heat is added at a constant rate.
  • On the sloped segments, the temperature increases as heat is added. The kinetic energy of the particles increases while their potential energy stays the same.
  • On the flat segments, the temperature stays the same even though heat is still being added.
    • The energy becomes potential energy, separating the particles in a phase change.
    • Kinetic energy stays the same.
  • The first flat segment is at the melting point (melting); the second is at the boiling point (vaporization).
  • The longer a segment, the more heat that change requires. The vaporization segment is longer than the melting segment because separating liquid particles completely into a gas takes more energy than loosening a solid into a liquid.

Cooling Curves

Cooling Curve

Molten lead poured into a mold to make fishing weights stays at 327 °C, its freezing point, until it has completely hardened, and only then does it cool further. A cooling curve is a graph of the temperature of a substance as heat is removed from it. It is the mirror image of a heating curve: it starts high on the left, steps down through the same phases in reverse order, and pauses at the same two temperatures.

On the sloped segments, the temperature falls, so the kinetic energy of the particles is decreasing while their potential energy stays the same. On the flat segments, heat is still being removed, but the potential energy of the particles is decreasing as they move closer together in a phase change, so the kinetic energy and temperature stay the same. The first flat segment is condensation, at the boiling point, and the second is freezing, at the freezing point. Since a substance condenses at its boiling point and freezes at its melting point, a cooling curve and a heating curve for the same substance have their flat segments at exactly the same temperatures.

As on a heating curve, the length of a segment shows how much heat is removed during that part of the process. The condensation segment is long, because condensing a gas releases just as much heat as was needed to vaporize it. This is why steam can cause such severe burns, and why the large amount of heat released when water vapor condenses into clouds powers thunderstorms and hurricanes.

  • Cooling Curve – a graph of the temperature of a substance as heat is removed from it.
  • On the sloped segments, the temperature decreases and the kinetic energy of the particles decreases. Potential energy stays the same.
  • On the flat segments, heat is still being removed, but the potential energy of the particles decreases as they move closer together in a phase change.
    • Kinetic energy and temperature stay the same.
  • The first flat segment is condensation (at the boiling point); the second is freezing (at the freezing point, which is the same temperature as the melting point).
  • The longer a segment, the more heat is removed during that interval.
