Electrical Charge

Electrical Charge

On a dry winter day, walking across a carpet and then touching a metal doorknob can give you a small shock. Your shoes rub electrons off the carpet as you walk, so your body builds up extra negative charge. When your hand gets close to the doorknob, the extra electrons jump across the gap in a tiny spark. The same thing, on an enormous scale, happens in a lightning bolt.

Electrical charge is a property of matter that can cause an attractive or repulsive force between particles. Charge comes in two types, positive (+) and negative (−). Objects can also be neutral (0), when they have an equal number of positive and negative charges, so that their net charge is zero.

The behavior of charges follows the charge law: opposite charges attract, and like (same) charges repel. A shock from a doorknob happens because the extra electrons on your hand repel one another and are attracted to the neutral metal, where they can spread out.

  • Electrical Charge – property of matter that can cause an attractive or repulsive force between particles
    • Charge comes in different types – positive (+), negative (−)
    • Objects can also be neutral (0).
      • They have an equal number of positive and negative charges.
      • Their net charge is zero.
  • Charge Law – opposite charges attract and like (same) charges repel

What Gives Objects Charge?

A balloon that has just been rubbed on a wool sweater sticks to the wall, while a balloon fresh out of the bag does not. Both balloons are made of exactly the same atoms, with the same protons and electrons. The only difference is that rubbing moved a small number of extra electrons onto the first balloon.

Objects possess particles that are both positively and negatively charged, and every object contains enormous numbers of both. Whether an object is charged depends on the balance between them. The total, or net charge, of an object is the sum of all the charges in the object, found by taking the number of positive charges and subtracting the number of negative charges:

An object with 3 positive charges and 2 negative charges has a net charge of 3 − 2 = +1. It makes sense, then, that an object with extra electrons has a negative net charge, while an object that has lost electrons has a positive net charge. Protons almost never move from one object to another, because they are locked in the nucleus, so changes in charge come from electrons moving.

  • Objects possess particles that are both positively and negatively charged.
  • The total, or “net”, charge of an object can be found by taking the number of positive charges and subtracting the number of negative charges.
    • Net Charge – the sum of all the charges in an object
net charge = positive charges − negative charges

Neutral

Everything around you, your desk, your pencil and your own body, contains countless protons and electrons, yet none of it gives you a shock when you touch it. Most matter is perfectly balanced. Neutral is a state in which an object contains equal numbers of positively and negatively charged particles, so its net charge is zero. A neutral object still contains charged particles; they simply cancel each other out.

  • Neutral – a state in which an object contains equal numbers of positively and negatively charged particles.
Neutral does not mean the absence of charge. Rather, it is when an object contains an equal number of positively and negatively charged particles, so the charges cancel.

Coulomb’s Law

Coulomb’s Law

In 1785, the French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb hung a thin rod from a fine wire and placed a small charged ball at one end. When he brought a second charged ball nearby, the force between the balls twisted the wire, and the amount of twist measured the force. Coulomb discovered two clear patterns: the force grew stronger as the charges grew larger, and it grew weaker, very quickly, as the balls were moved apart.

Coulomb’s law states that the force between charged particles depends on their charges and the distance between them. The force is directly proportional to the charges, while the force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance. It is on the Reference Table:

If we analyze each part of the equation, it becomes clear why the relationships exist. The charges, q1 and q2, are in the numerator, so making either charge larger makes the force larger. The distance, d, is in the denominator, so making the distance larger makes the force smaller, and since the distance is squared, the force drops off quickly. Additionally, the two particles always feel forces of equal size, pointing in opposite directions.

Force and Charge

A calcium ion, Ca2+, holds on to the oxide ions around it in a crystal of lime twice as strongly as a sodium ion, Na+, would at the same distance, because its charge is twice as large. According to Coulomb’s law, as the charges of the particles increase, the coulombic force between them increases, and as the charges decrease, the force decreases. This is a directly proportional relationship between force and charge: double one charge and the force doubles, and double both charges and the force becomes four times as strong.

  • According to Coulomb’s Law, as the charges of the particles increase, the coulombic force between them increases, and vice versa.
  • There is a directly proportional relationship between force and charge.

Force and Distance

Two magnets held a few centimeters apart barely tug on each other, but move them closer together and the pull suddenly becomes strong. Electrical forces behave in a similar way. As the distance between charges increases, the coulombic force between them decreases, and as the distance decreases, the force increases. The force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance, so doubling the distance makes the force 14 as strong, and tripling it makes the force 19 as strong. This is why distance matters so much inside an atom.

  • As the distance between charges increases, the coulombic force between them decreases, and vice versa.
  • Force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance: doubling the distance makes the force 14 as strong.

Charges Inside the Atom

The Nucleus and Its Electrons

A sodium atom gives up its outermost electron easily, which is why sodium metal reacts violently with water. Removing the next electron, which sits in a full shell closer to the nucleus, takes about nine times as much energy. Coulomb’s law explains the enormous difference.

The nucleus holds the electrons in the atom. The nucleus is positive, because it contains the protons, while the electrons are negative, and opposite charges attract. The strength of each attraction depends on distance and charge. By distance, electrons closer to the nucleus are attracted more strongly than electrons farther away. Thus, outer electrons are held the most weakly, and they are the easiest to remove. By charge, a nucleus with more protons pulls harder on its electrons, since a greater positive charge produces a greater force.

These two ideas, distance and nuclear charge, explain nearly every pattern in how atoms behave: why some atoms lose electrons easily, why others gain them, and why atoms get larger or smaller across the periodic table.

  • The nucleus holds the electrons in the atom.
    • The nucleus is positive (it contains the protons).
    • Electrons are negative.
    • Opposite charges attract.
  • Distance: electrons closer to the nucleus are attracted more strongly than electrons farther away.
    • Outer electrons are held the most weakly.
    • Outer electrons are the easiest to remove.
  • Charge: a nucleus with more protons (a greater positive charge) pulls harder on its electrons.

Atoms can gain or lose electrons. When the numbers of protons and electrons are no longer equal, the atom has a net charge and is called an ion.
