Electric Charge & Coulomb’s Law

Protons and electrons are charged. Charged particles push and pull on each other, and these forces hold the atom together.

Electrical Charge

Charge comes in two types. Opposites attract; like charges repel.

Electrical Charge

A balloon rubbed on hair will stick to a wall, and clothes taken out of a dryer often cling together. Lightning is a much larger example of the same effect. Each of these is caused by electric charge, the same property that holds electrons in an atom.

You met the charge law on the Atom Overview page. Here it is again in full, along with net charge and the rule for how strong the force is.

  • Electrical Charge – property of matter that can cause an attractive or repulsive force between particles
    • Charge comes in different types – positive (+), negative (−)
    • Objects can also be neutral (0).
      • They have an equal number of positive and negative charges.
      • Their net charge is zero.
  • Charge Law – opposite charges attract and like (same) charges repel

What Gives Objects Charge?

  • Objects possess particles that are both positively and negatively charged.
  • The total, or “net”, charge of an object can be found by taking the number of positive charges and subtracting the number of negative charges.
    • Net Charge – the sum of all the charges in an object
net charge = positive charges − negative charges

Neutral

  • Neutral – a state in which an object contains equal numbers of positively and negatively charged particles.
Neutral does not mean the absence of charge. Rather, it is when an object contains an equal number of positively and negatively charged particles, so the charges cancel.

Coulomb’s Law

The size of the force depends on how big the charges are and how far apart they are.

Coulomb’s Law

Not every attraction between charges is equally strong. The force depends on how much charge each particle carries and how far apart the particles are, two ideas that explain nearly every trend on the Periodic Table.

  • Coulomb’s Law – the force between charged particles depends on their charges and the distance between them.
    • The force is directly proportional to the charges.
    • The force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance.
    • It is on your Reference Table:

F = coulombic (electrostatic) forceq = charged = distance of separation
Both charges experience the same coulombic force, acting in opposite directions.

Force and Charge

  • According to Coulomb’s Law, as the charges of the particles increase, the coulombic force between them increases, and vice versa.
  • There is a directly proportional relationship between force and charge.
    • Ex: double one charge and the force doubles.

Force and Distance

  • As the distance between charges increases, the coulombic force between them decreases, and vice versa.
  • Force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance: doubling the distance makes the force 14 as strong.

Charges Inside the Atom

Coulomb’s law explains how the nucleus holds on to its electrons.

The Nucleus and Its Electrons

  • The nucleus holds the electrons in the atom.
    • The nucleus is positive (it contains the protons).
    • Electrons are negative.
    • Opposite charges attract.
  • Distance: electrons closer to the nucleus are attracted more strongly than electrons farther away.
    • Outer electrons are held the most weakly.
    • Outer electrons are the easiest to remove.
  • Charge: a nucleus with more protons (a greater positive charge) pulls harder on its electrons.

You’ll use these ideas on the Ions, Bohr Models and Valence Electrons pages.

Atoms can gain or lose electrons. When the numbers of protons and electrons are no longer equal, the atom has a net charge and is called an ion.
