Rate Laws

Doubling the amount of one reactant does not always double the rate of a reaction. For some reactions the rate quadruples, and for others it does not change at all. The only way to know is to run experiments. In this section, we will discuss the rate law and how to find it from experimental data.

Rate Laws

A rate law connects the rate of a reaction to the concentrations of the reactants.

  • Rate law – an equation that gives the rate of a reaction from the concentrations of the reactants. For the reaction A + B → AB:
    • rate=k[A]m[B]n
    rate – instantaneous rate (M/s)k – rate constant[A], [B] – concentrations (M)m, n – orders
  • Order – the exponent on a reactant’s concentration in the rate law. The orders are found by experiment, not from the coefficients in the equation.
  • The overall order of the reaction is the sum of the exponents.
  • Rate constant (k) – the number that connects the concentrations to the rate. The rate constant changes with temperature.
Order in a reactantWhen its concentration doubles, the rate…When it triples, the rate…
zero (0)does not change (×1)does not change (×1)
first (1)doubles (×2)triples (×3)
second (2)quadruples (×4)increases nine times (×9)
  • Steps to solving rate law problems:
    1. Write the general form of the rate law for the reactants.
    2. Find two trials in which the concentration of only one reactant changes.
    3. Compare the change in concentration to the change in rate to find the exponent for that reactant. Repeat for each reactant.
    4. Substitute the data from one trial into the rate law to solve for k.
