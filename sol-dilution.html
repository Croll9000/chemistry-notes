Dilution

Hospitals and laboratories buy hydrochloric acid at about 12 M and dilute it with water to the strength they need. Adding water does not change how many moles of acid are in the container; it only spreads them through a larger volume. That single idea is enough to calculate the concentration or the volume of any diluted solution.

Dilution

A glass of orange juice made from frozen concentrate tastes weaker if too much water is added, even though every bit of the concentrate is still in the glass. The amount of solute has not changed; only the volume it is spread through has. Adding solvent to a solution to lower its concentration is called dilution. Because molarity is moles of solute per liter of solution, a larger volume holding the same number of moles must have a lower molarity.

The moles of solute before dilution equal the moles after, and moles equal molarity times volume, so M1V1 = M2V2. This one short equation relates the concentrated solution to the diluted one. The volume of water to add is the final volume minus the starting volume.

  • Dilution – adding solvent to a solution to lower its concentration.
  • The moles of solute do not change: M1V1 = M2V2
  • The volume of water to add is the final volume minus the starting volume.
