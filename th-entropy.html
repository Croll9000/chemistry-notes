Entropy (S)

A block of dry ice slowly disappears into a cloud of gas, and a drop of food coloring spreads through a glass of water until the whole glass is evenly colored. Neither change ever runs backward on its own: the gas never collects back into a block, and the color never gathers back into a drop. Both changes make the particles more spread out and more disordered.

Entropy (S) is a measure of the disorder or randomness of a system. The change in entropy (ΔS) is Sproducts − Sreactants, and ΔS > 0 means the system becomes more disordered. Entropy increases when a substance changes phase from solid to liquid to gas (Ssolid < Sliquid < Sgas), since its particles gain more freedom to move; when the number of moles of gas increases during a reaction, since gas particles have the most possible arrangements; when the temperature increases, since faster particles spread their energy in more ways; and when a solid dissolves or two substances mix.

Thus, a reaction such as CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g), which turns a solid into a solid and a gas, increases in entropy. Nature favors changes that increase entropy, just as it favors changes that lower energy.

  • Entropy (S) – a measure of the disorder or randomness of a system.
  • Change in entropy (ΔS) – Sproducts − Sreactants. ΔS > 0 means the system becomes more disordered.
  • Entropy increases when:
    • a substance changes phase from solid → liquid → gas (Ssolid < Sliquid < Sgas);
    • the number of moles of gas increases during a reaction;
    • the temperature increases;
    • a solid dissolves or two substances mix.
  • Nature favors changes that increase entropy.

Entropy of the System and the Surroundings

Entropy of the System and the Surroundings

Water freezing into ice becomes more ordered, so its entropy decreases. Yet water freezes on its own in a cold freezer, which seems to break the rule that nature favors more disorder. The puzzle is solved by remembering that the second law is about the entropy of the universe, the system plus the surroundings, and not the system alone.

Heat that flows into the surroundings makes their particles move faster and spread their energy more widely. Thus, an exothermic process releases heat into the surroundings, so the entropy of the surroundings increases, while an endothermic process takes heat from the surroundings, so the entropy of the surroundings decreases.

It follows that a process can decrease the entropy of the system and still happen, if the surroundings gain more entropy than the system loses. When water freezes below 0 °C, the heat it releases raises the entropy of the cold surroundings by more than the water’s own entropy falls, so the entropy of the universe still increases. Above 0 °C, the same heat would raise the entropy of the warmer surroundings by less, which is why water does not freeze in a warm room.

  • An exothermic process releases heat into the surroundings, so the entropy of the surroundings increases.
  • An endothermic process takes heat from the surroundings, so the entropy of the surroundings decreases.
  • A process can decrease the entropy of the system and still happen, if the surroundings gain more entropy than the system loses. For freezing water below 0 °C, the heat released raises the entropy of the surroundings by more than the water’s entropy decreases.
