Gay-Lussac’s Law

Gay-Lussac’s Law

The label on an aerosol can warns against storing it near heat. Heating a sealed can from 25 °C to 50 °C raises the pressure of the gas inside by about 8%, and the can itself cannot stretch to relieve it. In 1802, Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac described what happens to the pressure of a gas in a rigid container like this one when its temperature changes. The relationship is known as Gay-Lussac’s law: the pressure exerted by a gas is directly proportional to its Kelvin temperature when the volume is held constant.

As with Charles’s law, the two quantities change by the same factor in the same direction. Doubling the Kelvin temperature of a gas in a rigid container doubles its pressure. As a result, the pressure divided by the temperature stays the same: PT = constant, and a graph of pressure against Kelvin temperature is a straight line with a positive slope that passes through the origin. At a higher temperature the particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and with more force. Because the container cannot expand, the pressure rises.

  • Gay-Lussac’s Law – the pressure exerted by a gas is directly proportional to the temperature of the gas when volume is held constant.
  • When the temperature increases, the pressure increases by the same factor. When the temperature decreases, the pressure decreases by the same factor (temperatures in kelvin).
  • Pressure and Kelvin temperature are directly proportional: doubling the Kelvin temperature doubles the pressure.
    • The pressure divided by the temperature stays the same: PT = constant.
  • Explanation using particles: at a higher temperature, the particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and with more force. Because the container cannot expand, the pressure rises.
  • Temperatures must be in kelvin. A graph of pressure against Kelvin temperature is a straight line with a positive slope that passes through the origin.
P1T1=P2T2
P1 – initial pressure (kPa or atm)T1 – initial temperature (K)P2 – final pressure (kPa or atm)T2 – final temperature (K)
Example
During summer months, a car tire’s pressure can increase as the temperature outside increases. During winter months, the tire pressure can decrease when the temperature outside drops.

Solving Gay-Lussac’s Law Problems

Solving Problems

Drivers are told to check their tire pressure when the tires are cold. After an hour on the highway, the air inside the tires can be 20 °C warmer, and because the tires barely stretch, the pressure is noticeably higher. Gay-Lussac’s law problems compare the pressure and the temperature of the same gas in a rigid container before and after a change. As with Charles’s law, the temperatures must be in kelvin, since only the Kelvin scale starts at zero kinetic energy and keeps the proportion true. To solve a Gay-Lussac’s law problem:

  1. List the variables and the unknown. Read the question and list every given variable, with its unit, and the unknown.
  2. Convert temperatures to kelvin. If a temperature is given in °C, add 273: K = °C + 273.
  3. Write down Gay-Lussac’s law.
  4. Substitute and solve. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation, then solve for the unknown algebraically.
  5. Write the final answer with units.
