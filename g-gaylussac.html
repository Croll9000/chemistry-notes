Gay-Lussac’s Law

Heating a sealed aerosol can from 25 °C to 50 °C raises the pressure of the gas inside by about 8%, which is why the labels on these cans warn against storing them near heat. The rigid can keeps the volume fixed, so the faster-moving particles strike the walls more often and harder. In this section, we will discuss Gay-Lussac’s law, the relationship between the pressure and the temperature of a gas.

Gay-Lussac’s Law

A pressure cooker, a sealed steel gas cylinder and an aerosol can all hold a gas in a container that cannot expand. In 1802, Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac described what happens to the pressure of a gas in a container like this when its temperature changes.

  • Gay-Lussac’s Law – the pressure exerted by a gas is directly proportional to the temperature of the gas when volume is held constant.
  • When the temperature increases, the pressure increases by the same factor. When the temperature decreases, the pressure decreases by the same factor (temperatures in kelvin).
  • Pressure and Kelvin temperature are directly proportional: doubling the Kelvin temperature doubles the pressure.
    • The pressure divided by the temperature stays the same: PT = constant.
  • Explanation using particles: at a higher temperature, the particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and with more force. Because the container cannot expand, the pressure rises.
  • Temperatures must be in kelvin. A graph of pressure against Kelvin temperature is a straight line through the origin.
P1T1=P2T2
P1 – initial pressure (kPa or atm)T1 – initial temperature (K)P2 – final pressure (kPa or atm)T2 – final temperature (K)
Example
During summer months, a car tire’s pressure can increase as the temperature outside increases. During winter months, the tire pressure can decrease when the temperature outside drops.

Solving Gay-Lussac’s Law Problems

Drivers are told to check tire pressure when the tires are cold, because after an hour on the highway the air inside can be 20 °C warmer and the pressure noticeably higher. Gay-Lussac’s law predicts the pressure of a gas held at constant volume at any temperature. In this section, we will solve Gay-Lussac’s law problems.

Solving Problems

Gay-Lussac’s law problems compare the pressure and temperature of a gas in a rigid container before and after a change. As with Charles’s law, the temperatures must be in kelvin.

  • Steps for solving gas law problems:
    1. List the variables and the unknown. Read the question and list every given variable, with its unit, and the unknown.
    2. Convert temperatures to kelvin. If a temperature is given in °C, add 273: K = °C + 273.
    3. Write down Gay-Lussac’s Law.
    4. Substitute and solve. Substitute the variables and their units into the equation, then solve for the unknown algebraically.
    5. Write the final answer with units.
  • The two pressures must have the same unit, and so must the two volumes.
