Intermolecular Forces of Attraction

Attractions between particles, not inside them.

Intermolecular Forces of Attraction

A gecko can walk up a glass window, and water beads up on a freshly waxed car. Both are caused by attractions between particles rather than by chemical bonds within them.

  • Molecules are attracted to one another by weak forces that arise due to differences in charge. These are known as intermolecular forces of attraction.
    • They are much weaker than the intramolecular forces (chemical bonds) that hold atoms together to form molecules.
  • Intermolecular Force of Attraction (IMF, IFA) – weak, attractive forces that pull atoms, ions and molecules together.
Recall that water is a polar molecule, meaning that it has a partially positive end and a partially negative end. When 2 water molecules are brought together, they will be attracted to one another because of these oppositely charged sides. This is an example of an intermolecular force. It is different from the intramolecular forces (chemical bonds) that hold the atoms together in the molecules.

Strength of Intermolecular Forces of Attraction

  • There are different types of intermolecular forces, and they can be differentiated by strength.

Ion–Dipole Forces

  • Recall that ions are atoms which possess a whole-number charge. Dipoles are polar molecules that have partially charged sides, one positive and one negative.
  • When brought together, ions and dipoles will exert an attractive force on one another.
  • Ion–Dipole Forces – attractive force between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule.
    • Found in mixtures of ionic and polar compounds.
Note how the ions in this diagram, Na+ and Cl−, are attracted to different ends of the polar water molecules. This is how an ionic compound like salt dissolves in water.

Hydrogen Bonding

  • Hydrogen has the capacity to form highly polar bonds when combined with oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine atoms, which are very electronegative. When molecules containing these bonds are brought together, there will be a strong attraction between them. This is known as a hydrogen bond.
  • Hydrogen Bond – strong intermolecular force between molecules that contain hydrogen bonded to a very electronegative element (oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine).
    • Note that a hydrogen “bond” is not a chemical bond but rather an attraction between molecules.
    • Found in compounds containing H + O, N or F.
When hydrogen bonds to fluorine, oxygen or nitrogen, it creates a very strong dipole due to the large electronegativity difference between the atoms. Hydrogen bonding is the strong IFA between molecules containing H + F, O, N.

Dipole–Dipole Forces

  • When two polar molecules are brought together, their oppositely charged ends will attract. This is known as a dipole–dipole force.
  • Dipole–Dipole Force – force of attraction between oppositely charged ends of polar molecules.
    • Found in polar compounds.
Both CH3F and HCl are polar molecules, meaning they each have a partially negative side and a partially positive side. Their oppositely charged ends will be attracted to one another.

London Dispersion Forces

  • Atoms and molecules with a symmetrical charge distribution can still apply an attractive force on each other. As electrons “move” around the atom, temporary charges can form around even nonpolar molecules, due to the brief uneven distribution of electron density. Attractive forces can arise due to these temporary charges. We call these forces London dispersion forces.
  • London Dispersion Forces – attraction between molecules due to temporary shifts in electron density that cause momentary dipoles.
    • Found in all substances.
    • Sometimes referred to as a van der Waals force, although that term technically includes hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole forces and London dispersion forces.
Even though oxygen (O2) is a nonpolar molecule, the electrons within the bonds can sometimes randomly shift to create an uneven charge distribution and therefore a dipole. This temporary dipole will be attracted to other nearby temporary dipoles.

London Dispersion Forces and Size

  • The size of an atom or molecule has an impact on the strength of its London dispersion forces.
    • Larger atoms/molecules have bigger electron clouds whose valence electrons are further from the nucleus and more easily moved around. This leads to a higher probability of temporary dipoles forming.
  • Larger atoms/molecules possess stronger London dispersion forces.
  • Smaller atoms/molecules possess weaker London dispersion forces.
Both fluorine (F2) and bromine (Br2) are diatomic, group 17 elements. However, bromine is a larger atom than fluorine. Therefore, its electron cloud is more susceptible to distortions and the formation of temporary dipoles. The London dispersion forces in Br2 are much stronger, causing it to exist as a liquid while F2 is a gas at STP.

Intermolecular Forces and Properties

Stronger attractions, harder to pull apart.

Intermolecular Forces and Physical Properties

Rubbing alcohol spread on the skin evaporates much faster than water. The strength of the attractions between particles determines how easily a liquid boils, holds together at its surface, flows and dissolves other substances.

  • The stronger the intermolecular forces in a substance, the more energy it takes to pull its particles apart.
  • To compare two substances, first find the strongest type of IFA in each (ion–dipole > hydrogen bonding > dipole–dipole > London dispersion). If both have the same type, compare size: larger molecules have stronger London forces.

Melting Point and Boiling Point

  • To melt a solid or boil a liquid, the particles must gain enough energy to pull away from each other. Only the attractions between molecules are overcome; the covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break.
  • Melting Point – the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It is the same temperature at which the liquid freezes.
  • Boiling Point – the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid. It is the same temperature at which the gas condenses.
  • Stronger IFAs → higher melting and boiling points.
  • Weaker IFAs → lower melting and boiling points. These substances are often gases at room temperature.
  • Example: CH4 (London forces only) boils at −162 °C, HCl (dipole–dipole) at −85 °C, and H2O (hydrogen bonding) at 100 °C.

Surface Tension

  • Inside a liquid, each molecule is pulled equally in all directions by its neighbors. At the surface, molecules are pulled only sideways and inward, so the surface acts like a stretched skin.
  • Surface tension – the tendency of a liquid’s surface to hold together and resist being broken.
  • Stronger IFAs → higher surface tension. Water’s hydrogen bonds let it bead up into tall drops and let small insects walk on it.
  • Weaker IFAs → lower surface tension. These liquids spread out into flat puddles.

Viscosity

  • Viscosity – a liquid’s resistance to flowing. Honey and glycerol have high viscosity; hexane and water have low viscosity.
  • For a liquid to flow, its molecules must slide past each other. Strong attractions hold the molecules in place, so the liquid flows slowly.
  • Stronger IFAs → higher viscosity. Glycerol has three O–H groups per molecule, so each molecule forms many hydrogen bonds.
  • Weaker IFAs → lower viscosity. Hexane molecules attract each other only weakly, so hexane flows easily.
  • Molecule size matters too: very long molecules (like those in vegetable oil) tangle together and have strong London forces overall, so oil is more viscous than water even though it is nonpolar.

Solubility: Like Dissolves Like

  • Solubility – how much of a substance (the solute) can dissolve in another substance (the solvent).
  • A solute dissolves when the solvent’s particles attract the solute’s particles strongly enough to pull them away from each other.
  • “Like dissolves like” – polar substances and ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents like water. Nonpolar substances dissolve in nonpolar solvents like hexane.
    • Ionic compounds in water: the partial charges of water molecules pull the charged ions apart. The δ− oxygen ends attract the positive ions, and the δ+ hydrogen ends attract the negative ions (ion–dipole forces). Water molecules surround each ion and carry it into the solution.
    • Polar molecules in water: sugar has O–H groups, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds with water molecules and are pulled into the solution.
    • Nonpolar molecules in water: polar water molecules have little attraction for nonpolar molecules, so they cannot pull them apart. Water molecules stay attracted to each other (hydrogen bonds), and the nonpolar substance stays separate. Oil and water form layers.
  • Stronger attractions between solute and solvent → more soluble.
  • Weak attractions between solute and solvent → not soluble.
