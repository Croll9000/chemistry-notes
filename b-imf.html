Intermolecular Forces

Bonds hold atoms together inside a molecule. Weaker attractions hold separate molecules to each other, and those attractions decide whether a substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid at room temperature.

Intermolecular Forces of Attraction

Intermolecular Forces of Attraction

Boiling a mole of water takes about 41 kJ of energy. Breaking all of the O–H bonds in a mole of water molecules would take more than 900 kJ. These two numbers tell us that boiling cannot be breaking bonds. When water boils, its molecules stay whole, and the steam above a pot is still H2O. Boiling only pulls the molecules away from one another.

The attractions being overcome are known as intermolecular forces of attraction (IMF, IFA). They arise from differences in charge between particles, and they are much weaker than the intramolecular forces, the chemical bonds that hold atoms together within a molecule.

Intermolecular Force of Attraction (IMF, IFA) – weak, attractive forces that pull atoms, ions and molecules together.

Although they are weak compared with chemical bonds, intermolecular forces decide whether a substance is a solid, liquid or gas at room temperature. They also control its melting point, its boiling point and what it dissolves in.

  • Molecules are attracted to one another by weak forces that arise due to differences in charge. These are known as intermolecular forces of attraction.
    • They are much weaker than the intramolecular forces (chemical bonds) that hold atoms together to form molecules.
  • Intermolecular Force of Attraction (IMF, IFA) – weak, attractive forces that pull atoms, ions and molecules together.
Recall that water is a polar molecule, meaning that it has a partially positive end and a partially negative end. When 2 water molecules are brought together, they will be attracted to one another because of these oppositely charged sides. This is an example of an intermolecular force. It is different from the intramolecular forces (chemical bonds) that hold the atoms together in the molecules.

Strength of Intermolecular Forces of Attraction

Not all intermolecular forces are equally strong. Their strength depends on how large the charges involved are. A full charge, as on an ion, attracts more strongly than a partial charge. A permanent partial charge, as on a polar molecule, attracts more strongly than a temporary one. From strongest to weakest, the types are ion–dipole forces, hydrogen bonds, dipole–dipole forces and London dispersion forces.

  • There are different types of intermolecular forces, and they can be differentiated by strength.

Ion–Dipole Forces

The strongest intermolecular force involves a full charge. Ions carry a whole-number charge, and dipoles are polar molecules with partially charged ends, one positive and one negative. The attraction between them is called an ion–dipole force. It is found in mixtures of ionic and polar compounds, such as salt water.

Ion–Dipole Forces – attractive force between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule.

When table salt is stirred into water, the water molecules crowd around each ion at the surface of the crystal. The negative oxygen ends of the water molecules point toward each sodium ion. The positive hydrogen ends point toward each chloride ion. Together, these attractions are strong enough to overcome the ionic bonds holding the crystal together, and the ions are pulled away into the water. This is how salt dissolves.

  • Recall that ions are atoms which possess a whole-number charge. Dipoles are polar molecules that have partially charged sides, one positive and one negative.
  • When brought together, ions and dipoles will exert an attractive force on one another.
  • Ion–Dipole Forces – attractive force between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule.
    • Found in mixtures of ionic and polar compounds.
Note how the ions in this diagram, Na+ and Cl−, are attracted to different ends of the polar water molecules. This is how an ionic compound like salt dissolves in water.

Hydrogen Bonding

Water, H2O, boils at 100 °C. Hydrogen sulfide, H2S, a similar molecule almost twice as heavy, boils at −60 °C. If water followed the pattern of the other compounds in its family, it would be a gas at room temperature. Something holds water molecules together unusually tightly.

That something is hydrogen’s partner. Oxygen, nitrogen and fluorine are very electronegative and very small. When hydrogen bonds to one of them, the bond is highly polar, and the hydrogen is left with a large partial positive charge. That hydrogen is strongly attracted to the lone pairs of a neighboring molecule. This attraction is referred to as a hydrogen bond, and it is found in compounds containing H bonded to O, N or F.

Hydrogen Bond – strong intermolecular force between molecules that contain hydrogen bonded to a very electronegative element (oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine).

Be careful with the name. A hydrogen “bond” is not a chemical bond, but an attraction between molecules. Hydrogen bonds explain why ice floats, why water has such a high boiling point and how the two strands of DNA are held together in every living cell. The strands must be able to separate when a cell copies its DNA, so it makes sense that they are held by many weak hydrogen bonds rather than by strong covalent ones.

  • Hydrogen has the capacity to form highly polar bonds when combined with oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine atoms, which are very electronegative.
    • When molecules containing these bonds are brought together, there will be a strong attraction between them.
    • This is known as a hydrogen bond.
  • Hydrogen Bond – strong intermolecular force between molecules that contain hydrogen bonded to a very electronegative element (oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine).
    • Note that a hydrogen “bond” is not a chemical bond but rather an attraction between molecules.
    • Found in compounds containing H + O, N or F.
When hydrogen bonds to fluorine, oxygen or nitrogen, it creates a very strong dipole due to the large electronegativity difference between the atoms. Hydrogen bonding is the strong IFA between molecules containing H + F, O, N.

Dipole–Dipole Forces

Hydrogen chloride, HCl, and fluorine, F2, have similar masses. Yet HCl boils at −85 °C and F2 boils at −188 °C. HCl molecules are polar, with a partially negative chlorine end and a partially positive hydrogen end, so they line up and attract their neighbors. Nonpolar F2 molecules do not.

This attraction between polar molecules is known as a dipole–dipole force. It is weaker than a hydrogen bond, since the partial charges are smaller.

Dipole–Dipole Force – force of attraction between oppositely charged ends of polar molecules.

  • When two polar molecules are brought together, their oppositely charged ends will attract. This is known as a dipole–dipole force.
  • Dipole–Dipole Force – force of attraction between oppositely charged ends of polar molecules.
    • Found in polar compounds.
Both CH3F and HCl are polar molecules, meaning they each have a partially negative side and a partially positive side. Their oppositely charged ends will be attracted to one another.

London Dispersion Forces

Nonpolar molecules have no permanent charged ends, yet they still attract one another. Oxygen and methane can both be cooled into liquids, so something must hold their molecules together. The explanation lies in the moving electrons.

As electrons move around an atom, they are not always spread evenly. For an instant, more of them may be on one side, creating a temporary charge, and that temporary charge can cause a similar shift in a neighboring molecule. The two temporary dipoles attract. These attractions are called London dispersion forces. They are sometimes called van der Waals forces, although that term technically also includes dipole–dipole forces.

London Dispersion Forces – attraction between molecules due to temporary shifts in electron density that cause momentary dipoles.

Since every atom and molecule has electrons, London dispersion forces are found in all substances. They are the only force between nonpolar molecules, such as O2 and CH4, and the only reason those substances can be liquefied at all.

A gecko shows how much these weak forces can add up to. Its toes are covered with millions of tiny hairs that split into even tinier tips, and each tip gets so close to a surface that London dispersion forces form between them. Each attraction is incredibly weak, but billions of them together let a gecko run up a pane of glass and hang from a ceiling by one toe.

  • Atoms and molecules with a symmetrical charge distribution can still apply an attractive force on each other.
    • As electrons “move” around the atom, temporary charges can form around even nonpolar molecules, due to the brief uneven distribution of electron density.
    • Attractive forces can arise due to these temporary charges.
    • We call these forces London dispersion forces.
  • London Dispersion Forces – attraction between molecules due to temporary shifts in electron density that cause momentary dipoles.
    • Found in all substances.
    • Sometimes referred to as a van der Waals force, although that term technically includes hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole forces and London dispersion forces.
Even though oxygen (O2) is a nonpolar molecule, the electrons within the bonds can sometimes randomly shift to create an uneven charge distribution and therefore a dipole. This temporary dipole will be attracted to other nearby temporary dipoles.

London Dispersion Forces and Size

The halogens are all nonpolar diatomic molecules. Yet at room temperature fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid and iodine is a solid. Since the only intermolecular force between their molecules is the London dispersion force, that force must be getting stronger down the group.

Larger atoms and molecules have bigger electron clouds, with valence electrons farther from the nucleus. These electrons are more easily shifted, so temporary dipoles form more easily and are larger. Thus, larger atoms and molecules possess stronger London dispersion forces, and smaller atoms and molecules possess weaker London dispersion forces. An iodine molecule, with 106 electrons, attracts its neighbors far more strongly than a fluorine molecule with 18.

  • The size of an atom or molecule has an impact on the strength of its London dispersion forces.
    • Larger atoms/molecules have bigger electron clouds whose valence electrons are further from the nucleus and more easily moved around. This leads to a higher probability of temporary dipoles forming.
  • Larger atoms/molecules possess stronger London dispersion forces.
  • Smaller atoms/molecules possess weaker London dispersion forces.
Both fluorine (F2) and bromine (Br2) are diatomic, group 17 elements. However, bromine is a larger atom than fluorine. Therefore, its electron cloud is more susceptible to distortions and the formation of temporary dipoles. The London dispersion forces in Br2 are much stronger, causing it to exist as a liquid while F2 is a gas at STP.
