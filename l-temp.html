What Temperature Measures

Temperature

A small teacup of boiling water and a large pot of boiling water are both at 100 °C, even though the pot holds far more water and far more energy. A drop of water from either one would burn just the same. This is due to the fact that temperature does not depend on how much of a substance there is. Instead, it describes how fast the particles are moving on average, and the particles in the cup move just as fast as those in the pot.

Temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample, measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius (°C) or kelvin (K). Kinetic energy is the energy an object has due to its motion, so the higher the temperature, the faster the particles move on average.

Temperature – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample.

Kinetic Energy – energy possessed by an object due to its motion.

Note that the word “average” matters. In any sample, some particles move faster and some slower than others, and a thermometer reports only their average. Since the average does not depend on the number of particles, temperature does not depend on the size of the sample.

  • Temperature – a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample.
    • Unit – degrees Celsius (°C) and kelvin (K)
    • Measuring device – thermometer
  • Kinetic Energy – energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
    • The higher the temperature, the faster the particles move on average.
  • Temperature does not depend on how much of the substance you have.
    Example
    A small cup and a large pot of boiling water are both at 100 °C.
    Their particles have the same average kinetic energy.

The Celsius and Kelvin Scales

Celsius Scale

When the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius created his temperature scale in 1742, he set the boiling point of water at 0 degrees and its freezing point at 100, the reverse of the scale used today. Soon after his death, the scale was flipped, so that higher numbers mean hotter. The Celsius scale is based on water: water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at standard pressure (1 atm), and the space between those two points is divided into 100 equal degrees. Anything colder than freezing water has a negative Celsius temperature, such as dry ice at about −78 °C.

Celsius Scale (°C) – a temperature scale based on water, which freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C.

  • The Celsius scale (°C) is based on water: water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C (at standard pressure, 1 atm).
  • The space between those two points is divided into 100 equal degrees.
  • Anything colder than freezing water has a negative Celsius temperature. Dry ice is about −78 °C.

Kelvin Scale

In 1848, the Scottish physicist William Thomson, later known as Lord Kelvin, proposed a temperature scale that starts at the coldest possible temperature rather than at the freezing point of water. Scientists have since cooled atoms to within a billionth of a degree of this limit, but never to the limit itself.

The Kelvin scale is the SI temperature scale, and it starts at absolute zero (0 K), the lowest possible temperature, at which particles have the least possible kinetic energy. Since no temperature can be colder, there are no negative Kelvin temperatures. Note that Kelvin temperatures are written with no degree sign: 298 K, not 298 °K.

Kelvin Scale (K) – the SI temperature scale, which starts at absolute zero.

Absolute Zero (0 K) – the lowest possible temperature, at which particles have the least possible kinetic energy.

Scientists found absolute zero before they could ever come close to it. What they saw is that as a gas cools, its volume shrinks in a straight line. If that line is extended to the point where the volume would reach zero, it lands at −273.15 °C for every gas tested. Since a gas cannot have a volume less than zero, they concluded that no temperature can be colder than this.

  • The Kelvin scale (K) is the SI temperature scale.
  • It starts at absolute zero (0 K), the lowest possible temperature.
    • At absolute zero, particles have the least possible kinetic energy.
    • Particles nearly stop moving.
  • How do we know where absolute zero is?
    • As a gas cools, its volume shrinks in a straight line.
    • Extending that line to zero volume lands at −273.15 °C.
    • No temperature can be colder.
  • There are no negative Kelvin temperatures.
  • Kelvin temperatures are written with no degree sign.

    298 K, not 298 °K

Comparing the Two Scales

We can think of the Celsius and Kelvin scales as two rulers with the same markings but different starting points. A change of 1 kelvin is the same size as a change of 1 Celsius degree, so heating water from 20 °C to 30 °C is a change of 10 degrees on both scales: from 293 K to 303 K. The scales only start at different places, 0 °C at the freezing point of water and 0 K at absolute zero, so converting between them only requires adding or subtracting 273.15.

  • A change of 1 kelvin is the same size as a change of 1 Celsius degree.
    • The scales just start at different places.
    • Converting only requires adding or subtracting 273.15.

Converting Between Kelvin and Celsius

The Reference Tables give the conversion K = °C + 273.15. Since 0 °C is 273.15 kelvins above absolute zero, every Celsius temperature is 273.15 more on the Kelvin scale, so 25 °C is 298 K. To find a Celsius temperature from a Kelvin temperature, we subtract 273.15 instead: °C = K − 273.15. Since the conversion is an addition or a subtraction, the answer is rounded to the same decimal place as the temperature given, so 25 °C becomes 298 K, not 298.15 K.

  • From the Mathematical Relationships table of your Reference Table: K = °C + 273.15
  • To find °C, subtract 273.15 from the Kelvin temperature: °C = K − 273.15
  • This is addition or subtraction, so round to the same decimal place as the temperature you were given.
Ex 1: Convert 25 °C into kelvin.
K = °C + 273.15 = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 → 298 K
25 °C has no decimal places, so round to the ones place.
Ex 2: Convert 305 K into °C.
°C = K − 273.15 = 305 − 273.15 = 31.85 → 32 °C
305 K has no decimal places, so round to the ones place.
Check your answer: a Kelvin temperature is always 273.15 larger than the same temperature in Celsius. If you converted to kelvin and got a smaller number, you subtracted instead of adding.
